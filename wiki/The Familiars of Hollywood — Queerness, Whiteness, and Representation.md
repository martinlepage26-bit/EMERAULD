# The Familiars of Hollywood — Queerness, Whiteness, and Representation

## Summary
Academic essay (**2025**) by [[Martin Lepage — Professional Profile]] analyzing *What We Do in the Shadows* (WWDITS) as an allegory of Hollywood's "vampiric economy of visibility" — an industry that consumes difference while reproducing whiteness as structural center. Six analytical movements: hidden labor in voice acting, dismantling of patriarchal vampire genealogies, Colin Robinson as allegory for whiteness's banal extractive power, Guillermo as allegory for queer Latinx servitude, multinational ensemble as parody of Hollywood's nationality coding, and the central paradox of identity politics that unsettles and sustains whiteness simultaneously. Part of the [[Historical Academic Portfolio — Pre-PHAROS Scholarly Work|cultural studies corpus]]. Related to [[The Scythe Already in Motion — Buffy, Queer Ritual, and the Politics of Glitch]] and [[Social Compass Paper — Ritual Authority and Régime de Preuve]].

The analysis references TV-specific characters and arcs (Guillermo, Colin Robinson, Baby Colin, Season 2 and 5 references), confirming the paper engages the TV adaptation. ## Core Argument

WWDID dramatizes **Hollywood's vampiric economy of visibility**: an industry that consumes difference even as it obscures it, thrives on marginal voices while continually centering whiteness.

Parody as the operative mode: the vampires mimic Hollywood caricatures so excessively that the caricatures appear absurd — yet the logic of caricature persists. This is Bhabha's mimicry: it destabilizes stereotypes by exaggerating them, but also reproduces them.

The central paradox: does the series' identity politics unsettle whiteness, or reproduce it under the guise of inclusion?

---

## Key Concepts

### Vampiric Economy of Representation
Hollywood consumes marginalized voices and labor while reproducing whiteness as neutral and structural. Talent is extracted but not recognized. The vampire is the ideal figure for this analysis: it subsists on the vitality of others.

### Voice/Invisibility Paradox
Voice acting as refuge and constraint for marginalized performers — heard but not seen. The essential yet invisible labor of the voice. Staten Island as metaphor: physically proximate to the center of power (Manhattan) but structurally invisible within it.

### Colin Robinson as Allegory for Whiteness
**The structural claim**: whiteness operates as both invisible norm and overwhelming presence — unmarked, neutral, yet structuring every field of representation. Colin's power is *banality*, not charisma. He thrives because he sounds "neutral." His compulsive reproduction (the Baby Colin arc) satirizes how whiteness returns as structural default even when interrupted or reformed.

He drains energy through internet arguments (Season 2) — debate as depletion. Not spectacular but gravitational: a black hole around which all else orbits.

### Guillermo as Allegory for Queer Latinx Servitude
Indispensable yet continually marginalized. His "half-vampirism" (Season 5) allegorizes the "not-yet" of queerness (Muñoz) — inclusion always partial, conditional, never complete.

His heroism (Season 2 massacre of the vampire theater) is repeatedly absorbed back into invisibility. His ancestry (Van Helsing descendant) becomes a gag. His family discovers his secret — and his mother's voice registers fear and disappointment. His usual role as translator collapses into silence.

## Six Analytical Movements

### 1. Voice Acting as Hidden Labor
Voice acting historically as a hidden corner of Hollywood. The essential labor of voice actors mirrors the structural invisibility of marginalized performers in the industry. Staten Island = the invisible periphery that sustains the visible center.

### 2. Counter-Patriarchal Allegory
The series replaces patriarchal bloodlines with chosen family, queer attachment, and care:
- **The Baron**: dies in farce, not battle — severing vampirism from Gothic law of inheritance
- **The Sire**: "domesticated" in a suburban home — the father of all vampires becomes dependent, cared for rather than obeyed
- **Nadja**: feminist rage, gleefully monstrous, outside patriarchal order
- **Laszlo**: queers masculinity through camp and paternal nurturing of Baby Colin
- **Nandor**: failed reproduction of authority; slides into homoerotic intimacy with Guillermo

Framework: Judith Butler and Kath Weston — queer kinship emerges where patriarchal lineage collapses and care replaces blood as basis for family.

### 3. Colin Robinson and the Banality of Whiteness
Whiteness as not spectacular but gravitational. It is not through overt domination but through endless repetition, exhausting others into silence. The Baby Colin arc: even when whiteness is nurtured, reformed, cared for by others, it reverts inevitably to its extractive neutral drone.

Reference: Richard Dyer — the "unmarked" voice coded as white; accented or culturally marked voices heard as excessive, comic, or suspect.

### 4. Guillermo and the "Not-Yet" of Queer Latinx Inclusion
Muñoz's "utopian impulse" of queerness: inclusion is always conditional, never complete. Guillermo is indispensable but never fully inside. His heroism is absorbed. His ancestry is a joke. His half-vampirism is his ceiling, not his recognition.

Ahmed's "non-performative" of diversity: difference is celebrated to reinforce whiteness as the unmarked center — "happy diversity."

### 5. Coding Nationality Through Accent
The ensemble as parody of Hollywood's coding of nationality:
- **Nadja** (Greek): exoticized but politically dismissed; feminist rage neutralized
- **Laszlo** (British): parodies imperial whiteness/camp, but still gravitationally central
- **Nandor** (Ottoman/vague Middle Eastern): flattened into caricature; erases Ottoman vampire genealogies
- **Colin Robinson**: aggressively normal — whiteness as unmarked center

Reference: Rey Chow — "accented cinema"; linguistic difference turned into spectacle.

### 6. The Central Question
Do the series' identity politics unsettle whiteness, or reproduce it under the guise of inclusion? The answer is both: parody illuminates structures even when it cannot dismantle them. The shadows are also sites of survival — chosen families, queer attachments, alternative masculinities mark cracks in whiteness's gravitational field.

## Key Theoretical Sources

- **Richard Dyer** — whiteness as unmarked norm; the invisible standard against which all else is marked
- **Rey Chow (1998)** — accented cinema; linguistic difference as spectacle
- **José Esteban Muñoz (2009)** — queerness's "not-yet"; the utopian impulse that remains always just ahead of inclusion
- **Sara Ahmed (2012)** — non-performative of diversity; "happy diversity" that centers whiteness
- **Barbara Creed** — *The Monstrous-Feminine* (1993): women reduced to reproductive vessels in Gothic
- **Judith Butler (2002)** + **Kath Weston (1991)** — queer kinship beyond blood
- **Homi Bhabha (1994)** — mimicry's ambivalence: destabilizes while sustaining
- **Nancy Fraser (2000)** — recognition vs. redistribution: symbolic inclusion without material change

## Insights

- The "vampiric economy of representation" is a more precise concept than "Hollywood diversity problem" — it names the mechanism: extraction of vitality from marginalized performers and communities, consumption without recognition, reproduction of the center at the expense of the margin
- Colin Robinson's gravitational whiteness is the analytical contribution with the widest application beyond the series: whiteness is not a loud, aggressive force but a compulsive, exhausting, self-replicating one that depletes through repetition
- Guillermo's trajectory is the most important governance-adjacent element: indispensable labor that produces no durable recognition; heroism that is absorbed and erased; half-inclusion that is the ceiling. This is the structure of precarious labor in any extractive system
- The Baby Colin arc is the most formally sophisticated allegory: even when whiteness is nurtured from infancy by people of color with genuine care, it reverts. The arc does not suggest change is impossible; it suggests the mechanism of reversion must be named before change can be designed
