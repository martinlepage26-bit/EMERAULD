# Queering Neo-Pagan Magic — 2025 Revised Version

## Summary

Revised 3,000-word version of [[Martin Lepage — Professional Profile|Martin Lepage]]'s "Queering Neo-Pagan Magic: Identity, Power, and the Uses of Magic," trimmed for journal submission requirements, in dialogue with Michael Bailey's "The Meanings of Magic." Dated 2025 in filename. Distinct from both [[Queering Neo-Pagan Magic — 2006 Paper]] and [[Queering Neo-Pagan Magic — 2026 Paper]]. Part of [[Research and Papers MOC]].

## Context

Two 2025–2026 files represent a cluster of versions of the same paper: `2025 - Pagan Magic.docx` (described as "revised to exactly 3000 words") and `2026 - Pagan Magic Identity, Power, and the Uses of Magic.docx` (a longer, more formally developed version). The 2025 version is a trimmed revision excluding an extended genealogical section; the 2026 version restores theoretical development. Together these constitute the manuscript sequence leading to [[Queering Neo-Pagan Magic — 2026 Paper]].

The paper continues and extends [[Martin Lepage — Professional Profile|Martin Lepage]]'s earlier ethnographic work on queer Pagan practitioners in Montreal, placing the category of **magic** itself — rather than identity or community — at the center of analysis. Part of the 20-year genealogy [[Queering Neo-Pagan Magic — Identity Power and the Uses of Magic]], methodologically rooted in the 2010 mythocritique substrate (see [[Mythocritique to PHAROS — The 2010 Master's Thesis as Methodological Keystone]]). Indexed in [[Queer Sociology of Magic and Ritual]].

## Details

**Core argument:** Magic is not only a symbolic or religious act — it is a **queer methodology of self and world-making**. Drawing from ethnographic work on queer Pagan practitioners in Montreal, the paper explores how magic is enacted, felt, and theorized through ritual, identity, and embodied transformation.

**Engagement with Michael Bailey:** Bailey's "The Meanings of Magic" argues that magic's usefulness lies in how it draws the limits of religious legitimacy. The paper's practitioners "inhabit those limits and stretch them" — they operate in the zone that Bailey identifies as magic's definitional edge, from a position of tactical illegitimacy.

**Key methodological move:** Rather than tracing the academic genealogy of magic (Bailey's approach), the paper begins with an "epistemological refusal": following Diane Lamoureux's feminist sociology, queer theory unsettles identity not merely by adding new terms but by undermining the very categories that claim coherence.

**Five interlocutors** (in the 2026 version): queer Pagan practitioners who use magic to navigate gender, power, and meaning in ways that defy linear logic or disciplinary categorization.

**Queer magic as method:** Magic here is "a vector of praxis where gender, power, and cosmology intersect in ways that often defy linear logic" — resonant with the broader methodological claims in [[Refusing Fixity — Aesthetic Governance and Fugitive Authorship]].

## Key Ideas

- Magic as queer methodology: magic is the practice by which queer practitioners make themselves and their worlds
- Epistemological refusal: the paper refuses genealogy in favor of lived categories
- Inhabiting the limits: queer practitioners live at the definitional boundary of magic/religion that academic discourse tries to police
- Nomadic epistemology (2026 version): mobile, non-fixed knowledge claims as a form of political resistance

## Insights

- The progression from 2006 to 2013 to 2025/2026 versions of this paper documents a 20-year theoretical deepening of a single question: what is magic doing for people who are excluded from dominant epistemological systems?
- The 2026 version's "tactical illegitimacy" concept is the queer equivalent of what the PHAROS governance method calls "bounded non-finality" — refusing the closure that produces settled authority
