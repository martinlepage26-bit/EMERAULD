# RECURSO Full Integration — Fluency, Interruption, and Theoretical Synthesis

## Summary
Enriched version of the fluency/interruption governance paper integrating 19 theoretical voices into the RECURSO governance framework. Author: Martin Lepage, PhD. Full document: `04_RECURSO_DRAFT1_FULL_INTEGRATION.docx`. This is a heavily annotated draft that maps the fluency/interruption arguments from [[Fluency, Interruption, and Institutional Accountability]] against a comprehensive theoretical genealogy, develops the **upstream classification** concept as the paper's most original contribution, and establishes three design principles and four analysis domains. Related to [[RECURSO — Final Audit and Ethical Review]], [[RECURSO — Recursive Governance Test Archive]], [[Fluency, Interruption, and Institutional Accountability]], and [[Recursive Deterministic AI Governance — Method and Paper]].

The document maps the fluency/interruption framework against contributions from:

1. **Foucault** — Discourse, discipline, and the production of docile subjects; apparatus analysis
2. **Butler** — Performativity; the iterative production of norms through repetition; agency within constraint
3. **Bourdieu** — Field theory; habitus; symbolic capital as the social mechanism that converts fluency into authority
4. **Giddens** — Structuration theory; the duality of structure (structure as both constraint and resource); reflexivity
5. **Austin** — Speech act theory; performative utterances; the conditions under which utterances count as actions
6. **Sedgwick** — Queer theory; the epistemology of the closet; the performative stakes of disclosure and concealment
7. **de Beauvoir** — Situated knowledge; the body as a situation; freedom as always-already constrained
8. **Becker** — Labeling theory; deviance as a product of social classification, not intrinsic property
9. **Bilge** — Intersectionality as methodology; the limits of single-axis analysis
10. **Anthias** — Translocational positionality; the situated, mobile character of social location
11. **Crenshaw** — Intersectionality; the structural, political, and representational dimensions

## Upstream Classification

The paper's most original contribution: the concept of **upstream classification** as a mechanism of governance by denial.

**Standard governance-by-denial analysis** (from [[Governance by Denial]]): mechanisms that formally provide rights, processes, or protections while structurally preventing their exercise through legibility gaps, capacity barriers, and classification denials.

**Upstream classification** extends this: the most effective governance denial does not occur at the point where a complaint is filed or a right is claimed. It occurs **upstream** — in the classifications that determine whether the situation is legible as a rights-violation before the right can be exercised.

Examples:
- A workplace harassment complaint requires the situation to be classified as "harassment" before the grievance mechanism engages. Upstream, the institutional culture may define "harassment" in ways that exclude many forms of harm
- An AI system's outputs may cause harm that is only actionable if classified as a "decision" under data protection law. Upstream, the system may be classified as "generating insights" rather than "making decisions" — placing it outside the accountability regime
- A job application rejected by an algorithm may involve discrimination that is only actionable if classified as a "hiring decision" subject to anti-discrimination law. Upstream, the algorithm's output may be classified as "screening" rather than "deciding"

**The governance implication**: Governance systems that focus on decision-accountability without addressing upstream classification are systematically incomplete. The most consequential governance moments are the ones that determine whether a situation is even legible as a governance object.

This connects directly to the [[PHAROS Invention Disclosure]] claim typing (R1/R2) and consequence classification (R3/R4): the PHAROS pipeline begins with upstream classification of claims before governance rules apply.

---

## Three Design Principles

1. **Legibility-first design**: Governance systems must begin by making the situation legible — classifying the actors, actions, and stakes — before applying accountability rules. Accountability without legibility is empty procedure

2. **Capacity-as-condition**: Rights and accountability mechanisms require capacity to exercise them. Governance systems that provide rights without providing capacity are governance theater. Capacity (time, resources, knowledge, institutional standing) is a design requirement, not an optional accessibility feature

3. **Classification-floor discipline**: Every governance system has upstream classification decisions that determine which situations enter the accountability regime. These classification decisions must be governed as first-order governance objects, not treated as technical definitions

## Four Analysis Domains

The paper applies the enriched framework to four institutional domains:

### Domain 1 — Workplace Algorithmic Management
Extended from [[Fluency, Interruption, and Institutional Accountability]]: upstream classification of worker monitoring outputs as "performance data" rather than "surveillance evidence" places them outside standard labor rights frameworks.

### Domain 2 — Algorithmic Recruitment
Extended from [[Fluency, Interruption, and Institutional Accountability]]: upstream classification of resume screening as "filtering" rather than "hiring decision" removes it from anti-discrimination law's coverage.

### Domain 3 — Content Moderation
Extended from [[Fluency, Interruption, and Institutional Accountability]]: upstream classification of content removal as "platform policy enforcement" rather than "speech restriction" removes it from the governance frameworks designed for speech restriction.

### Domain 4 — Clinical AI
New domain: upstream classification of model outputs as "clinical decision support" rather than "clinical decisions" may exempt them from the liability and accountability frameworks that apply to clinical decisions — even when the practical effect is indistinguishable.

## Theoretical Synthesis

The 19-theorist integration produces a synthesis claim:

**Fluency is not the absence of friction — it is the product of governance work that makes friction invisible.** Every fluent system has:
- A set of upstream classifications that determine what counts as a governance object
- An aesthetic governance regime that rewards compliance and penalizes deviation
- Capacity barriers that determine who can exercise formal rights
- A legitimacy infrastructure that converts recognition into enforceable authority

The PHAROS governance methodology addresses all four: upstream classification (corpus formation and claim typing), aesthetic governance (non-exceptionable gates that cannot be satisfied through form compliance alone), capacity support (the deterministic pipeline reduces the capacity required to navigate governance), and legitimacy infrastructure (consequence binding that makes governance decisions enforceable).

## Insights

- Upstream classification is the most important concept in the full integration draft — it completes the governance-by-denial analysis by showing where governance denial is most effectively performed (before the accountability mechanism engages, not at the point of complaint)
- The 19-theorist integration is not decorative — each theorist illuminates a specific mechanism in the fluency/interruption framework. Bourdieu on symbolic capital explains why fluency converts into authority (the field rewards the right habitus); Austin on speech acts explains why governance decisions must be performative (they must count as decisions to trigger accountability); Namaste on epistemic violence explains the specific harm of misclassification for trans and other marginalized people
- The four analysis domains (workplace, recruitment, content moderation, clinical AI) together show that upstream classification is a pervasive governance problem, not specific to one sector — which strengthens the argument for treating it as a design principle rather than a sectoral fix
