# Privacy as Contextual Integrity — Nissenbaum 2004 (Public Surveillance)

## Summary
Helen Nissenbaum’s 2004 *Washington Law Review* article reframes privacy as **contextual integrity**: privacy violations are not simply “disclosure” or “secrecy” failures, but breaches of *contextual information norms* (appropriateness + distribution). This is vault-usable as a governance bridge between “data protection” language and a more sociological account of why certain information flows feel wrong even when the data is not “sensitive”; sits adjacent to [[Consent Frameworks — Historical, Legal, Social, and AI Governance]] (consent as boundary condition) and to [[Governance by Denial]] (what institutions can recognize and sanction).

## Why it matters here
- **Governance lens:** “what’s allowed” is contextual, not only legal; privacy harm often comes from *cross-context transfer* (workplace → marketing, clinic → insurer, civic life → policing). The contextual lens reinforces the [[CONTROL 2 — External Data Lifecycle Protocol (Regulatory Grounding)]] requirement that policy-version drift trigger re-approval, and pairs with [[Reddit Data API — Access Terms and Rate Limits]] as a worked case of cross-context flow conditions.
- **Auditability:** contextual integrity implies you can audit a system by comparing its actual information flows against the expected norms of the context(s) it touches — operationally this maps onto the [[PHAROS Method — Technical Reference]] pipeline’s admissibility checks and onto [[NIST AI RMF 1.0 — NIST AI 100-1 (2023)]] *Map* / *Govern* functions.
- **PHAROS alignment:** supports a move from abstract “privacy principles” to executable checks framed as “flow allowed / flow disallowed / flow requires consent or justification” — an instance of the [[Fluency, Interruption, and Institutional Accountability|interruption-as-governance]] criterion applied to data flows.

## Key Ideas (Vault-usable)
- Privacy as a right is violated when **information gathering/dissemination becomes inappropriate to a context** or breaks the **norms of distribution** inside it; cf. [[The Data Gaze — Beer (Capitalism, Power, Perception)|Beer’s “data gaze”]] for the upstream rationality that normalizes context-collapse.
- Public surveillance cases (CCTV, shopper cards/data warehouses, RFID) become legible as privacy problems when seen as **norm-violations**, not merely “public data.” Compare with [[Automating Inequality — Eubanks (Digital Poorhouse, profiling and punishment)]] where context-collapse compounds classification harm and with [[Big Data’s Disparate Impact — Barocas & Selbst 2016 (Title VII, data mining)]] for the disparate-impact pipeline.
- Contexts have governing norms; governance work should encode those norms rather than treat privacy as a single universal rule — a position consonant with the [[AI Governance Reference Stack — Annotated Library (Operational 2026-03-11)|AI governance reference stack]] and with the bilingual / cross-jurisdictional posture of [[Martin Lepage — Professional Profile|the practice]].
