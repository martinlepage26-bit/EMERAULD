# Queer Neo-Pagan Spiritualities and the Question of Authenticity in Wicca

## Summary

Academic article (2017 version; an earlier 2002 version with the same title also exists in the archive) by [[Martin Lepage — Professional Profile|Martin Lepage]] examining how queer neo-Pagan practitioners — especially those aligned with Wicca in the Canadian context — reconfigure ritual, cosmology, and authenticity in ways that challenge and renew the tradition. Rooted in long-term ethnographic fieldwork (2011–2017). Part of [[Research and Papers MOC]] and [[Historical Academic Portfolio — Pre-PHAROS Scholarly Work]].

## Context

This article sits at the mature end of [[Martin Lepage — Professional Profile|Martin Lepage]]'s ethnographic arc on Montreal's queer neo-Pagan scene. It builds on earlier work including [[The Inner Goddess — Gender Normativity and Gay Identity in Wiccan Ritualities]], [[Queering Neo-Pagan Magic — 2006 Paper]], [[Neo-Shamanism and Gender — Montreal Pagan Community]], and [[The Circle Remains Open — Toward a Queer Sociology of the Sacred]]. A second version exists under the JCR submission filename (`LEPAGE-JCR-Queer Neo-Pagan Spiritualities and Authenticity in Wicca.docx`), suggesting journal submission to the *Journal of Contemporary Religion* or similar venue. Part of the queer Pagan corpus that extends the 2010 mythocritique substrate (see [[Mythocritique to PHAROS — The 2010 Master's Thesis as Methodological Keystone]]); indexed in [[Queer Sociology of Magic and Ritual]]. Connects to [[Agatha All Along — Wicca, Digital Mediatization, and Proof Regimes]] and [[Social Compass Paper — Ritual Authority and Régime de Preuve]] in its attention to authenticity as contested claim.

## Details

**Fieldwork base:** Long-term ethnographic engagement with Montreal's queer neo-Pagan scene from 2011 to 2017, supplemented by participant observation at national festivals including Kaleidoscope Gathering (Ontario) and Circle of Sparks (Quebec). Extended to digital spaces: TikTok, Tumblr, Instagram. Research ethics followed Tuck and Yang's relational accountability model (2014); certain narratives deliberately left untold per Glissant's "right to opacity" (1997).

**Author positionality:** Explicitly queer-identified scholar-practitioner: has cast circles at Kaleidoscope Gathering, sung in queer moon rituals, co-led workshops on queer spiritual care. Reflexivity framed not as methodological add-on but as commitment to vulnerability and porous boundaries.

**Core reframing of authenticity:** Rather than fidelity to historical continuity or institutional lineage, authenticity is understood as **resonance between lived experience and symbolic action** — a performative and affective criterion rather than a genealogical one.

**Central argument:** LGBTQ practitioners do not discard Wicca's symbolic frameworks (gendered binarisms, mythic genealogies) but queer them — rescripting rituals, reimagining deities, integrating personal and digital forms of spiritual expression. These ritual interventions are not merely adaptive responses but **generative acts of world-making** that recast the sacred as plural, embodied, and in flux.

**Digital religion dimension:** Online platforms play a constitutive role — not secondary — in shaping contemporary Wiccan practice. Visibility, legitimacy, and communal belonging are increasingly negotiated through social media aesthetics, hashtags, and livestreamed rituals. This connects to the [[Agatha All Along — Wicca, Digital Mediatization, and Proof Regimes|Agatha All Along]] argument about mediatization transforming proof conditions for ritual authority.

**Theoretical toolkit:** Gender performativity, queer theory, anthropology of religion, digital religion studies.

**Keywords:** queer spirituality, Wicca, neo-Paganism, authenticity, gender performativity, ritual, digital religion, LGBTQ religiosity.

## Key Ideas

- Authenticity as resonance, not genealogy: the criterion shifts from institutional lineage to felt alignment between experience and ritual action
- Queering as world-making: ritual rescripting is not adaptation but generative production of new sacred forms
- Digital enchantment: online platforms are not substitutes for in-person practice but distinct formations that produce real spiritual capital
- Embodied ritual experimentation: practitioners foreground their bodies as sites of knowledge, not just vessels for transmitted tradition
- Uneven recognition: inclusion in queer Pagan spaces remains conditional; tensions of representation persist even in ostensibly welcoming communities

## Insights

- This 2017 article represents the culmination of the Montreal fieldwork arc and directly feeds the theoretical infrastructure used in the [[Social Compass Paper — Ritual Authority and Régime de Preuve|Social Compass submission]]. The shift from "authenticity" to "régime de preuve" (proof regime) in later work can be read as a hardening of this paper's "resonance" criterion into a sociological mechanism.
- The ethnographic fieldwork overlaps substantially with [[The Circle Remains Open — Toward a Queer Sociology of the Sacred]] and [[Neo-Shamanism and Gender — Montreal Pagan Community]]; each paper cuts the same fieldwork at a different theoretical angle.
- The JCR version filename suggests at minimum one journal submission — likely prior to or concurrent with the Social Compass submission.
