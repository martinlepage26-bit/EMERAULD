# Sales Objection Handling — Diagnosing Fog Without Coercion

## Summary

Martin's ethical rewrite of a common sales objection-handling script. The original source (a LinkedIn-style post) advocated diagnosing objections rather than arguing with them — sound advice — but undermined itself with coercive assumptions (*"price was never the problem," "need to think about it is never truly the truth"*) and contemptuous framing (*"Karen" and "Samantha" jabs*). Martin's rewrite keeps the edge — diagnosing fog in the buying decision — while removing the coercive posture. The result: six diagnostic questions that help both sides see whether there is a real deal. Connects to [[PHAROS Procurement-Unblock Sprint]] and [[PHAROS External Proof Packet — Procurement-Unblock 2026-04-28]] as practical sales methodology.

## Context

This document is applied sales methodology for the PHAROS consulting context — a governance-heavy, high-ticket offer where buyers need to trust the consultant's judgment more than their own objections. The framing (*"remove fog from the decision"*) aligns with PHAROS's epistemic stance: make the situation legible, don't trap the client.

The rewrite demonstrates the same principle applied in [[AI Governance Manifesto — Upstream Institutional Practice]] — challenge assumptions instead of agreeing with them, and be adversarial in service of clarity.

## Details

### The Diagnostic Principle

> *"Do not argue with objections, diagnose them."*

This is the sound core of the original. Objections are often fog — the buyer doesn't have language for what they're actually uncertain about. The seller's job is to remove the fog, not win the argument.

**What the seller can diagnose:**
- Is the price objection a comparison to a competitor, doing nothing, or a pre-built number?
- Is the hesitation about the outcome, the investment, the timing, or the fit?
- Is budget actually the constraint, or is the offer structurally wrong?

### The Six Diagnostic Questions

**1. "Compared to what?"**
Reveals whether they're comparing to: a competitor, a cheaper alternative, doing nothing, or an internal budget expectation.

**2. "What would need to be true for this to feel like a worthwhile investment?"**
Finds the missing belief — the outcome, timeline, proof, risk, or budget structure needed. Moves from haggling to discovering the actual gap.

**3. "If this solved the problem you came in with, would the investment still feel too heavy?"**
Separates price concern from value concern without pretending money is irrelevant.

**4. "If nothing changes over the next six months, what does that cost you?"**
Helps the buyer compare the cost of action with the cost of delay — especially relevant in compliance and regulatory contexts like PHAROS.

**5. "That makes sense. Is what you need to think through more about the result, the investment, the timing, or whether this is the right fit?"**
Gives the buyer a safe way to name the actual concern rather than hiding behind "I need to think about it."

**6. "If budget were not the constraint, would this be the solution you would choose?"**
Distinguishes: (a) the offer is right but the commercial structure needs work → fix the structure; (b) the offer isn't the right fit → still a fit problem to solve.

### What to Avoid

The original script failed on:
- **Mind-reading as fact** — *"price was never the problem"* assumes too much; prices can actually be too high
- **Contemptuous framing** — naming buyer types pejoratively undercuts the authority of the piece
- **Coercive pressure** — trapping the client into yes is not the same as helping them decide

> *"The point is not to pressure people into buying. The point is to remove fog from the decision. Good objection handling does not trap the client. It helps both sides see whether there is a real deal there."*
