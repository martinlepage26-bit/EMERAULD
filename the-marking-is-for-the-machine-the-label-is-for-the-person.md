# The Marking Is for the Machine, the Label Is for the Person

Article 50 of the EU AI Act became applicable on August 2, 2026. The easy reading, a labeling law for AI content, misreads the structure. It creates two obligations aimed at two different addressees, and the gap between them is the real governance question.

The regulatory detail below draws on a practitioner's public commentary on the article, not an independent legal reading, and the transitional-regime dates deserve verification before anyone relies on them for compliance planning.

Four situations. Chatbots must disclose, by first interaction, that the user is talking to a machine, unless already obvious. Synthetic content needs a machine-readable technical marking, a provider duty. Emotion-recognition and biometric-categorization systems must inform the people exposed to them. Deepfakes and AI-generated public-interest text need a perceptible label, a deployer duty, with an exception for text under human review or editorial responsibility.

A transitional regime under the Digital Omnibus on AI, dated July 8, 2026, gives providers already on the market until December 2, 2026 for the technical marking alone; every other obligation is live now. Most AI-generated content, though, never gets a visible label. The perceptible label is reserved for legal deepfakes and a narrow slice of public-interest text, while the broader technical marking is machine-readable, built for detection infrastructure rather than for a reader.

That split creates a predictable incentive. A provider can satisfy the machine-facing tier, an invisible watermark checked by platforms and researchers, while the person actually reading or talking to the system gets little. A chatbot states once that it is a chatbot. A public-interest text can clear the visible-label requirement through nominal editorial review, however substantive that review was, since the exception turns on editorial control rather than a measured threshold of human authorship.

This is the familiar failure of disclosure regimes judged on whether information exists rather than on who can act on it. The technical marking builds an audit trail legible to regulators and platforms. The perceptible label, meant for an ordinary person, is scoped narrowly enough that most content a reader actually sees will never carry it.

The Commission's July 20 guidelines, a voluntary code of practice, and optional labeling icons sit on top of the binding article as adoption aids, not obligations. A voluntary layer paired with a four-month grace period on the hardest technical duty signals where the friction is expected.

The question worth tracking is not whether providers will mark synthetic content; most will, since it is checkable. It is whether the person-facing obligations get equal seriousness once the machine-facing tier is already checked off.
