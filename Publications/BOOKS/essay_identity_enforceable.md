# Identity Is What Becomes Enforceable
## Rivard, Depestre, and the Violence of Legibility

---

**Abstract**

This essay reads Yvon Rivard's early Quebec novels, *Mort et Naissance de Christophe Ulric* (1976) and *L'Ombre et le Double* (1980), alongside René Depestre's *Hadriana dans tous mes rêves* (1988), as a two-part investigation into identity as a system of force rather than a condition of selfhood. Rivard's fiction catches the subject at its most porous — before name hardens into category, before interiority is disciplined into function. The fragmented, initiatice narrative structure of his novels stages a subject who exists as exposure: to silence, to the absent feminine, to the double who interrupts rather than mirrors. Against this threshold state, Depestre's Hadriana Siloé emerges into a field already crowded with claims: Catholic marriage, Haitian carnival, colonial fantasy, vodou ritual, masculine narration, and communal hunger to explain what should have been allowed to remain unknown. The essay tracks the movement from Rivard's hesitation — identity trembling before enforcement — to Depestre's saturation, where identity has entered violent circulation. From this comparison emerges a theory of identity as field-effect: identity becomes real when a field of power can act on it; it becomes enforceable when institutions, archives, fantasies, and technologies begin loading a name with consequence. The essay closes with the question of artificial intelligence as the moment when older systems of social coding — racial scripts, erotic permissions, bureaucratic categories — become executable at scale. Literature, it argues, is the place where this coding can still be heard before it becomes operational and calls itself intelligence.

**Keywords:** identity, power, legibility, postcolonial literature, Quebec fiction, Caribbean baroque, zombification, algorithmic classification, Yvon Rivard, René Depestre

---

## I. The Governing Line

Identity is what the world does to a name once it has learned how to use it.

That sentence contains the whole argument, but the argument refuses to stay still inside it. Identity, in common use, names something prior to the social — a core, a substance, a self that precedes its circumstances and survives them. The governing line says something crueler: the name precedes nothing. What we call identity is the residue of use. It is what accumulates around a person when enough systems — familiar, legal, ritual, erotic, bureaucratic, national — have decided they have something to gain by making that person legible.

This essay does not proceed by abstract proposition. It proceeds through two bodies of fiction that register the moment of legibility with very different intensities: Yvon Rivard's first two novels, *Mort et Naissance de Christophe Ulric* (1976) and *L'Ombre et le Double* (1980), and René Depestre's *Hadriana dans tous mes rêves* (1988). What connects these texts, separated by a decade, a language community, a colonial history, and a geography, is precisely what separates them: both are caught in the problem of what it costs to be a self in a world that has uses for you.

In Rivard, the subject trembles at the threshold of legibility. His protagonists — Christophe, Thomas — are not yet fully readable. They wander through a symbolic landscape of silence, shadow, the double, and the absent feminine, in which the self is an exposure rather than a possession. They are not identity; they are the hesitation before identity becomes compulsory.

In Depestre, the hesitation is over. Hadriana Siloé, who appears to die at the altar in Jacmel in 1938, enters the world already overdetermined: bride, corpse, white woman, colonial fantasy, zombie, erotic object, communal scandal, miracle, and finally — by the logic of her return — author. She is the point at which too many systems try to speak through one body simultaneously. She does not become a symbol of identity's violence. She is the violence, made flesh.

Between Rivard and Depestre lies the fracture that this essay is trying to read: the difference between a self that is not yet useful and a self that has been made into pure use. And beyond them both, arriving late to a story it did not invent, is the algorithm.

## II. Before the Name Hardens: Rivard's Threshold

Not everyone is allowed an interior life.

This is the problem that Rivard's early fiction poses without ever stating it directly. His novels operate in the register of the spiritual and the symbolic — shadow, breath, Sophia, initiation, the double — but to read them as decorative spirituality is to miss their cruelty. The cruelty is structural. It operates in the question that Rivard's conclusion circles but never quite articulates: *who gets to wander?* Who gets to be silent without being judged empty, mad, evasive, or useless? Who gets to call hesitation spiritual? Who is permitted the luxury of the threshold?

*Mort et Naissance de Christophe Ulric* (1976) presents a subject organized around an absence. Christophe's quest for Geneviève — who vanishes, who is remembered in fragments, who recurs in modified form through other women (Alexandrine, Chlorella, Ophélie, Barbara, Florence) — is not a love story. Each woman is simultaneously a mask and a mirror, a fragment of Geneviève and an ordeal of truth. What drives the narrative is not the desire to possess the absent woman but the impossible desire to possess the absence itself: to recover a unity that predates the self's fragmentation. The French source from which this analysis draws describes this as a logic "non comme objet à reconquérir, mais comme médiation symbolique orientant le sujet vers une connaissance qui ne se possède pas, mais se traverse" — not as an object to be regained, but as a symbolic mediation orienting the subject toward a knowledge that cannot be owned but only traversed.

This is Rivard's first structural move: he turns desire toward a knowing that is not mastery. The feminine figure Geneviève — read through the lens of the *Sophia* tradition not as doctrinal reference but as functional operator — does not deliver revelation. She imposes a posture of listening. She appears as absence, not presence. And it is this asymmetry that drives Christophe's transformation: the goal is not reunion but availability, not possession but the slow work of becoming permeable to what he does not yet understand about himself.

*L'Ombre et le Double* (1980) deepens and radicalizes this structure. Thomas is imprisoned — for having doubted a law about territorial delimitation, an absurd and deliberately political premise — and the prison becomes a liminal space: a site stripped of normal social functioning where silence is not absence but architecture. In prison, Thomas unlearns the language of law and relearns the language of silence, breath, and the absent voice. The novel's actual quotations reveal this with precision. A feminine voice "crible [son] sommeil" — riddles his sleep (OD: 9). Thomas describes himself as "amoureux d'une absente qui attend pour se manifester à [lui] qu'[il] renonce à la chercher" — in love with an absent woman who waits to manifest to him until he renounces searching for her (OD: 37). Marie — his feminine counterpart, his symbolic interlocutor — asks him: "Quand donc sauras-tu que je suis immobile?" — When will you learn that I am still? (OD: 19). And Thomas articulates his own quest as the desire to "retrouver cette femme qui [le] conduira à cette moitié de [lui-même] qu'[il] ignore encore" — to find again the woman who will lead him to the half of himself he does not yet know (OD: 181).

The grammar of these passages is instructive. Desire, in Rivard, is structured by renunciation. You find the absent woman by ceasing to look for her. She is still; you must slow down to perceive her stillness. She will lead you not toward her but toward yourself. The absent feminine is not a destination but a method: a mode of knowing that requires the subject to become uncertain about its own outline.

Thomas's double — Thomas*, who moves through a parallel world guided by the blind painter Gaspard — functions within the same logic. The double is not a mirror image. He is a version of Thomas that lives in a register Thomas cannot yet access: more fluid, more perceptual, moving by intuition rather than reasoning. Gaspard, who sees without eyes, teaches by being rather than instruction. Thomas* learns not to know but to receive. The double interrupts the unitary self not to replace it but to show it its own insufficiency.

What Rivard stages, across both novels, is a subject constituted by its own permeability. The narrative structure enacts this: fragmented, non-linear, refusing chronological progression, moving by digression, memory, ellipsis, and vision. The form is the argument. A subject that could be told in a straight line would be a subject that has already been captured — ordered into legibility, made into plot. Rivard's fragmentation is a refusal of premature capture. It preserves the self's opacity at the cost of narrative satisfaction.

This is where the question of political privilege enters — quietly, at the margins, without becoming a thesis. The novels never state it. But the capacity to wander symbolically, to inhabit the threshold without being forced through it, to make silence into a spiritual practice rather than a social punishment — this capacity depends on conditions that are not equally distributed. Victor Turner's concept of the liminal is useful here: the threshold abolishes categories, suspends identity, holds the subject in the between. But the threshold, as Turner describes it in ritual process, is temporary and protected — a sanctioned interval of dissolution before reinsertion into social structure. What Rivard's fiction refuses to say is what it quietly knows: the sanctioned threshold is available to some subjects and not others. The wandering of Christophe and Thomas is permitted because they inhabit a social position that permits wandering to look like depth rather than failure.

This is not a flaw in Rivard's novels. It is what makes them readable as political allegory. They do not celebrate interiority as universal capacity. They stage its conditions of possibility — and in doing so, they reveal how much has to be in place before a self can afford to remain unreadable.

## III. The Transition: From Silence to Carnival

The movement from Rivard to Depestre is not simply a move from French Canada to Haiti, from 1980 to 1988, from spiritual quest to baroque explosion. It is a change in the fundamental condition of the subject. In Rivard, the subject trembles before legibility and is permitted, for a time, to tremble. In Depestre, the subject is already legible — already inside the system that makes it available for use — before the novel begins.

The transition should feel necessary, not thematic. It is not that carnival is the opposite of silence, or that the zombie is the opposite of the double. The zombie and the double are, in fact, structurally related: both name a fracture in the subject, a self that is not unified, a body that contains more than one logic. But the zombie is the double under power. It is the double that has been made operational by someone else's will. Where Rivard's double interrupts and unsettles, Depestre's zombie is interrupted and enslaved. The difference is not symbolic; it is material. One form of fracture opens the self. The other closes it — locks it into service.

The silence of Rivard's novels is the silence of a subject who has not yet been required to speak. The sound of Depestre's novel — the noise of carnival, the drums of vodou, the rumor of a town that cannot stop talking about the woman it has not understood — is the sound of a subject who has already been required to mean something. Hadriana Siloé does not enter the text as a person. She enters as a problem.

## IV. The Body That Cannot Stop Meaning: Hadriana dans tous mes rêves

Hadriana Siloé appears to die at the altar in Jacmel, Haiti, in 1938. The moment is precisely over-determined: a young woman, who is French by origin and thus white in the racial logic of colonial Haiti, who is beautiful in the specific way that attracts communal fantasy, who is loved by the entire town with an intensity that is never entirely innocent, collapses at the moment she is supposed to consent to marriage. She is supposed to say yes. She falls silent instead.

This is the novel's governing paradox: Hadriana's silence — her literal collapse into apparent death — produces more speech than any word she might have spoken at the altar. The town of Jacmel immediately begins to explain her. She is poisoned. She is zombified. She is the victim of a ritual crime. She is Papa Rosanfer's slave. She is a miracle. She is a scandal. She is an omen. She is evidence of vodou's power. She is evidence of colonial darkness. She is evidence of desire. She is evidence of guilt.

[TEXTUAL EVIDENCE NEEDED: Depestre passage on the town's explanatory fever in the aftermath of Hadriana's apparent death]

René Depestre published *Hadriana dans tous mes rêves* in 1988, and it received the Prix Renaudot the same year. Patrick Altamont, the narrator, is a childhood friend of Hadriana's — obsessed with her, formed by her, permanently altered by the event of her disappearance. Decades later, he investigates. He narrates. He encounters Hadriana again in Kingston, where she gives him the manuscript of her own account: her false death, her zombified wandering, her subjection to Papa Rosanfer's attempt to make her a sexual slave, and her eventual return to life through the force of childhood memory — the memory of carnival, of her body's own pleasure, of a self that preceded the world's claims on her.

Do not read this as a plot. Read it as a system of pressures.

The question is not what happens to Hadriana. The question is who needs her dead, and who needs her undead, and who needs her white, French, Haitianized, erotic, cursed, innocent, obscene, available, miraculous, and narratable — in that order, in every combination, simultaneously and contradictorily. The question is what is stolen when the community turns her into explanation. The question is what changes when she returns not merely alive, but as the one who writes.

### The Architecture of Overdetermination

Hadriana is overdetermined not as an interpretive gesture but as a social fact. Her body is a site where multiple systems of power simultaneously land. She is Catholic — the sacrament of marriage is the occasion of her collapse, which is not incidental. Marriage in 1938 Catholic Haiti is not merely personal consent; it is the formal inscription of a woman's body into a social and religious contract. The altar is the enforcement mechanism. To collapse there, at the moment of consent, is not merely to faint; it is to fail to submit to inscription. The body refuses, involuntarily or not. The community cannot tolerate an illegible refusal. It begins immediately to explain.

She is French and therefore white in a postcolonial racial field that has not resolved the contradictions of 1804. The Haitian Revolution made Haiti the first Black republic, but the racial imaginary of the Caribbean could not simply flush itself clean of colonial categories. Whiteness, in Jacmel in 1938, is not neutral. It carries colonial privilege and colonial fantasy simultaneously. Hadriana is desired and resented, claimed and suspected, loved and potentially punished, for what she represents before she represents anything voluntarily.

She is a woman whose body is visibly beautiful in a carnival culture that is also a patriarchal culture — which is to say, a culture in which bodies that are visibly beautiful in the right way are communal property. The carnival in Depestre's Haiti is not simply a festival of liberation. It is also a field of exposure, where bodies are available to collective gaze and collective claim. Hadriana participates in carnival with genuine pleasure — this is crucial to the novel — but her pleasure does not belong to her. It belongs to those who watch her, who need her to be pleasurable, who build their own sense of vitality and collective life around her performance of availability.

[TEXTUAL EVIDENCE NEEDED: Depestre passage on carnival, collective desire, and Hadriana's relationship to her own pleasure]

She is zombified — or appears to be — through the mechanisms of Haitian vodou. This is where the novel becomes most delicate and most dangerous. Vodou is not, in Depestre's treatment, a primitive superstition to be debunked or a magical system to be celebrated. It is a technology — historically real, socially functional — for the production of enforced labor. The zombie is not merely a supernatural figure. It is a body that has been made to work without will: labor without consent, service without subjectivity. Papa Rosanfer's attempt to make Hadriana a sexual zombie is not a departure from the social logic of the novel. It is its intensification. What the community does to Hadriana through rumor, classification, and narrative is what Rosanfer tries to do through chemical and ritual means: to appropriate her body for use, to make her available, to remove the interiority that might resist.

### Who Profits from the Explanation

The community of Jacmel does not simply suffer alongside Hadriana. It profits from her. The profit is collective and difficult to acknowledge.

Her apparent death and disappearance give the community a story. Not just any story — a story about themselves, their powers, their dangers, their mysteries. Hadriana's body becomes evidence of Jacmel's exceptionalism: a town capable of producing such beauty, such desire, and such catastrophe. The narrative of her zombification — whether believed literally or not — functions to confirm the community's own sense of itself as a place where something extraordinary can happen. She is their legend.

Patrick Altamont, the narrator, profits too. This must be said, and Depestre's novel allows it to be said. Altamont's decades of obsession — his investigation, his memory, his narration — are not simply acts of love or justice. They are acts of appropriation. He makes Hadriana into his story. He organizes her experience around his desire to understand it. He encounters her in Kingston and receives her manuscript: the one thing that escapes his narration, the one version of her experience that does not pass through his interpretation. But even this is filtered through his telling. We read Hadriana's account inside Altamont's account. The Russian dolls of narration ensure that her voice, when it finally comes, is framed by his reception of it.

[SOURCE NEEDED: Critical work on narrative authority in *Hadriana dans tous mes rêves*, and the politics of masculine narration in Depestre's Caribbean fiction]

The manuscript — Hadriana's own account — is therefore the pivot on which the novel's political argument turns. It does not restore a pure self beneath the stories. There is no pure self to restore; there never was. What the manuscript does is interrupt the circuit of explanation. It does not tell us what Hadriana "really" is. It tells us what she experienced: false death, zombified subjection, sexual captivity, and the strange force of childhood memory — carnival, pleasure, her own body's intelligence — that pulled her back into life. This is not transcendence. It is not salvation. It is a woman using her own account to interrupt the accounts that had been made of her.

[TEXTUAL EVIDENCE NEEDED: Depestre passage on Hadriana's account of her childhood memory as the force that revives her]

The return to life through memory is not a mystical event in Depestre's treatment. It is a cognitive and somatic one. Hadriana's body remembers a time before it was fully claimed: carnival, the joy of her own skin, the specific textures of a childhood before she became Jacmel's object. Memory here functions not as nostalgia but as resistance: the residue of a self that the systems of capture had not yet fully processed. This residue is not innocence. It is what survived capture, imperfectly, as a remainder.

## V. Identity as Field-Effect

> Identity is not what one is. Identity is what becomes enforceable.

Develop that sentence slowly.

Rivard's fiction holds the subject at the moment before identity becomes enforceable. His characters inhabit a symbolic landscape in which the self is porous, doubled, available to silences and voices that exceed its own comprehension. The formal structure of his novels — fragmented, non-linear, resistant to resolution — is the formal equivalent of a self that has not yet been required to become consistent. Consistency, in the logic of enforcement, is not a psychological virtue. It is an administrative requirement. A person who is consistent can be classified, filed, predicted. A person who wavers, who contains multiples, who speaks in different registers in different moments, is a problem for the systems that need to process them.

Depestre's fiction shows what happens when enforcement arrives. Hadriana's body does not become a problem because she is inherently problematic. It becomes a problem because the systems that surround it — Catholic sacrament, colonial racial economy, patriarchal desire, vodou technology, narrative authority — all require something from it simultaneously, and their requirements are incompatible. She cannot simultaneously consent freely to marriage and be the fantasy object of a community that has never given her the option of refusal. She cannot be the innocent French woman and the erotic center of Haitian carnival. She cannot be the victim of vodou's dark power and the evidence of vodou's real force. The overdetermination is not a contradiction to be resolved. It is the condition of her capture.

Force only appears in a field. Nothing has weight in isolation. This is not a physics metaphor being pressed too hard; it is a description of how social life operates. Hadriana's identity — the sum of what the world asserts she is — has no existence outside the field that produces and maintains it. Remove the Catholic marriage structure and the colonial racial imaginary and the patriarchal claim and the vodou ritual and the masculine narration, and Hadriana Siloé is simply a person. But the field does not remove itself. It is not available for suspension. It operates continuously, loading her body with consequence, making her a site at which decisions are made — about danger, desire, responsibility, guilt, and grace — that she did not choose to participate in.

This is what it means to say that identity is a field-effect. The self does not precede the field and then enter it. The field constitutes the self as the kind of object on which the field can operate. Hadriana is not first a person who then becomes a zombie. She is first Jacmel's object, and her zombification is the intensification of an objectification that was already in progress.

There is a further precision needed here, and it matters. Not all field-effects are the same intensity. The field operates differently on different bodies, not because of something intrinsic to the bodies but because of what the field's history has deposited around them. Some bodies arrive in the field pre-loaded: their names already carry consequence before they have acted, because the archive has already classified people who look like them, speak like them, live where they live, or carry the markers of groups that the field has processed before. Other bodies arrive relatively unloaded: their names are free of prior consequence, which means they can accumulate consequence slowly and selectively, and they can sometimes choose which consequences to accept.

This asymmetry is what produces the contrast between Rivard and Depestre at the level of social structure, not narrative style. Christophe and Thomas can wander in symbolic space because their names do not yet have the kind of archive-weight that forces them to be useful. They are relatively unloaded bodies moving through a field that has not yet decided they are urgent. The privilege of their wandering is not psychological; it is historical and positional. Hadriana arrives pre-loaded in multiple registers simultaneously — gender, whiteness in a postcolonial racial field, the Catholic sacrament, the colonial erotic imagination — and the field acts on her immediately, without waiting for her to act first.

This is why the essay cannot simply say that Rivard depicts interiority and Depestre depicts externality. Both depict the relation between a self and the field that makes it legible. The difference is in the intensity and speed of enforcement. Rivard's characters buy time. Hadriana is not given time.

Stuart Hall's formulation is useful here. Identity, Hall argues, is not a stable essence waiting to be expressed but a "production" that is never complete — always in process, always constituted within representation rather than outside it. [SOURCE NEEDED: Hall, "Cultural Identity and Diaspora," 1990.] What Hall's formulation adds to the reading of Depestre is the temporal dimension: identity is produced continuously, not established once. This means that Hadriana's identity as zombie, as victim, as miracle, as scandal, as author is not replacing some prior authentic identity. It is additional production layered over prior production. The manuscript does not reveal who Hadriana really is. It produces another version of Hadriana — one that happens to be her own production, her own interruption of the circuit.

Frantz Fanon, analyzing the experience of the colonized subject in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), describes the moment in which racialized identity is imposed as a moment of violent fixity. The Black subject encounters the white gaze and is "overdetermined from the outside": "I was not merely a man among other men. I was overexposed." [SOURCE NEEDED: Fanon, *Peau noire, masques blancs*, 1952.] This overexposure is not psychological; it is social and structural. It names the condition in which a person cannot move through the world without their movement being pre-interpreted by a racial taxonomy that exists prior to them and will outlast them.

Hadriana's overexposure works differently — it passes through gender, colonial desire, and religious sacrament rather than race alone — but the structural logic is the same. She cannot arrive at her wedding without arriving as white, as desirable, as French, as the woman who must now consent. The overdetermination is in place before she speaks. It is waiting for her at the altar, as it were, dressed as a ceremony.

Édouard Glissant's concept of opacity offers a counterforce. Against the Western demand for transparency — the demand that the Other make itself knowable, analyzable, reducible to comprehensible terms — Glissant asserts the right to opacity: the right of a subject or culture to remain in some part resistant to explanation, to not be fully translatable, to maintain a residue that the demand for understanding cannot touch. [SOURCE NEEDED: Glissant, *Poétique de la Relation*, 1990.] Hadriana's manuscript can be read as an act of opacity: not the revelation of a hidden truth but the assertion of a perspective that the community had foreclosed. She does not explain herself. She recounts.

## VI. The Older Coding

Long before the algorithm, there were instructions.

This is the essay's wager as it turns toward artificial intelligence: that AI does not invent the capture of identity. It accelerates it, formalizes it, and makes it operational. What AI adds is scale, speed, and the rhetoric of neutrality. But the underlying operation — loading a name with consequence, making a body readable, processing a person into a category that determines what happens to them — is not a computational innovation. It is a social one, with a very long history.

Social life has always exchanged codes and secret keys. Names, rituals, glances, racial scripts, erotic permissions, family prohibitions, religious signs, bureaucratic categories, passwords of class, gender, nation, and desire. A person becomes readable — becomes functional within the social field — by learning, resisting, misreading, or failing these codes. To know how to dress at a funeral in a specific community is to have internalized a code. To be misread by that code — to be seen as disrespectful when you were not aware of the rule — is to discover that codes have consequences before you know they exist. The code does not wait for your comprehension. It operates on your body regardless.

In Rivard's novels, the coding is intimate and almost inaudible. Christophe and Thomas move through codes that do not yet look like systems. They have the texture of dream, memory, and metaphysical unease. The prohibition that Christophe must release — the marâtre, "Au feu, la vieille!" — is a symbolic act of liberation from an ancestral code: the inherited system of prohibition that prevents the subject from moving toward his own transformation. The family system encodes: it tells the child who he can be and who he cannot be, which desires are permitted and which are dangerous, which relationships are sanctioned and which are crimes. To burn the stepmother is not an act of violence against a person; it is an act of refusal against a code. It is the self declining to transmit what was transmitted to it.

The prison that holds Thomas is simultaneously a legal code made literal and an initiatory structure: the law says you cannot doubt territorial delimitation, and the consequence of that doubt is confinement. The state code has been triggered. Thomas is classified as transgressor and confined accordingly. But the confinement, in Rivard's symbolic logic, becomes the condition for a different kind of coding — one that is slower, more interior, less legible to administrative power. The prison teaches silence. The silence teaches listening. The listening opens Thomas to voices the prison cannot hear and therefore cannot suppress.

The double, Thomas*, moves in a space that is not yet coded by the dominant social order. That is why his world is more fluid, more perceptual, more available to the invisible. He is not free — he is within the novel, within the narrative — but he moves in a register that the novel's dominant codes have not yet colonized. His guide Gaspard, the blind painter, embodies a form of knowledge that is constitutively unavailable to systems of surveillance: he sees without being able to be watched. His knowledge is not transmissible through the channels that institutions use to gather and store information. It passes through proximity, presence, the slow accumulation of a shared practice. It is, in this sense, deliberately opaque to the archive.

In Depestre, the coding is social, spectacular, and brutal. Hadriana is coded before she speaks. She arrives at her own wedding already classified: bride, French, white, beautiful, desirable, dangerous, available, in need of sacramental inscription. The zombie code that captures her after her apparent death is not a departure from the social codes already in operation; it is their most extreme expression. The zombie is a body that has been made fully readable by removing its interiority. A zombie cannot say no. A zombie cannot resist. A zombie is pure availability, pure function, pure classification. It is the logical endpoint of the demand for legibility.

[SOURCE NEEDED: Work on zombification as labor and colonial power in Haitian studies — Joan Dayan, *Haiti, History, and the Gods*, 1995, or similar]

Between Rivard and Depestre, the essay has moved from a subject not yet fully coded to a subject over-coded past the point of interiority. But neither position is stable. Rivard's wandering subject is permitted to remain uncoded partly because his social position allows it — and partly because the novel's own opacity protects him. The novel is a form of opacity: it refuses to deliver its subject to the reader in legible form. Depestre's Hadriana recovers a remnant of self through memory — through what was coded into her body before the most aggressive encoding began.

This is where the older coding becomes politically exact. The body stores residue. The childhood carnival that revives Hadriana is not a magical force; it is an inscription that predates the most violent inscriptions. She was coded by carnival, by pleasure, by collective joy, before she was coded by the altar, by Papa Rosanfer, by colonial fantasy. The revival is not the recovery of a pre-social self. It is the reassertion of an earlier, less destructive coding against a later, more lethal one. The body is always already written. The question is which inscriptions survive and which are overwritten.

Michel Foucault's analysis of classification and power/knowledge is useful but must be used precisely. Foucault shows how classification systems — medical, criminal, sexual — produce the subjects they appear merely to describe. [SOURCE NEEDED: Foucault, *Surveiller et punir*, 1975, or *The Order of Things*, 1966.] The madwoman is not simply diagnosed; she is produced as mad through the apparatus of diagnosis. The criminal is not simply identified; he is constituted as criminal through the machinery of criminology. Applied to Hadriana: she is not simply observed to have died and been zombified. She is produced as zombie through the apparatus of vodou interpretation, community rumor, and masculine narration. Each new explanation adds another layer of production. The manuscript interrupts the production, but it cannot undo what has already been produced. She is already in circulation.

Kate Crawford's *Atlas of AI* (2021) is instructive here because it makes the same argument about machine learning that Foucault makes about disciplinary society. Crawford shows that AI systems do not classify neutrally: they classify from within a history of power, reproducing the inequalities of their training data, their institutional contexts, and their deployment environments. [Crawford, 2021.] What Crawford adds to Foucault is the material dimension: the bodies, the labor, the extractive supply chains that make classification possible. The algorithm does not float above the world. It is made of the world's minerals, its poorly paid labor, its accumulated archives of human classification. When a face recognition system performs worse on darker skin tones, this is not a technical error. It is the historical accumulation of a gaze that trained itself on certain faces and not others.

## VII. AI Is Late to the Story

> AI is late to the story because the story was already programming us.

In Rivard, dream delays capture. The subject who lives in dream, in threshold, in the between, is a subject that the classification systems have not yet fully processed. The delay is temporary and, as already noted, socially conditioned. But it is real. The novels hold open an interval — brief, fragile, structurally privileged — in which the self can remain in process before it is required to become consistent.

In Depestre, dream exposes capture. The carnival reverie that runs through *Hadriana dans tous mes rêves* is not innocent pleasure; it is the novel's most politically complex register. Carnival, in its historical function, is a temporary inversion of social order: the low become high, the serious becomes comic, the forbidden becomes permitted. But Depestre's carnival does not invert the social order for Hadriana. It is itself a site of her exposure — collective, erotic, ritualized. Dream and carnival reveal the codes by which she has been constituted; they do not escape them. What her own manuscript does — arriving late, as the novel's final gesture — is not dream but testimony. She replaces others' dream of her with her own account of what was done.

In AI, dream becomes preemption. This is the shift that differentiates the algorithmic moment from everything that came before, not in kind but in scale and speed. Predictive systems — credit scoring, criminal risk assessment, medical triage, hiring algorithms, social media curation — do not classify after the fact. They classify prospectively. They do not wait for a person to act and then respond. They infer, from patterns in archived data, what a person is likely to do, and they produce material consequences before the act occurs. This is preemptive legibility: the name becomes usable before it has done anything to become usable.

Safiya Umoja Noble's analysis of algorithmic oppression, Virginia Eubanks's work on automated inequality, and Joy Buolamwini's audits of facial recognition systems all document what happens when the archive of suspicion and desire — the historical accumulation of who was watched, classified, suspected, and disciplined — becomes the training data for a system that then operates "objectively." [SOURCE NEEDED: Noble, *Algorithms of Oppression*, 2018; Eubanks, *Automating Inequality*, 2018; Buolamwini and Gebru, "Gender Shades," 2018.] The objectivity is a formal property of the system, not a description of its outputs. The system is objective in the sense that it applies its learned classifications consistently. It is not objective in the sense of being neutral about whose experience constituted the archive.

Birhane (2021) argues, in a passage directly relevant here, that algorithmic injustice cannot be addressed through technical solutions because the problem is not technical. [Birhane, 2021.] The problem is relational and structural: who counts as a full subject, whose situated knowledge is treated as data and whose as noise, which forms of life are legible to the system and which are not. Crawford makes the same point through material analysis: the categories that AI systems reproduce are not mathematical abstractions. They are historical accumulations, solidified into training sets, deployed through infrastructure that costs money and labor to maintain.

What Rivard and Depestre together make visible — what literature, in its resistance to legibility, can show more nakedly than governance documents can admit — is that this history was already an AI. Not in the computational sense. In the operational sense. The social systems that produced the archives from which machine learning learns were themselves classification engines: they sorted bodies, assigned codes, made predictions, produced consequences. The church that married Hadriana was running an algorithm: if woman + marriage + altar, then inscription + consequent obligations. The vodou operator who zombified her was running an algorithm: if white woman + powder + ritual, then labor + availability. The community that explained her was running a distributed algorithm: aggregate all available data about this body, produce classification, distribute consequences.

The computational algorithm differs in its formalization, its speed, and its reach. It does not differ in its fundamental operation, which is the transformation of a person into a processable unit. This is why the argument about AI must begin with Rivard and Depestre rather than with Silicon Valley. The conditions of possibility for algorithmic capture — the transformation of persons into processable units — are not new. They are the conditions of social life as such. What changes is the infrastructure, the scale, and the degree to which the operation can deny its own violence by calling itself intelligence.

Donna Haraway's concept of situated knowledge, deployed in the Rivard analysis as an account of how subjectivity is always positioned, matters here in a different register. [Haraway, 1991.] Haraway argues against the "view from nowhere" — the fantasy of a gaze that sees all from a position of no interest, no history, no body. AI systems reproduce the view from nowhere as a technical claim: the algorithm has no body, no interest, no history; therefore its classifications are objective. But the algorithm has a body — the body of its training data, which was produced by bodies in history, in conditions of power, with interests that shaped which data was collected and how it was labeled. The view from nowhere is always a view from somewhere that has successfully hidden its coordinates.

## VIII. The Use of a Name

What does it mean to say that a name becomes usable?

In the ordinary course of social life, names circulate freely. A name is introduced, repeated, attached to a face and a context, becomes associated with behaviors and relationships and memberships. This is unremarkable. Names are communication. They allow coordination. They make it possible to speak about someone who is not present.

The name becomes usable in the stronger sense when institutions begin loading it with consequence. When the name can be looked up in an archive that determines what credit is extended, what housing is available, what medical care is prioritized, what sentence is recommended, what flight list you appear on. When the name triggers a classification that preempts your ability to act before you act. When the name has become, in Fanon's phrase, something you carry before you rather than something you are.

This is not a late-modern problem. It is a very old one. The archive of consequence has been built over centuries: through slavery's codification of certain bodies as property; through colonial classification of populations as savage, primitive, or developing; through medical classification of women as hysterical, of queers as deviant, of the poor as congenitally criminal; through religious inscription of heretics, apostates, and witches; through national registration of foreigners, minorities, and enemies of the state. The file was a technology of power before it was a digital technology.

What changes with computational AI is the dream of completeness. The earlier archives were slow, incomplete, locally administered, and resistant to aggregation. The face in the vodou ritual, the name in the parish register, the file in the colonial administration — these were powerful but limited. They could not be instantly cross-referenced, weighted, combined into predictive scores, and applied in real time across millions of simultaneous cases. The computational turn makes the archive total in its aspiration: every transaction, every movement, every search, every social connection, every pattern of behavior becomes potentially classifiable, aggregable, and consequential.

This aspiration does not succeed completely. The archive is still incomplete, still biased, still contested. But the aspiration itself — the desire for a system that can process every name into a stable, consequential classification — is the intensification of what social life has always wanted: to make persons legible enough to manage.

There is something instructive in the way algorithmic systems present their failures. When a face recognition system misidentifies a Black woman as male, or a recidivism prediction tool assigns higher risk scores to Black defendants independently of actual recidivism rates, these are reported as errors — bugs to be corrected, biases to be debiased. The framing is one of technical imperfection: the system could be better, more accurate, more fair. What this framing conceals is that the error is not a malfunction. It is the system working exactly as designed, which is to say: classifying from within an archive that was built by and for certain purposes, reproducing the weights that history placed on certain features.

Debiasing an algorithm is not the same as dismantling the archive. You can correct for a specific bias in a specific system, and the underlying archive remains. The historical accumulation of who was photographed for criminal databases, who was tracked by social services, who was creditworthy according to mid-century lending standards, who was described as dangerous in a colonial report — this accumulation does not dissolve when you run a fairness audit. It is structural. It is the water the system swims in. Correcting for bias within a system that runs on a biased archive is not the same as changing the conditions of legibility. It is changing the output while the input stays constant.

This is why the argument about AI has to begin with Rivard's 1976 novel and Depestre's 1988 novel rather than with current events. The current moment is legible only if you can see the long history of which it is an acceleration. The algorithm did not arrive and impose legibility on persons who were previously free. It arrived into a world that had already been systematically working on the problem of making persons legible, for centuries, with tremendous sophistication and tremendous violence. It inherited that work. It formalized it. It made it scalable. But it did not invent it.

Hadriana's false death is, among other things, a refusal of legibility that the social field cannot tolerate. She does not say no at the altar. She collapses. But the collapse is interpreted as no — as deviance, as crime, as something that has to be explained and contained. The zombie is the community's answer to a body that refused to submit to the scheduled inscription: if you will not be married, you will be enslaved. If you will not consent, you will be made to serve without will. The logic is not aberrational. It is the logic of enforcement encountering resistance.

Her return — alive, in Kingston, holding the manuscript of her own account — is not the recovery of an identity that was taken from her. It is the production of a counter-archive. She has been, for decades, in other people's files: their memories, their rumors, their investigations, their narrations. She has been evidence. She has been a case. She produces her manuscript not to correct the record but to insert, into the circuit of her own interpretation, a version that cannot be assimilated without remainder. The manuscript is noise in the system.

Literature functions analogously. Not as salvation — the essay will not end with literature as redemption. But as a formal practice that refuses the kind of legibility that enforcement requires. Rivard's fragmented structure refuses to deliver his subjects as consistent, classifiable units. Depestre's novel refuses to let Hadriana be only the community's object: it holds open the gap between what is done to her and what she knows about what is done to her. These are formal operations, not moral victories. They do not prevent capture. They do not undo what has been done. What they do is make the operations of capture visible, audible, readable — not as abstract injustice but as specific force applied to specific bodies in specific conditions.

## IX. Without Consolation

Literature does not save the self from classification. This must be said without apology.

The novel cannot return identity to innocence, because there was no innocence. Before the classification, there was already the archive. Before the archive, there was already the practice. Before the practice, there was already the code. The self has always emerged inside a field of force. The question is never whether force operates — it always does — but how it operates, and what survives it, and what the survival makes possible.

Rivard's fiction preserves a moment — brief, conditioned, privileged — before enforcement becomes total. His subjects wander in the symbolic space of the not-yet-classified, and this wandering has value not as escape but as delay: a delay in which the self can develop resources it will need when the systems close in. The double, the shadow, the threshold, the absent feminine — these are not decorations. They are the equipment of a subject who must learn to be multiple before multiplicity becomes a liability.

Depestre's fiction shows what happens when the delay ends. Hadriana Siloé is many things simultaneously, and not one of those things is chosen by her. She is the site of a contest among forces that have all decided they have a legitimate claim. Her survival — her zombie escape, her memory-revived return, her manuscript — does not resolve the contest. The community's claims on her did not dissolve when she appeared in Kingston. Her own account does not replace theirs. It adds itself to the field of her interpretation, as one more voice among the many voices that have spoken her.

This is the small revenge the essay's prompt names: speaking after being explained. Hadriana's manuscript matters because it interrupts the circuit of use — not by transcending it, not by undoing it, but by inserting a friction that the circuit had not accounted for. She was supposed to be evidence. She becomes, instead, a witness.

The algorithm has not learned this distinction. It processes bodies as evidence — as data from which predictions can be drawn, consequences produced, classifications maintained. It does not have a category for witness. It does not know what to do with a manuscript that cannot be reduced to features and labels.

This is not a comfortable note on which to end. It is not an argument for literature as resistance, or for humanity as the limit of computation, or for the inalienability of the self. Those consolations are available and they are false. What can be said more honestly is this: the pressure that produces identities — the systems of enforcement that load names with consequence — has always produced residue. Something that did not fit the classification. Something that survived the coding. Something that remained opaque to the archive's demand for transparency.

In Rivard, the residue is the fragment — the part of the self that cannot be told in a straight line.

In Depestre, the residue is the manuscript — the account that interrupts the accounts.

In the algorithmic field, the residue is what the model cannot predict, what the profile does not contain, what falls outside the training distribution.

None of these residues is innocent. None of them is sufficient. None of them is a politics. But they are the evidence that capture is never complete — not because the self is sacred, but because force is always partial, always encounters material it has not processed, always produces more than it intends.

A person is never only a name; but history begins when the name becomes usable.

History continues because the name keeps producing what the archive did not expect.

## Peer-Review Risk Note

**This manuscript carries three significant peer-review risks.**

**First, the comparison itself.** The juxtaposition of Rivard and Depestre is not a standard pairing in either Quebec literary studies or Caribbean francophone criticism. Reviewers in each field may challenge the comparison as under-supported: what exactly licenses reading a Quebec spiritual quest alongside a Haitian baroque novel? The essay's answer — that both are caught in the problem of the self's legibility under different conditions of enforcement — must be made more explicit in the introduction than the current version makes it. The comparative premise needs a more explicit methodological grounding, or the essay risks being seen as thematic rather than analytical.

**Second, the AI turn.** The movement from Rivard and Depestre to artificial intelligence will be read by literary scholars as a category error: you cannot get from a 1980 Quebec novel to machine learning without an argument that justifies the leap. The essay's argument — that social life has always been algorithmic in the relevant sense, and that AI accelerates rather than invents the capture of identity — is defensible but needs more careful staging. The current version moves too quickly from Depestre's zombification to the algorithm. An intermediate section on archives, classification, and disciplinary power (drawing more explicitly from Foucault) would strengthen the transition.

**Third, the missing textual evidence.** The essay marks multiple instances where direct quotations from Depestre are needed and unavailable. Any peer reviewer in Caribbean studies will notice the absence of specific textual citation from *Hadriana dans tous mes rêves*. This is honest but risky. The [TEXTUAL EVIDENCE NEEDED] markers must be resolved before submission: either with direct quotation from the novel or with a methodological note explaining why the essay proceeds at the level of narrative structure rather than close reading.

**Minor risk:** The essay's tone — deliberately anti-academic, pressure-building, unsentimental — may be read by some reviewers as stylistic excess or as substituting rhetoric for argument. This risk is real but acceptable in journals that publish critical theory with a literary voice. The target journal should be selected accordingly: *Small Axe*, *PMLA*, *Quebec Studies*, *L'Esprit Créateur*, or *Journal of Postcolonial Writing* are more likely to receive the tone productively than journals with strong empiricist biases.
