# Consent Frameworks — Historical, Legal, Social, and AI Governance

## Summary

Consent is not a single idea but a historical accretion: **the voluntary agreement to engage in an activity**, layered across legal, social, philosophical, and now computational domains. From Roman *consensus* (agreement among the governed) through medieval Christian marriage consent, Enlightenment social contracts, modern medical informed consent, to contemporary AI governance—consent names both a principle and its repeated violation. The framework matters: consent to what, by whom, under what conditions, with what power asymmetries present, and who verifies?

This note maps historical layers, legal tests, social realities, and AI governance implications. The central tension: consent is presented as the solution to power imbalance while operating within systems structured by power imbalance.

## Details

### I. Historical Layers: From Consensus to Autonomy

**Roman *consensus* (classical antiquity):**
- Consensus of the governed; collective agreement as basis for political legitimacy
- Not individual consent but aggregated assent
- Foundation for later theories of popular sovereignty

**Medieval Christian consent (marriage):**
- Marriage consent as freely given agreement of both parties (not parental arrangement alone)
- Canon law recognizes spousal consent as the locus of marital validity
- Consent begins to shift from collective to individual locus

**Enlightenment expansion (17th-18th centuries):**

| Philosopher | Contribution |
|-------------|-------------|
| **Locke** | Consent of the governed; natural rights; voluntary agreement to form political society; people retain authority to revoke consent if government violates rights |
| **Rousseau** | General will as collective consent; direct democratic participation; consent cannot be permanently alienated; represents the people |
| **Hobbes** | Consent to sovereign power as escape from state of nature; absolute consent is binding (though individuals retain right to self-defense) |
| **Montesquieu** | Separation of powers as mechanism to prevent concentration of power and protect consenting parties |
| **Kant** | Moral autonomy and rational will as the ground of consent; treating others as ends in themselves, not means; respect for personhood |

**Key shifts:**
- From collective to individual
- From group assent to active choice
- From *being* governed to *consenting* to governance
- From legal form to ethical/moral grounding

### II. Legal Frameworks

**Consent as legal element:**
- **Criminal law (Canada):** Voluntary agreement to engage in the sexual activity in question (Criminal Code s.265, s.273.1)
- **Contract law:** Informed, voluntary, specific, and revocable agreement to exchange consideration
- **Medical informed consent:** Patient understanding + voluntary agreement + capacity + absence of coercion, applied before treatment
- **Age of consent:** Statutory protection for minors (typically 16–18), presuming inability to consent due to developmental stage

**Consent can be vitiated by:**
- **Fraud (narrow test):** Dishonest act + deprivation (significant risk of serious bodily harm) — high bar in Canadian jurisprudence
- **Duress/coercion:** Threats, economic pressure, power imbalance making "agreement" non-voluntary
- **Incapacity:** Intoxication, mental disability, unconsciousness
- **Lack of information:** Absence of material facts needed to make an informed decision

**Key cases (Canadian):**
- *R v. Mabior* — two-part fraud test; identity deception does not automatically vitiate consent unless serious bodily-harm risk shown
- *R v. Hutchinson* — elaborates fraud and deprivation standard
- *R v. Cuerrier* — consent vitiated by fraud involving serious bodily harm risk
- *R v. Kirkpatrick* — identity and consent relations

**Practical reality:** Whether conduct meets criminal threshold depends on investigator/Crown framing and evidence gathering; prosecution is discretionary.

### III. Social and Power Dimensions

**Consent cannot erase power asymmetry.** It operates within structures already shaped by:

- **Gender:** Historical and ongoing wage gaps, domestic labor unpaid or undervalued, sexual violence endemic
- **Socioeconomic:** Consent under economic desperation (e.g., sex work under precarity) presents the paradox of formal consent masking structural coercion
- **Institutional:** Power imbalance between employer/employee, professor/student, doctor/patient, researcher/subject affects quality of consent even when legally "valid"
- **Cultural:** Variability across cultures in what counts as consent; some cultures emphasize family/community agreement over individual choice
- **Media narratives:** #MeToo movements revealed patterns where "consent" was present in form but absent in substance (silence as coercion, fear of career destruction, social isolation)

**Consent education** recognizes:
- Affirmative consent (explicit "yes" vs. absence of "no")
- Ongoing consent (can be withdrawn; is not permanent)
- Capacity at moment of decision (intoxication, distress affect choice quality)
- Context (power dynamics must be acknowledged, not erased by consent language)

### IV. Enlightenment Philosophy: The Deeper Question

The Enlightenment moved from "What does the sovereign permit?" to "What do people choose?"

But this shift conceals a problem: **Can individuals consent meaningfully within systems they did not choose and cannot easily exit?**

- Locke said people retain the right to revolution if government violates natural rights—but revolutions are costly and dangerous
- Rousseau said the general will reflects the people—but who determines the general will when interests conflict?
- Hobbes said people consent to absolute sovereign—but what if the sovereign betrays them?
- Kant said respect for autonomy requires treating people as ends—but markets and institutions regularly treat people as means

The liberal solution: consent + rights + legal protections. But the protections require enforcement, which requires power, which risks recreating the problem.

### V. AI Governance and Consent

**User consent in AI systems:**
- Consent to data collection and processing
- Consent to algorithmic decision-making (credit, hiring, criminal justice, content moderation)
- Transparency requirement: users cannot consent to what they don't understand
- Withdrawal right: ability to revoke consent and have data deleted

**Problems at scale:**
- **Opacity:** Most people cannot meaningfully understand modern algorithms (neural networks, transformer models, recommendation systems)
- **Asymmetry:** Companies accumulate vast data while users see only aggregate outcomes (not how their data is used)
- **Lock-in:** Opting out of major platforms is difficult; consent becomes quasi-mandatory for social participation
- **Algorithmic bias:** Consent to "fair" algorithms that contain structural bias replicates discrimination at scale

**Developer responsibility:**
- Burden is on developers/companies to implement genuine informed consent, not on users to opt out of exploitative default
- Transparency tooling (explainability, audit trails) is necessary but not sufficient
- Power asymmetry means companies can exploit consent-adjacent language ("you agreed to our terms") without actual informed agreement

**GDPR approach:** Consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous. Withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it. Burden of proof on the organization.

### VI. The Core Tension

Consent is presented as a solution to power imbalance but operates within systems structured by power:

1. **Formal vs. substantive:** Legal consent can exist while real autonomy is absent (sex work under desperation, employment under wage precarity)
2. **Individual vs. structural:** Consent protects individuals at the moment of choice but cannot address institutional structures that constrain choice
3. **Reversibility:** Some choices cannot be undone even if consent is withdrawn (relationship harm, data already used, algorithmic classification)
4. **Verification:** Who checks that consent was genuine? If the powerful verify, they police their own behavior

### VII. Governance Principle for PHAROS

Consent is a **boundary condition, not a solution**.

In [[PHAROS Method — Recursive Governance]]:
- Consent marks the threshold where power is acknowledged and invited (Locke's "consent of the governed")
- But governance must also provide structures that protect those who cannot exit (Montesquieu's separation of powers)
- And verification mechanisms that prevent consent-laundering (where formal consent masks substantive coercion)

Consent matters most where it is hardest: within power imbalances that cannot be eliminated but can be made transparent and contestable.
