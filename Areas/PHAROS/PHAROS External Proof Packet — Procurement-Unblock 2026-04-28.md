## One-Page Brief

### Offer

PHAROS helps AI companies whose enterprise deals are stalled in procurement, audit, vendor review, or executive scrutiny.

In 21 days, PHAROS produces a governance dossier, decision-rights map, evidence pack, and executive response materials that help the buyer understand what is controlled, what is documented, what remains unresolved, and what can be defended.

### Who It Is For

- AI companies with a real enterprise deal under review.
- Founders or operators being asked for governance, auditability, risk, compliance, or human-oversight evidence.
- Teams that already have a product or pilot, but cannot yet explain the governance chain clearly enough for a regulated buyer.

### Problem

Enterprise buyers are using AI to evaluate vendors, but trust has not scaled with AI adoption. Polished claims are easy to generate; defensible evidence is harder to produce.

The procurement blocker is often not "AI ethics" in the abstract. It is a missing proof structure:

- What AI systems are in scope?
- What decisions or workflows do they affect?
- What controls are enforced?
- Who can override, review, or halt use?
- What evidence exists today?
- What remains a bounded gap?

### Deliverables

- Scope map: what is being governed and what is out of scope.
- Risk and consequence map: where the system can affect users, clients, revenue, rights, or regulated decisions.
- Evidence inventory: what documents, logs, policies, controls, tests, and decision records exist.
- Gap register: what is not proven yet, stated plainly.
- Executive response packet: buyer-facing answers for procurement, audit, compliance, or board review.
- Next-control plan: what must be implemented, documented, or retired before broader deployment.

### Timeline

21 days, fixed scope. The sprint is for a live deal with real procurement pressure, not for general brand positioning.

### Positioning Sentence

PHAROS turns AI governance from a reassuring claim into an evidence packet someone else can inspect.

## LinkedIn Sequence — Trimmed To Strongest Posts

### Post 1 — Evidence Over Assertion

AI governance fails when organizations confuse confident language with control.

"We have human oversight."

Where? Who can intervene? At what stage? What evidence shows the intervention happened?

"We monitor risk."

Which risks? How often? Who owns escalation? What happens when the threshold is crossed?

The next phase of AI adoption will reward organizations that can answer those questions without improvising.

Governance has to become inspectable: scope, controls, evidence, gaps, and accountable decision rights.

That is the PHAROS premise.

### Post 2 — Procurement Is A Governance Test

Procurement is often the first place an AI governance claim becomes real.

A buyer does not only ask whether the product works. They ask whether the organization can defend the product under scrutiny.

Where is the system used? What decisions does it affect? What controls are in place? What is logged? Who can halt or override? What remains unresolved?

If those answers are scattered across policy documents, product claims, and informal operator knowledge, the deal slows down.

The issue is not a lack of innovation.

The issue is a lack of governable evidence.

### Post 3 — Bounded Claims Build Trust

The most trustworthy governance packet is not the one that claims everything is solved.

It is the one that separates:

- what is proven;
- what is bounded;
- what is not yet known;
- what must not be claimed.

That distinction matters because enterprise AI decisions now move through buyers, legal teams, compliance teams, security teams, and executives who all need different forms of assurance.

Overclaiming creates risk.

Bounded evidence creates room to proceed.

### Post 4 — Governance As Infrastructure

AI governance is not mainly a principles problem anymore.

It is an infrastructure problem.

Where are the intake rules? Where is provenance captured? Who classifies the artifact? Who approves release? What triggers escalation? How is failure logged? What gets monitored after deployment?

If those questions do not have operational answers, governance remains a statement of intent.

The work is to turn intent into structure.

Use this when an introduction lands:

1. What deal is stuck?
2. What revenue, deployment, or strategic outcome is at risk?
3. Who is blocking the decision?
4. What exactly did they ask for?
5. What documents already exist?
6. What systems, models, workflows, or decisions are in scope?
7. What is the deadline?
8. Who can approve the sprint?
9. What would count as useful movement after 21 days?

Disqualify if there is no live deal, no deadline, no economic buyer, or no governance/evidence blocker.
