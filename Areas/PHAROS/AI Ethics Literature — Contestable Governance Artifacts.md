# AI Ethics Literature — Contestable Governance Artifacts

## Summary

Literature-support note arguing that contemporary AI ethics has moved beyond principle formulation toward the harder problem of preventing fluent outputs, safety claims, organizational assurances, and technical documentation from functioning as self-validating evidence. This supports [[PHAROS AI Ethics Submission — Springer Draft]], [[AI Governance Manifesto — Upstream Institutional Practice]], and [[Emotional Alliance vs. Evidentiary Discipline in AI]].

## Governing Claim

Responsible AI governance should treat model self-description, safety claims, alignment summaries, and organizational documentation as **contestable governance artifacts**, not transparent windows into system capacity, system intent, or institutional compliance.

The methodological implication: trustworthy oversight depends less on whether AI systems or organizations can articulate attractive ethical accounts of themselves, and more on whether those accounts survive adversarial testing, institutional accountability, and materially grounded lifecycle evaluation.

## Literature Logic

| Literature cluster | What it supports | Representative references in source capture |
|---|---|---|
| Principle convergence and operationalization gap | Fairness, accountability, transparency, privacy, beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice recur across AI ethics, but principles weaken under abstraction, incentives, and weak enforcement | Jobin et al.; Floridi et al.; Hagendorff; Mittelstadt; Whittlestone et al.; Greene et al.; Metcalf et al. |
| Accountability, transparency, and audit limits | Transparency and documentation are necessary but insufficient unless tied to contestability, review, and institutional responsibility | Ananny & Crawford; Kroll et al.; Wieringa; Raji et al.; Mökander & Floridi; Mökander et al. |
| Dataset/model documentation | Dataset and model lineage make claims inspectable and reduce laundering of outputs as evidence | Gebru et al.; Mitchell et al.; Sambasivan et al. |
| Foundation model and LLM risks | Persuasive first-person or explanatory language can create false impressions of epistemic access, moral reliability, and compliance | Bender et al.; Bommasani et al.; Weidinger et al.; Birhane et al. |
| Sociotechnical harms and lifecycle evaluation | Harms arise across data, design, deployment, institutional context, and feedback loops, not only at model output | Selbst et al.; Shelby et al.; Suresh & Guttag; Barocas & Selbst; Morley et al. |

## Connection to PHAROS

This note gives literature backing to the PHAROS rule that fluent, decisive, eloquent, or ethically attractive outputs must not be treated as evidence of governance adequacy. It maps directly onto the [[PHAROS AI Ethics Submission — Springer Draft]] exclusion of stylistic/lexical noise as inadmissible evidence and the [[Recursive Deterministic AI Governance — Method and Paper]] insistence on admissibility boundaries, evidence hierarchy, and failure harvesting.

It also strengthens [[AI Governance Manifesto — Upstream Institutional Practice]] by grounding the manifesto's anti-theater claim in AI ethics literature: governance must produce contestable evidence, not reassurance.

## Key Ideas

- Principle-level consensus is not the same as operational governance.
- Self-description by a model or institution is a claim to be tested, not a fact to be accepted.
- Documentation, model cards, datasheets, audits, and impact assessments matter because they make claims inspectable and contestable.
- Ethics-washing occurs when attractive ethical language substitutes for enforceable mechanisms, accountability, or lifecycle evidence.
- The strongest AI ethics implication is methodological: governance must test whether ethical accounts survive pressure.
