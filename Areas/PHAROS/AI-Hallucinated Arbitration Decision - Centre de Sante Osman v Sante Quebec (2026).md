# AI-Hallucinated Arbitration Decision - Centre de Sante Osman v Sante Quebec (2026)

## Summary

A Quebec Superior Court ruling by Justice Martin F. Sheehan reportedly set aside an August 2025 arbitration decision by Michel A. Jeanniot because the arbitration reasons relied on non-existent legal authorities. The dispute involved Centre de Sante Osman, a health authority that later became part of Sante Quebec, and a contested payment of roughly $1.2 million to $1.225 million. The clinic argued that the arbitration decision rested on fabricated doctrines and jurisprudence, likely generated by an AI tool.

The governance point is not that legal professionals may never use AI. The failure is that a decision-maker appears to have allowed unverified generated authorities to enter the legal reasoning chain as if they were valid law. That converted a support tool into an unacknowledged source of authority and undermined the affected party's ability to answer the case against it.

## Why This Matters

This is a high-value [[AI Governance Failure Cases|AI governance failure case]] because it moves the familiar hallucinated-citation problem from lawyer submissions into adjudicative reasoning. The harm is procedural as much as epistemic:

- fabricated sources made the legal basis of the decision unstable;
- the clinic could not meaningfully contest authorities that did not exist;
- the arbitrator's personal decision-making duty was treated as delegable;
- the arbitration system's legitimacy was damaged because the reasons looked formal while losing contact with law.

In PHAROS terms, the case is a clean example of [[Emotional Alliance vs. Evidentiary Discipline in AI|evidentiary discipline]] failing at the authority boundary. Fluency, legal formatting, and apparent citations cannot substitute for source verification.

## Governance Reading

### 1. AI use is not the forbidden act

The reports say Justice Sheehan accepted that reliable AI tools may be used for tasks such as document identification, summarization, trend analysis, transcription, and writing refinement. The line is crossed when a decision-maker or party relies on AI output without rigorous verification and lets it carry legal authority.

### 2. Delegation is the core defect

The legal concern is not merely "wrong citations." It is delegation of decision-making power. An adjudicator must perform the reasoning process personally because the discipline of writing reasons is part of the decision. If AI output supplies invented authorities, and those authorities drive the result, the human has not merely used a tool; they have allowed the tool to occupy the judgment function.

### 3. Hallucinated law destroys contestability

This case belongs in [[Legal and Institutional Cases]] because the losing party's right to be heard depends on answerable reasons. A party can distinguish, challenge, or appeal real authorities. It cannot meaningfully respond to fabricated precedents masquerading as jurisprudence.

### 4. Verification is an institutional duty

The case reinforces the Canadian Judicial Council principle that courts must not delegate decision-making to AI and the Quebec Superior Court warning that AI-generated legal material requires rigorous human control. For PHAROS, that means any AI-assisted legal or governance workflow must keep the following controls visible:

- source retrieval before citation;
- citation validation against primary legal databases;
- explicit AI-use boundaries;
- human ownership of the final reasoning;
- adversarial review of authorities before promotion into a decision.

## Use In Future Work

Use this case when arguing that AI governance is not only about model accuracy. It is about preserving the chain of authority between source, reasoning, decision-maker, and affected party. The case is especially useful for PHAROS legal/compliance materials because it shows that "AI-assisted" becomes institutionally dangerous when verification is treated as optional and when the human role is reduced to accepting fluent output.
