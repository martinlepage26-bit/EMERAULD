# Neo-Shamanism and Gender — Montreal Pagan Community

## Summary

Academic paper by [[Martin Lepage — Professional Profile|Martin Lepage]] examining how LGBTQ-identifying individuals in the Montreal Pagan community engage with neo-shamanic practices in relation to their gender and sexual identities. Multiple versions across 2003–2013. Part of [[Research and Papers MOC]] and [[Historical Academic Portfolio — Pre-PHAROS Scholarly Work]].

## Context

This is one of the most multiply-versioned papers in the archive: 2003, 2010 (two variants), 2013 (three variants). **Publication status: unpublished manuscript — valid research, not abandoned.** The *Lithuanian Journal of Anthropology* (2019) attribution appearing in the [[Sealed Card Protocol — Legitimacy, Glitch, and Charging]] document came from a grant proposal bibliography that listed planned publications; the grant was not awarded and the paper was never submitted to that venue. All versions (2003, 2010, 2013) are drafts. The research is substantive and the manuscript is a potential submission candidate. The paper is one of [[Martin Lepage — Professional Profile|Martin Lepage]]'s most sustained engagements with Indigenous critique and the problem of cultural appropriation in neo-shamanic practices. Part of the queer Pagan corpus that extends the 2010 mythocritique substrate (see [[Mythocritique to PHAROS — The 2010 Master's Thesis as Methodological Keystone]]); indexed in [[Queer Sociology of Magic and Ritual]].

## Details

**Core question:** How do LGBTQ individuals in the Montreal Pagan community use neo-shamanic practices in relation to their gender and sexual identity? What tensions arise between personal agency, binary gender frameworks in Wicca, and ethical concerns about appropriation of Indigenous spiritualities?

**Method:** Life narratives and ethnographic fieldwork. Two case studies illustrate how queer Pagans create meaningful spiritual practices while remaining critically aware of broader power dynamics.

**Key term — "queer negotiations":** The strategies LGBTQ individuals employ to reconcile their gender and sexual identities with religious practices that are, at times, embedded within binary frameworks or influenced by appropriated spiritual forms.

**The appropriation problem named explicitly:** Pagans in urban Montreal live in "extremely different cultural settings than Siberian shamans." What they practice is best described as **neo-shamanism** (Rabinovitch and Lewis; Davy; Azria and Hervieu-Léger) — a good number are attracted by traditional shamanic practices but "a highly small number are aware that what they are doing is highly disconnected from Indigenous, Aboriginal or traditional shamanism."

**Finding — differentiated strategies:** Rather than a singular model of queer neo-shamanic religiosity, the study highlights the differentiated strategies individuals employ. The two case studies show how gender and queerness impact the relationship Pagans have with their beliefs, shamanic practices, and community belonging.

**Keywords:** Contemporary Paganism; Gender; Sexual identities; Neo-shamanism; Cultural appropriation; Intersectionality; Life narratives.

## Key Ideas

- Queer negotiations: the term developed in this paper carries forward through the entire portfolio
- The cultural appropriation problem as internal: queer Pagans are both subjects of marginalization and participants in appropriative practices — the paper holds this tension without resolving it
- Life narrative method: the turn to individual life trajectories rather than abstract community description
- Neo-shamanism vs. shamanism: the distinction is epistemic and ethical, not just descriptive

## Insights

- "Queer negotiations" is one of [[Martin Lepage — Professional Profile|Martin Lepage]]'s most durable theoretical contributions — the term appears across the full portfolio from 2003 to the [[Social Compass Paper — Ritual Authority and Régime de Preuve|2025 Social Compass submission]]
- The appropriation critique built into this early paper is what distinguishes it from more naive approaches to neo-Paganism — it takes seriously the power asymmetries that queer liberation politics cannot simply bypass
