# Crossing Under Watch — Troll's Toll and Digital Ritual Passage

See also [[Queering Neo-Pagan Magic — 2026 Paper]].
See also [[Spider-Man and the Phonetic Glitch — Voice as Infrastructure]].
## Summary
Academic paper using the **troll** as an analytic figure to theorize institutional toll-taking in digital, scholarly, and ritual contexts. Author: Martin Lepage, PhD (2019, self-cited as drawing on Lepage 2016 and 2017). The troll functions simultaneously as: folkloric guardian of thresholds, digital antagonist, algorithmic gatekeeper, and institutional obstacle. Three toll types — temporal, semantic, and formal — describe the mechanisms by which crossing is made costly, delayed, or impossible. Four case studies: Beltane latency, Samhain caption drift, peer review as toll booth, and live captioning as toll-passage governance. Related to [[Sealed Card Protocol — Legitimacy, Glitch, and Charging]], [[Fluency, Interruption, and Institutional Accountability]], and [[Historical Academic Portfolio — Pre-PHAROS Scholarly Work]].

## Context
Source: `2019 - Crossing Under Watch - Troll's Toll.docx`. The 2019 date is consistent with the self-citations to Lepage 2016 and 2017 publications. This paper operates at the intersection of the ritual studies and digital governance work — using a folkloric figure (the troll) to analyze institutional mechanisms that would otherwise require more abstract theoretical vocabulary. The troll's advantage as an analytic figure is its explicit acknowledgment that crossing has a cost and that cost is enforced by a party with an interest in the toll.

---

## The Troll as Analytic Figure

The paper develops the troll across four registers simultaneously:

**1. Folkloric troll**: Guardian of bridges, thresholds, and crossings. Demands payment (gold, riddles, the right answer) before permitting passage. The toll is not random — it is the price of crossing the threshold the troll controls. The troll does not own the territory on either side; it owns the crossing.

**2. Digital troll**: The internet antagonist who disrupts, derails, and demands emotional labor from those targeted. The digital troll's toll is not monetary — it is time, attention, emotional stability, and willingness to continue. The toll is paid or passage is abandoned.

**3. Algorithmic troll**: Automated gatekeeping systems (content moderation, access controls, verification requirements) that impose costs on crossing without visible human decision-making. The algorithmic troll extracts the toll without being named as doing so — its operation is fluent (in the [[Fluency, Interruption, and Institutional Accountability]] sense) and therefore appears as infrastructure rather than as extraction.

**4. Institutional troll**: Bureaucratic gatekeeping — the form that must be completed, the credential that must be demonstrated, the wait that must be endured. Institutional tolls are normalized as procedure, making their extractive function invisible.

---

## Three Toll Types

### Temporal Toll
The cost paid in time:
- Delays imposed by processing requirements, review periods, or bureaucratic sequencing
- Waiting as a form of attrition — those with fewer resources to sustain the wait pay disproportionately
- The temporal toll is racially, class, and disability-coded: those with more resources can pay the time cost; those without cannot

### Semantic Toll
The cost paid in meaning-compliance:
- The requirement to express a claim in the vocabulary, format, and register of the gatekeeper's proof regime
- A claim that is semantically correct but formally incompatible with the gatekeeper's vocabulary must be translated — and translation extracts meaning
- Semantic tolls are particularly burdensome for marginalized knowledge claims: Indigenous knowledge, experiential evidence, oral tradition — all must be translated into institutional semantic registers to cross institutional thresholds

### Formal Toll
The cost paid in procedural compliance:
- Meeting formal requirements (citations, formatting, credentials, institutional affiliation) regardless of their bearing on content quality
- Formal tolls systematically advantage those trained within institutional formal systems
- The peer review toll booth (see below) is primarily a formal toll: the paper must perform the ritual of academic citation and argumentation regardless of whether those rituals add epistemic value

## Four Case Studies

### Beltane Latency
A ritual event analysis: a Beltane ceremony (spring ritual marking the transition between winter and summer) that required digital coordination. The temporal toll is extracted by platform latency — the time between action and acknowledgment on digital platforms disrupts the ritual's real-time requirement.

**Finding**: Digital platforms impose temporal tolls on ritual practices that require synchrony. The toll is not charged by a visible gatekeeper — it is embedded in the infrastructure. Platform latency is a governance mechanism.

### Samhain Caption Drift
A live event with digital captioning: a Samhain ceremony (autumn ritual marking the transition between summer and winter) where live captioning technology produced errors, delays, and semantic drift.

**Finding**: The semantic toll of live captioning imposes a translation cost on ritual language — specialized vocabulary, non-standard syntax, emotional register — that degrades the content being captioned. The toll is paid by the least-served community members (Deaf and hard-of-hearing participants) who depend on the captioning to access the ritual.

### Peer Review as Toll Booth
Academic peer review analyzed as institutional troll infrastructure:
- Reviewers extract semantic tolls (demands to use their preferred vocabulary)
- Temporal tolls (months-long review periods that disproportionately affect early-career researchers)
- Formal tolls (citation requirements, structural conventions, journal-specific formatting)
- The anonymous reviewer is a troll without a face — the toll is extracted without accountability

**Finding**: Peer review's stated function (quality gatekeeping) is partially enacted and partially replaced by toll-extraction that advances institutional interests (slow competitors, enforce citation of reviewers' work, maintain disciplinary vocabulary policing).

### Live Captioning as Toll-Passage Governance
Extended analysis of live captioning as a governance system:
- Captioning is an accessibility accommodation — its formal purpose is to remove a barrier
- In practice, it imposes its own tolls (latency, error, semantic translation, speaker burden of "caption-friendly" speech)
- The toll analysis reveals that accessibility accommodations can themselves be toll booths: the accommodation is provided, the barrier is technically addressed, but the crossing is still costly

**Finding**: Access provisions that impose their own tolls are not neutral accommodations — they are governance mechanisms that provide formal compliance with accessibility requirements while preserving substantive exclusion. This is the [[Governance by Denial]] capacity lever at the accommodation level.

## Connection to Scholarly Passage

The paper applies the troll analysis to academic career progression:
- Each stage of academic passage (PhD defense, article submission, tenure review, promotion) is a toll booth with defined toll types
- The troll at each stage is institutionally authorized to extract the toll and has structural interests in doing so (maintaining discipline standards, protecting citation economies, preserving institutional prestige)
- First-generation academics, disabled academics, and academics working in non-dominant languages pay compound tolls — temporal, semantic, and formal costs stack

## Insights

- The troll figure's greatest analytic advantage is its acknowledgment of economic interest: the troll has a stake in the toll. Infrastructure models of gatekeeping (platforms, algorithms, bureaucracy) naturalize the extraction by making the gatekeeper invisible. The troll makes visible that there is always a party that benefits from making crossing difficult
- The compound toll analysis (multiple toll types stacking) is the most socially specific contribution: it explains why formal accessibility provisions (captioning, extended deadlines, accommodation letters) often fail to remove the cost of crossing — they address one toll type while leaving others intact
- The peer review toll booth analysis connects to the [[Refusing Fixity — Aesthetic Governance and Fugitive Authorship]] peer review experiment: the governance failure of academic evaluation is not incidental — it is structurally produced by the peer review mechanism's toll-extraction function
