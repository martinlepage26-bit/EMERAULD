# Inductive Literary Discourse Analysis — Witchcraft in Song Lyrics

See also [[Pagan and Queer Ritual Studies MOC]].
## Summary
An inductive textual analysis of 20+ songs (1942–2025) from multiple genres and languages centering on witchcraft, magic, and supernatural transformation. Four emergent thematic categories are identified from the corpus through iterative open coding. Connects to [[Queering Neo-Pagan Magic — 2006 Paper]], [[Sociology of Modern Magic — Song Corpus and Queer Ritual Synthesis]], and [[Social Compass Paper — Ritual Authority and Régime de Preuve]].

## Context
Source: `raw sources/Inductive Literary Discourse Analys.txt`. Standalone inductive discourse analysis document. Methodology is qualitative textual analysis using constant comparison. Covers jazz, blues, folk, rock, pop, electronica, and rap in English, Italian, German, French, Ladin, and Portuguese. Forms the empirical base of the [[Sociology of Modern Magic — Song Corpus and Queer Ritual Synthesis]] essay. The "inductive" mythocritical-irradiation procedure is operationalized via `trace-investigator` per [[Mythocritique to PHAROS — The 2010 Master's Thesis as Methodological Keystone|the keystone]]; the methodology is the same Brunelian *archétypocritique* described at MA p. 34. Indexed in [[Media Studies and Pop Culture Analysis]] and [[Queer Sociology of Magic and Ritual]].

## Details

### Methodology
Inductive: no preconceived categories imposed. Each song coded line by line for recurring motifs, figurative language, and grammatical structures. Constant comparison method. Treats canonical and obscure works equally.

### Four Emergent Thematic Categories

**1. Enchantment as Desire and Submission**
Magic as metaphor for overwhelming attraction, loss of control, or erotic binding — usually speaker toward enchanting other.
- Sinatra, "Witchcraft" (1957) — tactile imagery, surrender framed as external force
- Santana, "Black Magic Woman" (1970) — danger and seduction combined
- Screamin' Jay Hawkins, "I Put a Spell on You" (1956) — spell as curse and surrender simultaneously
- Lady Gaga / Sugar Ray, "Abracadabra" (1999/2025) — desire as incantation

**2. The Witch as Agency and Fear**
The witch figure as self-possession, mystery, or social transgression — revered and persecuted.
- Eagles, "Witchy Woman" (1972) — magnetic, persecuted, edge-geography
- Fleetwood Mac, "Rhiannon" (1975) — sovereign self-governance
- Schelmish, "Hexenlied" (2008) — church as oppressor, victim as witch
- Elizabeth Winterbourne, "Salem" (2025) — direct historical reclamation

**3. Ritual Language and Incantation**
Lyrics employ ritual prosody — repetition, imperative verbs, enumeration — to produce an alternate linguistic order where utterance equals action.
- Dave & Toni Arthur, "Hearken to the Witches' Rune" (1970) — directional invocation
- Goethe/musicalized, "Der Zauberlehrling" (1797) — incantation as uncontrollable process
- FM GVCC¥, "Liber Vel Aba – Gevurah" (2024) — Chaos magik as flow
- Anderson-Lopez & Lopez, "The Ballad of the Witches' Road" (2024) — coven invocation, elemental enumeration, chant-as-spell; see [[The Ballad of the Witches' Road — Lyrics]] and [[The Ballad of the Witches' Road — Analysis]]
Phonetic rhythm (Abrakadabra, Hix-Hex, Walle-Walle) binds meaning to sound: magical performativity, not narrative description.

**4. Ambivalence Between Sacred and Profane Power**
Magic oscillates between moral danger and creative enlightenment; the lyrical speaker negotiates fascination and fear.
- Sinatra/Garland, "That Old Black Magic" (1942) — foundational ambivalence
Cross-genre pattern: enchantment desired and feared simultaneously.

## Key Ideas
- Witchcraft lyrics are not about the occult; they are about the social management of transgression
- Mid-century men's songs externalize desire through magic to maintain masculine coherence
- Ritual prosody (repetition, imperatives) performs rather than describes — closest to speech act theory
- The corpus spans eight decades and maintains structural consistency across genres and languages

## Insights
- The textual analysis provides the evidence base that the [[Sociology of Modern Magic — Song Corpus and Queer Ritual Synthesis]] essay theorizes
- The ritual performativity category anticipates the argument in [[Agatha All Along — Wicca, Digital Mediatization, and Proof Regimes]] about witchcraft as a proof regime
- "Legitimacy at the margins" runs through all four categories — who controls the label "witch" determines whether the figure is threat or commodity

## Open Questions
- How does the 2024/2025 set (Lady Gaga, Salem, FM GVCC¥) differ structurally from 1970s texts?
- Does the multilingual corpus reveal cross-cultural patterns or mainly confirms that genre (rock, pop) homogenizes the themes?
