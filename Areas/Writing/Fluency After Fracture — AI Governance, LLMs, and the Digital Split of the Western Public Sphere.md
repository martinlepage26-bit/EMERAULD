# Fluency After Fracture

## AI Governance, Large Language Models, and the Digital Split of the Western Public Sphere

**Martin Lepage**  
Complete working draft v1.0 — 2026-08-07  
*Not yet submitted. Controlled quotations are drawn only from sources opened for this manuscript (see Citation Integrity Note).*

**Target venue:** *AI & Society* (conceptual / critical AI governance essay; distinct from the author’s RECURSO / RDAIG method track)

---

## Abstract

The “digital fracture” of the Western world is usually narrated as polarization, platform capture, or information disorder. This paper argues for a more precise diagnosis. The fracture is a **governance failure of fluency**: large language models and adjacent generative systems accelerate the conversion of smooth, complete, and persuasive speech into institutional and cultural legitimacy, while eroding the interruption points through which authority remains contestable. Drawing on the fluency/interruption framework, on deferred-authority analysis across archives and AI memory, and on disability epistemology (compulsory fluency as an institutional sorting mechanism), the paper reframes LLM-mediated public life as a regime in which **authority is laundered through smoothness**. The result is not only epistemic noise. It is a structural split between synthetic consensus (fluent, scalable, difficult to interrupt) and revisable public reason (slow, awkward, evidence-bound). The contribution is threefold: (1) descriptive, mapping the fracture as a fluency regime rather than a culture-war surface; (2) analytic, specifying the mechanisms of gap-closing, authority laundering, deferred proof, and compulsory fluency; (3) field-level, treating interruption design as a problem of public reason under generative systems, not only a product-ethics checklist.

**Keywords:** AI governance; large language models; fluency; interruption; deferred authority; disability epistemology; public sphere; digital fracture

## 1. Introduction

Something has broken in the Western digital public sphere, and the usual names for the break no longer hold. Polarization explains enemy formation but not why fluent falsehood and fluent half-truth travel as if they were competence. Misinformation research explains error rates but not why **smoothness itself** becomes a proxy for legitimacy. Platform critique explains capture and advertising incentives but not why generative systems deepen a fracture even when content policies improve at the margins.

The break is better named a **fluency fracture**. Large language models do not invent the Western preference for continuous, polished, gapless speech. They industrialize it. They close unfinished sentences, complete unfinished thoughts, and supply institutional prose at a speed that makes interruption feel like sabotage rather than responsibility. In doing so, they intensify a pattern already analyzed in algorithmic workplace and recruitment systems: **fluency naturalizes authority; interruption restores contestability** (Lepage, *Fluency, Interruption, and Institutional Accountability*; Lepage, *From Hegemonic Fluency to the Ethics of Interruption*).

What is new is scale and intimacy. The workplace algorithm scored candidates. The LLM scores the texture of ordinary thought. It sits in the drafting of memos, briefs, student essays, campaign posts, legal letters, and intimate self-narration. The same architecture that can assist careful work can also **launder authority**: a claim that would fail under slow evidentiary discipline succeeds because it sounds finished.

This matters for AI governance. Much of the field still organizes around safety, bias, alignment, and compliance. Those concerns are necessary. They are not sufficient. A system can be “safe” in a narrow product sense while still devastating the conditions of public reason. The fracture is not only that models hallucinate. The fracture is that **institutions and publics increasingly lack designed pauses** at which fluent output must become accountable output.

### Governing claim

The digital fracture of the Western public sphere is not primarily a failure of content moderation or media literacy. It is a **failure of interruption under conditions of machine fluency**. Large language models intensify a long-standing institutional pattern: they naturalize authority by making speech continuous, complete, and competent-sounding, while hiding the conditions under which claims are produced, verified, or refused. Until AI governance installs interruption as a first-order design principle (at model, workflow, and institutional layers), the fracture will widen between fluent synthetic consensus and contestable public reason.

### Contribution

| Level | Claim |
|-------|--------|
| **Descriptive** | Shows how LLM fluency, deferred authority, and compulsory fluency co-produce a split public sphere. |
| **Analytic** | Explains the mechanism: smoothness launders authority; interruption restores contestability; archives and memory harden fluent claims into infrastructure. |
| **Field-level** | Moves AI governance from safety and compliance checklists toward a theory of public reason under generative systems. |

### Scope

The object is generative LLMs as institutional and cultural actors in Western public life (roughly 2018–2026), not all AI. The site is the public sphere plus institutional workflows (administration, hiring, media, research), not a single platform case study. The method is conceptual analysis, mechanism tracing, and autoethnographic disability epistemology, not statistical causal inference. “Western” names a political-media formation of North Atlantic liberal democracies and their cultural spillover, not a civilizational essence or exclusive crisis.

### Path of the argument

Section 2 positions the paper in public-sphere theory, critical algorithm studies, and related debates. Section 3 defines fluency, interruption, deferred authority, and compulsory fluency. Section 8 notes limits; Section 9 concludes.

## 2. Positioning

This paper is not a full empirical study of one model release or election cycle; not a replacement for the RECURSO or RDAIG method track; not a claim that Western societies uniquely suffer digital pathology; and not a rejection of generative tools. It is a theoretical-political essay with governance consequences: a mechanism paper that tries to name the fracture precisely enough that design and institutional response become possible.

### 2.1 Public sphere and democratic legitimacy

The Habermasian tradition still supplies the strongest vocabulary for legitimacy as something other than force or market success. *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (Habermas 1989; German 1962) remains the historical-sociological baseline for the public sphere as a category of bourgeois society and as a problem of mediated public opinion. In *Between Facts and Norms*, Habermas (1996) ties legal-political legitimacy to communicative processes in which those subject to norms can participate in their formation. *Legitimation Crisis* (Habermas 1975) already frames mass loyalty under systems that struggle to reconcile economic imperatives with normative justification. Together these texts license the question this paper sharpens under LLM conditions: what happens to legitimacy when the speech that fills the public and administrative sphere is machine-fluent, continuous, and only weakly answerable?

Michael Warner’s *Publics and Counterpublics* (2002) sharpens the object further. Warner distinguishes *the* public as social totality from *a* public that forms through discourse:

> “The public is a kind of social totality. Its most common sense is that of the people in general. … whenever one is addressed as *the* public, the others are assumed not to matter.” (Warner 2002, 49; *Public Culture* abridgment)

> “A public is a space of discourse organized by nothing other than discourse itself. … It exists by virtue of being addressed.” (Warner 2002, 50)

> “A public is a relation among strangers. … A public, however, unites strangers through participation alone, at least in theory.” (Warner 2002, 55)

Generative fluency threatens not only “debate quality” but the conditions under which a self-organized public of strangers can form, attend, and answer back. When speech is machine-completed at scale, address and circulation accelerate without a matching increase in answerability.

What this tradition underweights (especially in pre-generative formulations) is machine fluency as a *mode of authority*, not only as a medium that carries opinions. The issue is not only who speaks in public, but which speech-forms are treated as finished enough to count as public reason.

### 2.2 AI ethics, alignment, and the transparency trap

AI ethics and alignment correctly center harm, safety, and value loading. What they often leave secondary is institutional interruption design beyond model weights: the workflow and public-law pauses where fluent output becomes actionable power. A “safe” model can still devastate proof regimes if organizations treat completion as competence.

Ananny and Crawford (2018) supply a needed brake on the transparency ideal. In “Seeing without knowing,” they argue that looking inside a system (code or data) does not guarantee knowing how it works or holding it accountable; visibility is not governability (*New Media & Society* 20(3): 973–989). That argument supports this paper’s refusal to treat more fluent model cards, more fluent ethics statements, or more disclosure theater as substitutes for interruption rights with capacity. Kroll et al. (2017) similarly push accountability beyond inspection of code toward systems that remain answerable in law and procedure (*University of Pennsylvania Law Review* 165: 633–705). The present paper does not re-litigate technical accountability mechanisms; it asks what public-reason failure looks like when fluency outruns those mechanisms.

### 2.3 Misinformation is necessary but incomplete

Misinformation research tracks false content and virality. Necessary, incomplete. Fluency can launder legitimacy even when content is not straightforwardly false: hedges disappear, uncertainty attenuates, and institutional tone supplies authority independent of truth value. This paper therefore treats **smoothness as a legitimacy proxy** rather than equating the fracture with a pure “fake news” problem.

### 2.4 Critical algorithm studies: classification, then completion

Critical algorithm studies have shown how classification, scoring, labor extraction, and predictive sorting reorganize life chances. Noble (2018) demonstrates that search and ranking systems reproduce racism and sexism rather than neutrally mirroring the web. Benjamin (2019) names the “New Jim Code”: technologies that encode and amplify racial hierarchy while appearing neutral or benevolent. Eubanks (2018) documents automated decision systems in U.S. public services as a “digital poorhouse.” Crawford (2021) maps AI as extractive material infrastructure (minerals, labor, data, energy), not floating “cloud intelligence.”

Barocas and Selbst (2016) show how discriminatory outcomes emerge from the data-mining pipeline rather than from explicit intent alone:

> “Worse still, because the resulting discrimination is almost always an unintentional emergent property of the algorithm’s use rather than a conscious choice by its programmers, it can be unusually hard to identify the source of the problem or to explain it to a court.” (Barocas and Selbst 2016, 671)

They walk the pipeline (target variable, training data, feature selection, proxies) and show how Title VII can struggle to govern correlations that look like “business necessity” even when they encode historic prejudice (Barocas and Selbst 2016, 671–677).

These works authorize the starting point: technical systems carry social power. **The specific delta of this paper is generative completion.** Classification sorts. Completion *finishes*. Authority laundering through finished prose (memo-ready, brief-ready, post-ready) is the mechanism tracked under LLM conditions. That is an extension of the critical line into a fluency and public-reason problem, not a claim these authors already made about large language models.

Nissenbaum (2004) adds a governance lens on information flows. Privacy violations are breaches of contextual informational norms, not only secrecy failures:

> “I posit two types of informational norms: norms of appropriateness, and norms of flow or distribution. Contextual integrity is maintained when both types of norms are upheld, and it is violated when either of the norms is violated. The central thesis of this Article is that the benchmark of privacy is contextual integrity…” (Nissenbaum 2004, 128)

> “A central tenet of contextual integrity is that there are no arenas of life not governed by norms of information flow, no information or spheres of life for which ‘anything goes.’” (Nissenbaum 2004, 128)

Under generative systems, the relevant flow is not only data transfer but **claim transfer**: incomplete human uncertainty becomes complete institutional speech that then travels across contexts (chat → brief → archive → precedent) without a matching norm for that migration.

### 2.5 Compulsion, design, and the machine zone

Schüll (2012) analyzes how gambling-machine design engineers a “machine zone” of absorbed, continuous play. Compulsion is socio-technical, not only individual:

> Mollie: “The thing people never understand is that I’m not playing to win.” / “To keep playing—to stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters.” / “You aren’t really there—you’re with the machine and that’s all you’re with.” (Schüll 2012, 2)

Industry discourse matches the desire: “time-on-device,” or TOD; designers celebrate products on which players “can really get into that zone” (Schüll 2012, 2–3). Machine gambling is “solitary, continuous, and rapid”; a game can complete every few seconds (Schüll 2012, 17–18). A consultant states the design goal without romance: “The key is duration of play… I want to keep you there as long as humanly possible” (Schüll 2012, 58–59).

This paper does not equate LLMs with slot machines. It borrows the design insight: **systems can be optimized for continuous engagement and gapless flow in ways that make interruption feel like failure.** Paired with the author’s gap-closer diagnostic for generative assistants (Lepage, *The Compulsion to Complete*), Schüll supports treating fluency as engineered tempo, not as natural intelligence.

### 2.6 Phantom publics and clusters

Petersmann and Van Den Meerssche (2024) argue that algorithmic governmentality increasingly acts on **clusters** rather than on stable liberal subjects who can recognize themselves as a public:

> “we qualify these emergent algorithmic categories as ‘clusters’—pulsing patterns distilled from disaggregated data. As fluid, modular, and ever-emergent forms of association, these ‘clusters’ defy stable expressions of collective representation and social recognition.” (Petersmann and Van Den Meerssche 2024, 107)

> “at the ‘virtual border’, the human subject is splintered into a ‘cluster’ of pulsing patterns distilled from disaggregated data.” (2024, 108)

> “the subject that is being enacted is a cluster of inferred attributes: a relational association between dispersed data points…” (2024, 111)

They name three traits: the cluster is fluid and relational; emergent and composite; inferential and correlated (2024, 111–112). Governance can produce affected populations **before** mutual recognition or political appearance becomes possible. Fluency intensifies that problem. Clusters and synthetic consensus can operate as fluent governance objects (already decisive) while contestation is still assembling a recognizable speaker. Their caution against treating transparency and inclusion as complete solutions aligns with Ananny and Crawford: more visibility can mean more capture. Interruption, as used here, is not total transparency. It is **structured reopening** of action before consequences harden.

### 2.7 Authorized language and performative institutional speech

Bourdieu’s sociology of language treats linguistic exchanges as exchanges of symbolic power: the value of an utterance depends on the speaker’s authority and position, not only on grammar (Bourdieu 1982). Austin (1962) supplies the speech-act frame for institutional utterances that *do* things rather than only describe them. Together they underwrite a claim central to fluency analysis: **authoritative-sounding form can substitute for earned legitimacy.** LLM output is a mass production technology for that substitution risk.

### 2.8 Disability studies as method hinge

Disability studies and crip theory supply compulsory able-bodiedness, access politics, and the critique of cure. This paper treats fluency itself as a **governance technology** that runs from speech-therapy norms through hiring algorithms to LLM product design. The autoethnographic anchors are the author’s *Beyond Fluency* program and *AI Recruiting Has an Accessibility Problem* (Lepage 2026). Full development follows in Section 6.

## 3. Conceptual frame

### 3.1 Fluency

Fluency, in this paper, is not eloquence. It is a **governance effect**: the cluster of features through which computational and institutional speech appears competent, neutral, continuous, and routine.

Six dimensions:

1. **Interactional smoothness** — friction and uncertainty are minimized.  
2. **Authority cues** — formatting, tone, and completeness signal legitimacy.  
3. **Procedural compression** — intermediate steps disappear.  
4. **Labor concealment** — human maintenance work is hidden.  
5. **Uncertainty attenuation** — provisional results look definitive.  
6. **Gap-closing compulsion** — the system completes what is unfinished, converting silence, hesitation, and incomplete evidence into continuous prose.

Fluency is therefore a mode of **authority exercise**. It produces consequences while making the authority producing those consequences hard to locate, slow to contest, and easy to naturalize.

### 3.2 Interruption

Interruption is not crash, rage, or random delay. It is a **structured reopening** of a chain of action to explanation, challenge, or revision before consequences harden.

Examples include contestation windows before irreversible decisions; forced disclosure of uncertainty and provenance; escalation to human reviewers with real veto power; evidentiary stops that refuse completion when evidence is incomplete; and disability-informed design that treats non-fluent speech as legitimate participation.

The governance claim is simple and hard: **fluency without interruption produces authority without accountability**. Interruption without any fluency produces accountability without workable institutions. Design is the art of *where* to interrupt.

### 3.3 Deferred authority

Deferred authority names the process by which contingent labels, outputs, and traces become infrastructure through repetition. In archives, a colonial name hardens into a finding aid. In ritual and media, a proof regime decides what counts as evidence and then forgets that it decided. In AI systems, prior outputs re-enter as context, memory, fine-tuning signal, or “what everyone already knows.”

The mechanism: **repetition does not merely preserve a claim. It makes the claim feel natural, then hides the conditions that produced it.** LLMs accelerate deferred authority because they recycle the textual surface of the past at generative speed. What was once a contested sentence becomes a default continuation.

### 3.4 Compulsory fluency

Compulsory fluency is the institutional demand that bodies, voices, and minds perform smoothness as the price of recognition. Disability epistemology (especially stuttering as lived interruption) shows that this demand is not a side issue of accessibility. It is a **sorting technology**. The classroom, the interview, the conference panel, and the AI-mediated recruiting funnel all teach the same lesson: the right to be heard is rationed through fluent performance. When LLMs become the default drafting layer of institutions, compulsory fluency is no longer only a human demand placed on disabled speakers. It becomes an **infrastructure default**: systems that always complete, always smooth, always sound finished.

## 4. Large language models as fluency engines

### 4.1 Completion as politics

An LLM is, among other things, a **completion machine**. Prompted with an incomplete world, it returns a complete sentence. That property is commercially valuable and cognitively seductive. It is also politically loaded.

| Gap | What completion does | Governance risk |
|-----|----------------------|-----------------|
| Linguistic | finishes the sentence | hides uncertainty as polish |
| Evidentiary | supplies missing connective tissue | invents bridges across thin evidence |
| Institutional | produces ready-to-send prose | accelerates decisions without interruption |

The risk is not that models sometimes invent. The risk is that **invention arrives dressed as institutional competence**.

### 4.2 Synthetic consensus

At public scale, fluent generation produces a new kind of consensus object: text that *feels* like what a reasonable public already believes because it is patterned on aggregated prior speech. This is not democracy. It is **statistical echo with rhetorical authority**.

Synthetic consensus has three properties. It is fast enough to outrun institutional fact-checking cycles. It is smooth enough to pass as neutral summary. It is recursive enough that tomorrow’s training and retrieval environments re-ingest today’s fluent outputs.

The digital fracture begins here: one side of public life still runs on slow proof; another runs on fluent synthesis. They no longer share a tempo, a proof regime, or a shared sense of what counts as “said carefully.”

### 4.3 Authority laundering

Authority laundering is the process by which a claim gains legitimacy by changing clothes: from contested opinion to “the model said,” from provisional draft to formatted memo, from uncertain evidence to confident paragraph.

1. Human uncertainty enters as prompt.  
2. Model fluency returns as complete text.  
3. Institutional formatting marks the text as actionable.  
4. Archive or memory stores the text as precedent.  
5. Later actors cite the precedent as if it had always been solid.

This chain is the public-sphere version of deferred authority. Without interruption points at steps 2–4, the chain is a conveyor belt from doubt to infrastructure. It is also a Nissenbaum problem of claim transfer: norms of appropriateness and flow for chat are not the norms of a legal letter or a policy brief, yet the text migrates as if contexts were interchangeable (Nissenbaum 2004, 128).

## 5. The digital fracture of the Western public sphere

“Digital fracture” here does **not** mean that the West is uniquely doomed, nor that pre-digital publics were healthy. It means a specific late-digital split:

> **A split between fluent synthetic consensus and contestable public reason, intensified by generative AI, platform tempo, and institutions that mistake smoothness for legitimacy.**

### 5.1 Four fracture lines

**Epistemic fracture.** Proof regimes diverge. One regime still asks: what is the source, what is the uncertainty, who can be answered? Another asks: does it sound complete? Under LLM mediation, the second regime gains speed advantages that feel like truth advantages.

**Institutional fracture.** Organizations adopt generative tools to reduce drafting cost. They rarely redesign interruption. Grievance procedures, peer review, legislative deliberation, and clinical second opinions still run on human tempo. Fluent systems run on machine tempo. Accountability becomes structurally late.

**Corporeal fracture.** Compulsory fluency hardens. Disabled, non-native, non-fluent, accented, and hesitant speakers face a double demand: perform human fluency *and* compete with machine fluency. The stutter is not a metaphor only. It is a diagnostic instrument for a society that punishes pause.

**Temporal fracture.** Public reason requires time for evidence, grief, revision, and minority report. Generative systems compress time into continuous output. The fracture is chronological before it is ideological. Parties who cannot afford slow proof are pushed either into silence or into fluent counterfeit.

### 5.2 Working vignettes

These vignettes are analytic sketches that fix the fracture lines in scenes. A later submission version can thicken them with additional documented public cases.

**Epistemic: the finished brief.** A policy office needs a one-page summary before a meeting. The model returns a fluent brief with numbered claims and the look of research. Two of the strongest sentences rest on thin or invented connective tissue. The room treats the document as a decision object because it is complete. Completeness outranked evidence.

**Institutional: the letter that became action.** An administrator drafts a consequential letter with an LLM. Legal review is informal. The letter ships because the prose sounds institutional. When the recipient contests the decision, no one can reconstruct which claims were model-generated, which were human-asserted, and which were never checked. Fluency collapsed the workflow’s interruption points.

**Corporeal: scored speech.** An applicant who stutters enters an AI-mediated recruiting pipeline. Hesitations, restarts, and self-repairs are treated as signal degradation. Accommodation requests produce no escalation path and no legible human review (Lepage 2026). The system evaluates speech production as if it were competence, then denies the interruption that would make that substitution contestable.

**Temporal: the week that cannot wait.** During a high-tempo public controversy, fluent synthetic posts and fluent counter-posts fill the feed. Journalistic verification and minority report writing still require days. By the time careful proof arrives, the fluent frame has already hardened into common sense. Proof arrives after authority has been laundered.

### 5.3 Why “Western” remains in the title

The phrase is retained provisionally for three reasons, all open to critique. First, the institutional examples (liberal-democratic administration, North Atlantic platform cultures, academic and corporate knowledge work) are concentrated there. Second, the author’s professional field (Québec/Canada, Anglophone AI governance discourse) sits inside that formation. Third, “Western” names a **self-image of rational public reason** that is currently failing under its own fluency technologies, not a claim of cultural superiority. Comparative extension (Global South platform governance, multilingual LLM asymmetries, non-Western proof regimes) is future work.

## 6. Disability epistemology as method

The stutter is not decoration for a theory of AI. It is the method through which the theory becomes available.

Disability is often invited into technology writing as moral color after the real argument has already been made. Here the order is reversed. **Compulsory fluency is first encountered as a body-level governance technology.** Only later is it recognized at institutional and machine scale. Without that order, “interruption” collapses into a slogan. With it, interruption names something that has already been lived as cost.

### 6.1 Compulsory fluency as sorting technology

*Beyond Fluency* (Lepage; source draft 2012 with later revision) defines compulsory fluency as an institutional system that treats smooth, rapid, uninterrupted speech and writing as the unmarked norm of intelligence and competence; pathologizes hesitation, rewriting, delay, and dysfluency as disorder or lesser capacity; distributes grades, jobs, and recognition according to fluency compliance; and presents those standards as natural rather than as governance mechanisms.

This is not an aesthetic preference for nice speech. It is a **sorting mechanism**. Four autoethnographic scenes fix it:

| Scene | Institutional demand | What becomes visible |
|-------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Classroom** | Demonstrate comprehension through fluent oral reading | Fluency as assessment technology; intelligence misclassified |
| **Job interview** | Perform continuous professional presence | Evaluation migrates from qualifications to management of discomfort |
| **Academic conference** | Deliver argument as continuous performance | Social contract of form overrides content quality |
| **Writing-with-AI** | Produce compliant text from interrupted input | Assistance can liberate access *or* erase the epistemological position that made the need for help meaningful |

The fourth scene is the hinge into AI governance. When a model converts halting, associative, interrupted thought into formally fluent prose, two outcomes are available. One is access: the speaker reaches audiences that compulsory fluency would have blocked. The other is erasure: the production conditions disappear, and the institution receives a finished object with no trace of uncertainty, repair, or dissent. Liberation that erases its own conditions is not liberation. It is **substitution**.

### 6.2 Interruption as knowledge

From the position of dysfluency, the pause is not empty. It is often the densest moment of thought: monitoring, repair, rerouting. Compulsory fluency pathologizes the *visible sign of thinking*. Governance work requires exactly those skills (error detection, course correction, revision) that fluency regimes devalue when they appear on a body.

Methodological rule: **If a system cannot survive an interrupted human voice, it will not survive interrupted machine output. It will only demand better fluency.**

### 6.3 Against cure-as-governance

Under a medical or therapeutic model, stuttering is deficit; success is approximation to fluent norms. Under a social model, stuttering is difference; the problem is the compulsory fluency regime. AI governance currently defaults to the first model in digital form. “Better models,” “more natural dialogue,” “more helpful completion” are often cure narratives with product branding. Accessibility checklists can become compliance theater: formal rights without capacity, escalation, or redesign of the evaluation criterion itself.

The AI recruiting case makes the theater concrete. Systems that score hesitations and restarts as deficits are not measuring expertise. They are measuring speech production. When applicants raise accessibility concerns and receive no human escalation, the silence is not a glitch. It is a design choice that preserves fluency as proxy while denying interruption as right (Lepage 2026).

### 6.4 Machine limitation

Disability epistemology also clarifies a claim that technical AI discourse often softens: **fluency is not reliability.** A language model operates in language. The gap between model and world is structural, not temporary. When institutional actors treat fluent completion as if it closed that gap, they perform a category error with political consequences. The institution already knew how to punish human interruption. The machine now offers an escape hatch that appears merciful (never stutter, never hesitate, never leave a gap) while intensifying the underlying demand. The body that still pauses becomes more illegible against a background of infinite fluent text.

### 6.5 From personal dysfluency to public design principle

> If institutions already punish the interrupted human voice, then machines that never interrupt themselves will not democratize speech. They will perfect the demand that made interruption a liability.

Disability epistemology therefore does not sit beside AI governance as an ethics add-on. It supplies the diagnostic: **the digital fracture is compulsory fluency at civilizational scale.**

## 7. Governance implications

If the fracture is a fluency regime, then AI governance cannot stop at model safety, bias benchmarks, or post-hoc content labels. Those tools can be necessary. They do not install interruption where authority is laundered.

### 7.1 Three layers

**Model / product layer.** Failure mode: confident completion under uncertainty; persuasive tone without provenance; gap-closing as default. Design responses: uncertainty surfacing that cannot be stripped by polite prompting alone; refusal or degraded modes when evidence, authorization, or corpus bounds are missing; explicit anti-gap-close settings for institutional use; separation between stylistic fluency and epistemic claim status in the interface. The product goal is not uglier text. The goal is text that cannot pretend to be finished when it is not.

**Workflow / organization layer.** Failure mode: fluent draft becomes decision because formatting looks official and review is expensive. Design responses: named stage gates before consequential action; dual control for high-impact outputs; **consequence binding** (a decision must leave an auditable institutional trace); human veto with record, not a symbolic “human in the loop” who rubber-stamps fluent pages; provenance tags for model-assisted passages that survive copy-paste into archives. This is where most organizations currently fail. They buy fluency and keep pre-LLM accountability rituals that assume human tempo and human authorship.

**Public institution layer.** Failure mode: synthetic consensus outruns legislative, journalistic, judicial, and scientific proof. Design responses: slow channels that retain real power; minority report mechanisms with procedural force; archive hygiene with supersession rules so fluent errors do not harden into deferred authority; contest rights paired with **capacity** (time, language access, disability accommodation, counsel); public standards that treat fluency-as-legitimacy as a governance hazard, not a quality metric.

### 7.2 Five minimal principles

1. **No fluency without provenance.**  
2. **No completion without an uncertainty ledger.**  
3. **No institutional action without a named interruption point.**  
4. **No interruption right without capacity to exercise it.**  
5. **No memory without supersession rules.**

These principles align with recursive governance work (corpus admissibility, failure harvesting, deterministic promotion status, consequence binding) without collapsing this paper into a method manual. The method papers specify executable controls. This paper specifies **why those controls are required for public reason** under LLM conditions.

### 7.3 What not to do

Do not treat “AI literacy” as a substitute for institutional redesign. Literacy without interruption rights trains people to cope with fluency regimes rather than contest them. Do not equate more fluent AI ethics statements with governance. Fluent ethics is still fluency. Do not outsource interruption to individual users (“verify everything”). Capacity is uneven; the fracture will map onto existing inequality. Do not romanticize dysfluency as automatic virtue. Interruption is a design object. It can be captured, emptied, or turned into theater.

## 8. Limits

This is a conceptual and mechanism paper. It does not offer a measured causal model of polarization, vote share, or platform engagement. “Western” is a working bound for institutional examples and author positionality, not a completed comparative theory of global digital publics. Disability epistemology here is grounded primarily in stuttering and related voice and fluency experience; other disabilities will reorganize the frame and should not be assimilated without their own method work. Several adjacent literatures (Noble, Benjamin, Eubanks, Crawford, Bourdieu, Austin, Habermas 1975/1996, Ananny and Crawford at page level, and STPS page quotations) remain bibliographically installed but not fully page-mined; claims from those works stay high-level except where noted. Vendor-specific model gossip is intentionally minimized so the argument stays institutional rather than product-journalistic. Personal legal materials are treated as boundary-setting illustrations only where already part of the author’s public research architecture.

---

## 9. Conclusion

The digital fracture will not be healed by better vibes, better content labels, or more fluent AI ethics statements. It will be governed, if at all, by societies that relearn how to **interrupt**.

Large language models are not only tools. They are fluency infrastructures. They close gaps, complete sentences, and make authority sound finished before authority has been earned. In a public culture already trained to treat smoothness as competence, that property does not stay technical. It becomes political. It widens a split between synthetic consensus and revisable public reason; between machine tempo and institutional accountability; between bodies that must pause and systems that never do.

Disability epistemology already priced this confusion. Compulsory fluency was never only about speech therapy. It was about who gets to count as a knower. AI governance now faces the same question at scale: will interruption remain a personal liability, or become a designed condition of legitimate power?

The task is not to abolish fluency. Institutions need workable prose, usable interfaces, and shared language. The task is to stop letting fluency **launder authority**: to install pauses where claims must show their proof, their provenance, their uncertainty, and their human answerability.

A Western public sphere that cannot interrupt its own machines will keep fracturing along the lines this paper has named. One world will speak in continuous synthetic confidence. The other will exhaust itself trying to prove, slowly, that confidence was never the same thing as truth.
