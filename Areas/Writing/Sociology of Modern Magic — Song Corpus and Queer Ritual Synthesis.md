# Sociology of Modern Magic — Song Corpus and Queer Ritual Synthesis

See also [[Pagan and Queer Ritual Studies MOC]].
## Summary
A sociological integration essay synthesizing two empirical sources: an inductive literary discourse analysis of witchcraft in popular song lyrics (1940s–2020s) and [[Queering Neo-Pagan Magic — 2006 Paper|Martin Lepage's ethnography]] of queer Pagan practitioners in Montreal. Argues that magic functions as a moving boundary object across art and religion, entertainment and ontology, norm and resistance. Connected to [[Social Compass Paper — Ritual Authority and Régime de Preuve]] and the broader [[Agatha All Along — Wicca, Digital Mediatization, and Proof Regimes]] thread.

## Context
Source: `raw sources/The Sociology of Modern Magic From.txt`. The document presents itself as an integrative sociological reading, treating both the song corpus and the ethnography as parallel empirical terrains. It adds Durkheim, Bourdieu, Turner, and Foucault as interpretive anchors. Part of the queer Pagan corpus extending the 2010 mythocritique substrate (see [[Mythocritique to PHAROS — The 2010 Master's Thesis as Methodological Keystone]]); the song corpus side connects to *le souffle* of the recent [[Pourquoi rêver encore — Lecture allégorique et métaphysique d'Yvon Rivard|Pourquoi rêver encore]] essay. Indexed in [[Queer Sociology of Magic and Ritual]]. Related to [[Authority After Legitimacy — Disenchantment and Queer Political Theory]] and the [[Writing and Novels MOC]] through the cultural theory thread.

## Details

### Three-Axis Integration
1. **Cultural circulation of enchantment** — music as social narrative
2. **Embodied production of magical identity** — queer ritual practice
3. **Labor of normativity** — how legitimacy boundaries are enforced and undone

### The Song Corpus: Mid-Century to Now
From Sinatra to Lady Gaga, witchcraft in popular music functions as a "socially sanctioned language of transgression." Mid-century crooners externalize emotional vulnerability through the idiom of enchantment — "It's witchcraft" is a safe confession. Magic externalizes affect to maintain masculine coherence.

This mirrors the academic impulse Bailey describes: stabilizing an unstable category. Both the love song and the scholarly text manage the same normative anxiety about loss of control.

### Queering the Category
Where popular music aestheticizes magic, queer practitioners operationalize it. The shift is from "bewitched" to "bewitcher," from muse to maker. This embodies a sociological transition from objectification to subjectification.

### Ambiguity as Social Function
Shared insight across both datasets: ambiguity is socially functional.
- Song loops enact Durkheimian *collective effervescence*
- Queer magic's refusal to define itself (Bourdieu) undermines *symbolic violence* — the imposition of legitimate categories

### Community and Re-enchantment
Pop enchantment: fleeting identification, escape through aesthetic pleasure. Queer Pagan ritual: enduring alternative kinship structures. This marks a late-modern transformation from collective spectatorship toward micro-communities of embodied meaning.

Connects to Sabina Magliocco's call for re-enchantment as resistance against bureaucratic rationality.

### Toward a General Sociology of Magic
Proposed sociological propositions from the synthesis:
- Magic as social boundary maintenance
- Magic as a mirror of gendered power (witch feminized in pop; reclaimed by queer practitioners)
- Ritual communitas through repetition and rhythm (Turner)
- Disenchantment as structural nostalgia capitalism metabolizes

## Key Ideas
- Pop music domesticates magic as commodity; queer practice weaponizes its illegitimacy
- Both registers refuse final definitions — each mobilizes ambiguity differently
- The witch is always already a gendered figure; what changes is who controls the designation
- Re-enchantment is political, not nostalgic
