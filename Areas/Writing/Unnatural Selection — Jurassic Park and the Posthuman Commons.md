# Unnatural Selection — Jurassic Park and the Posthuman Commons

## Summary
Academic paper reading the *Jurassic Park* franchise (film series and source novels) through queer posthumanism to theorize the **posthuman commons** — a form of shared life and governance that emerges from the breakdown of human control over non-human actors. Author: Martin Lepage, PhD (1993 original, 2022 revision). The revision introduces Maisie Lockwood (from *Fallen Kingdom*) as the central figure for queer kinship, and Blue/Owen's negotiated care relationship as a model for posthuman governance. The franchise is read as a sustained meditation on the juridical limits of human governance over non-human life: the law cannot protect what it cannot name. Related to [[The Returning Light — Monograph]] and [[Historical Academic Portfolio — Pre-PHAROS Scholarly Work]].

## Context
Source: `2022 - Unnatural Selection - Jurassic Park.docx`. This is the 2022 revision of a 1993 paper — an extraordinary 29-year revision arc. The original was written during or shortly after the first film's release; the 2022 revision incorporates all subsequent films through *Dominion* (2022) and develops the posthuman commons framework that appears in [[The Returning Light — Monograph]]. Listed in [[Historical Academic Portfolio — Pre-PHAROS Scholarly Work]] as a core example of the posthumanism and non-human agency thematic thread.

## The Posthuman Commons

A **posthuman commons** is a shared ecological, technological, and bodily condition that:
- Exceeds the boundaries of human governance and ownership
- Is born from the **breakdown** of enclosure and control rather than from planned design
- Includes non-human actors (animals, ecosystems, technologies, artificial life) as genuine participants in governance rather than objects of governance
- Cannot be restored to pre-commons conditions once the breakdown has occurred

**The Jurassic Park thesis**: InGen's attempt to enclose prehistoric life — to own, control, and commodify it — produces the very conditions (feral ecology, behavioral adaptation, evolutionary pressure) that make enclosure impossible. The commons emerges from the failure of enclosure.

This is the ecological version of the [[For Her Alone to Wield — Buffy Power as Infrastructure]] framework: enclosure → breakdown → fragmentation → commons. In Jurassic Park, the fragmentation phase is the emergence of a feral ecosystem that operates beyond human governance.

## Maisie Lockwood and Queer Kinship

Maisie Lockwood (introduced in *Fallen Kingdom*, 2018) is a human clone — a copy of a girl who died. She is:
- Neither biological offspring nor purely artificial
- The inheritor of two forms of parenthood (biological/genetic and relational/chosen)
- The most direct embodiment of the posthuman commons: a human who is also a non-human artifact

**Queer kinship as improvisation**: Maisie's family relations cannot be legibly expressed within the normative kinship structures of law, biology, or tradition. Her kinship is improvised — assembled from available materials (genetic inheritance, chosen family, shared condition with cloned animals) without institutional authorization.

**The governance gap**: Because Maisie exists at the intersection of human and artifact, the law cannot adequately protect her. The juridical categories designed for humans do not extend to clones; the categories designed for artifacts do not extend to sentient beings. The law cannot protect what it cannot name — and Maisie is unnameable within existing categories.

This connects to the [[Governance by Denial]] analysis: the classification gap is not neutral — it is a governance mechanism that denies protection by denying legibility.

## Blue and Owen's Negotiated Care

Blue (the Velociraptor) and Owen Grady's relationship across the franchise is read as a model of **posthuman governance through negotiated care**:

- The relationship is not ownership (Blue is not Owen's property)
- It is not pure domestication (Blue retains autonomous agency and frequently overrides Owen's commands)
- It is a sustained negotiation: Owen earns Blue's cooperation through consistent, caring behavior; Blue grants cooperation conditionally, based on that track record

**The governance model**: Authority through earned trust rather than institutional position or force. This is the same governance principle as Ursula the Bear in [[Authority After Legitimacy — Disenchantment and Queer Political Theory]] — care-based authority that does not claim institutional right.

**The limits of negotiated care**: *Dominion* shows that individual relationships of trust cannot substitute for institutional governance. Blue and Owen's relationship cannot protect Blue's offspring from exploitation; it cannot create the conditions for a just posthuman commons. Personal care is necessary but insufficient — it requires structural support to become governance.

## Feral Ecology as Governance Object

Once dinosaurs escape the islands (end of *Fallen Kingdom*), a feral ecology emerges:
- Dinosaurs occupy niches in existing ecosystems
- They adapt to new conditions outside the settings they were designed for
- They become uncontrollable precisely because they are acting like living organisms rather than like controlled artifacts

**The governance implication**: Feral ecologies cannot be governed by the same mechanisms as contained ones. Once the commons emerges, enclosure is not recoverable — only negotiation, coexistence, and governance redesign remain as options.

This is the ecological version of the [[PHAROS Invention Disclosure]] governance problem: once an AI system is deployed at scale, its behavior becomes a feral ecology of use cases, contexts, and emergent properties that cannot be re-contained into a controlled laboratory. Governance must shift from control to negotiation.

## The Juridical Limits Argument

The paper's most systematic theoretical contribution: a taxonomy of what law cannot govern in the posthuman commons.

**The law cannot protect what it cannot name**:
- Cloned humans (Maisie) fall outside both human rights frameworks and property frameworks
- Feral dinosaurs fall outside both wildlife protection frameworks and property frameworks
- Non-human intelligences fall outside both animal welfare frameworks and personhood frameworks

In each case, the failure is **categorical**: the entity exists, has interests, and can be harmed — but the governance apparatus was designed for entities that fit existing categories. The gap is not a loophole; it is a structural feature of governance systems built on exhaustive human categories.

**The governance design principle**: Governance systems for the posthuman commons must include explicit provisions for **entities that resist existing classification** — not as an exception but as a design requirement. This is the [[RECURSO — Final Audit and Ethical Review]] principle of classification gap governance applied to non-human actors.

## Five-Section Structure

1. **Enclosure and Its Failures** — InGen's privatization of prehistoric life; the enclosure model and its systemic vulnerabilities
2. **Maisie and the Limits of Kinship Law** — Clone personhood; queer kinship as improvisation; the juridical gap
3. **Blue and Owen: Negotiated Care as Governance Model** — Trust-based authority; the limits of personal care in structural contexts
4. **Feral Ecology and the Emergent Commons** — Post-enclosure governance; adaptation; the impossibility of re-containment
5. **The Posthuman Commons as Governance Framework** — Synthesis; implications for AI, biotechnology, and non-human governance

## Insights

- The 1993→2022 revision arc is itself evidence for the "queerness is the refusal to cohere" governing thread: the same text, revised across 29 years, does not try to smooth the early argument into the later theoretical vocabulary. The revision marks its own history by including both the original insights and their subsequent elaboration
- Feral ecology as an analogy for deployed AI systems is one of the most precise governance metaphors in the archive: once deployed at scale, AI systems adapt to use contexts they were not designed for, occupy niches in existing social ecologies, and become difficult to re-contain without massive disruption. The governance implication is identical: shift from control to negotiated coexistence
- The juridical limits argument anticipates the AI governance debate about AI personhood, rights, and accountability — but from a direction (ecological posthumanism) that most AI governance frameworks have not engaged
