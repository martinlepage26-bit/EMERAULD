# Queering Neo-Pagan Magic: Identity, Power, and the Uses of Magic

**Abstract**

This article explores how queer neo-Pagan practitioners in Montréal mobilize magic as a site of identity formation, healing, and world-making. Drawing on ethnographic fieldwork with five practitioners who identify across the queer and trans spectrum, we examine magic not as a stable belief system but as a pragmatic repertoire of uses — a relational verb rather than a metaphysical noun. Engaging Michael Bailey's influential account of magic as a constitutively unstable, boundary-marking category, we argue that our contribution lies not in disputing his diagnosis but in extending it: where Bailey tracks how instability functions in historical and scholarly classification, our fieldwork shows queer practitioners inhabiting that same instability as lived method, ritual resource, and political ground. Magic, as these practitioners use it, refuses both scientific reductionism and doctrinal coherence, functioning instead as a technology of self-making, a grammar of relational energy, and a means of rewriting inherited narratives of gender, kinship, and sacred legitimacy. We conclude that studying queer magic requires not only new interpretive frameworks but a willingness to queer scholarly method itself.

**Keywords: queer theory, neo-Paganism, magic, ritual, Montréal, ethnography, gender, identity, healing, legitimacy, belief, practice, embodiment, religious studies, feminist theory**






## Introduction

The term "to queer," both as a theoretical operation and as an embodied practice, implies a deliberate and layered movement, one that simultaneously adapts to, and resists, dominant normative frameworks. It functions as a form of critical negotiation: making space within oppressive systems while refusing to be fully contained by them. To queer is to reorient the gaze, to subvert the expected, to linger in contradiction. It marks a gesture that not only destabilizes categories such as gender, sexuality, and power, but also reveals the regulatory logics, social, epistemological, theological, that underpin them. In our earlier work, we explored this dynamic through the ritualized identities of Montréal's neo-Pagan community, focusing on the embodied and symbolic strategies by which non-binary practitioners articulated spiritual legitimacy in spaces still structured by assumptions of gender complementarity. These rituals, far from being ancillary expressions of belief, operated as tactical interventions, recasting sacred meaning in ways that exposed and unsettled the binary scaffolding that undergirded them.

This article extends that line of inquiry by placing the category of magic at the center of analysis, not merely as a residual belief system or marginal ritual form, but as a dense and contested terrain where norms are actively contested, rewritten, and at times, re-enchanted. Magic, as it appears in our fieldwork, is neither reducible to superstition nor easily assimilable to institutionalized religion. It operates instead as a queer vector, a site of praxis where gender, power, and cosmology intersect in ways that often defy linear logic or disciplinary taxonomy. We do not seek to offer a singular or stable definition of magic; rather, we attend to how magic is inhabited, felt, and mobilized by queer practitioners in order to reconfigure their relation to self, world, and other. In doing so, we engage, if at an angle, Michael Bailey's seminal text "The Meanings of Magic."¹ Our interlocutors dwell precisely at those thresholds Bailey describes. They do not merely test the boundaries he maps; they twist, bend, and reimagine them, transforming magic into a modality of queer survival and speculative world-making.

This intervention should not be read as a rejection of Michael Bailey's influential argument, but rather as a deepening of its implications. Bailey's account already understands magic as a slippery, relational category whose meanings emerge through contested acts of differentiation, exclusion, and boundary-making. His repeated insistence on magic's constitutive instability — its analytic value lying precisely in its historical slipperiness, its dependence on acts of differentiation, and its frequent designation by authorities rather than practitioners themselves — is not, in fact, an argument for definitional clarity. It is an argument against it. Magic, in Bailey's account, functions less as a coherent system than as a relational category, one that marks boundaries of legitimacy, power, and acceptable ritual action across time and culture. Our intervention begins from that insight. What it adds is a shift in perspective: not from stability to slippage, but from how scholars and authorities have used magic to mark boundaries of legitimacy to how queer practitioners inhabit that very instability as a method of survival, ritual action, and world-making.² The difference, in other words, is not in the diagnosis of magic's instability, but in who is doing the theorizing — and from where.

## Queering the Category of Magic

Rather than retracing the long and well-worn academic genealogy of magic, mapped out with considerable precision in Bailey's article, we begin instead with an epistemological refusal. This refusal is not born of disregard but of strategy. It resists the impulse to stabilize magic through typology or chronology, to trap it within the strictures of definition. To queer magic, in this sense, is to disrupt the very desire for conceptual containment. As feminist sociologist Diane Lamoureux reminds us, queer theory is not simply additive; it does not merely broaden the list of recognized identities or beliefs. It is subtractive and destabilizing, it undermines the presumed coherence of categories themselves.³ Queer theory interrogates the production of knowledge, tracing how classificatory systems become naturalized and how certain modes of being are rendered intelligible only at the cost of others' erasure. In this light, queer is less a political stance or identity label than a method, a critical grammar of unmaking. It is a way of refusing the "naturalness" of binary logics, such as man/woman, heterosexual/homosexual, sacred/profane, or, most pertinently here, religion/magic.

This methodological stance becomes particularly generative when applied to the study of religion, a field historically invested in the maintenance of binaries and boundaries. Religious studies has long relied on oppositions — between belief and practice, dogma and heresy, transcendence and immanence — to give shape to its object. The impulse to secure analytic objects by fixing their edges persists even where scholars have shown magic to be constitutively unstable. Queer magical practice unsettles this fixation, showing that instability and ambiguity can themselves be epistemic resources. But what if magic, in the hands and hearts of queer practitioners, resists such parsing? What if its epistemological force lies not in coherence, but in precisely the excess, the contradiction, the blur? What if its refusal to resolve is the very site of its potency?

The queer magical practices we encountered in Montréal do not cohere into a single theory of magic. They do not aspire to doctrinal unity or systematic clarity. Instead, they manifest what Lamoureux terms "a nomadic epistemology"⁴ — an orientation that moves across and between identities, rituals, affects, and cosmologies without settling into fixity. This mode of knowing is grounded not in hierarchy but in movement, in shifting resonance rather than definition. It is performative, relational, and often improvisational. Magic, in this context, is not a thing to be categorized but an experience to be inhabited. It pulses in the spaces between identity and desire, between trauma and transformation, between silence and voice. To queer magic, then, is not simply to elevate it from the margins, it is to reveal that the margins themselves hold methodologies, metaphysics, and meanings that disrupt the center. It is to suggest that what has been cast out as incoherent or irrational may in fact offer the most radical insights into how knowledge, power, and possibility circulate through ritual, belief, and embodiment.

## Three Motifs of Magical Practice

Among the queer neo-Pagan practitioners we spoke with, three recurring motifs emerged: transformation, healing, and meaning-making. These are not "meanings of magic" in Bailey's sense, as bounded, historical functions, but rather affective, embodied patterns of use.

Magic as transformation was a recurring theme in our interviews and observations. Jay, for example, identifies as a witch but avoids public ritual. For him, magic is not about gods or spirits but about energy, "electrical fields between people, something you can strengthen, concentrate, make into something real." It is a framework of empowerment, a spiritual physics grounded in self-responsibility.

This understanding aligns with Luhrmann's account of magic as a rational and transformational system,⁵ but Jay reframes it through a queer logic. His ritual world is not institutional, it is immediate, intimate, and self-fashioned. The magic he names is as much about refusing religious submission ("No! Thank yourself.") as it is about metaphysical efficacy.

The second motif, healing, surfaces in deeply personal accounts of pain, embodiment, and repair. Maria, raised Catholic and now identifying as bisexual, describes magic as "energy work" and "another sense," something that responds to balance and ethical intent. "There's always a cost," she says. "Like, you climb stairs, it strengthens your system, but it also wears you down. Same with magic." For Maria, healing is relational and energetic. Magic is both sensory and moral, operating in a field of reciprocity. Her account collapses the distinction between internal and external, self and world, sacred and profane. Magic is not symbolic, it is somatic, intuitive, and difficult to quantify.

As Sarah Pike argues, Pagan ritual spaces often function as "homeplaces," sanctuaries for those dispossessed by dominant religious or social orders.⁶ The practitioners we met used magic to narrate their pain, restore continuity, and reconnect with a sacred that does not condemn difference.

Magic, finally, became a means of meaning-making. Manon, a trans woman and experienced practitioner, told us: "I don't do a lot of magic, because it works. And it rarely works how you want. [...] When I do magic, I'm writing new stories." Here, magic is mythic intervention. It is not about results, but about the narrative possibilities that ritual opens up. It is a way of challenging dominant scripts, about gender, love, kinship, and writing oneself into another ontology. Manon's view, like others, refuses the instrumental logic that underlies both scientific critique and some rationalist defenses of magic (including parts of Bailey's piece). Magic, in this reading, is not irrational, it is imaginative, improvisational, and epistemically plural.

## From Belief to Use: Practitioners' Views

Across our fieldwork, one pattern emerged with striking regularity: a pronounced disinterest in defining magic as an abstract or universally applicable concept. While scholars often feel compelled to stabilize "magic" for comparative or analytic clarity, the queer neo-Pagan practitioners we interviewed displayed a far more situated and pragmatic orientation. For many, magic was not a matter of doctrinal consistency or ontological conviction. Its significance lay not in what it is, but in what it does, not in definition, but in deployment. In this sense, magic functions more as a relational verb than a static noun. It is a tool, a medium, a mood, a force, one that acquires meaning only in context, through use, and often through affectively charged improvisation.

Helena, a practitioner whose cosmology leans strongly toward animism, exemplifies this fluidity. "Magic is energy. It's in everything," she told us. "In emotions, in thoughts, in touch… You can change yourself through your prayers, your cards, your speech." Her conception of magic is expansive and intensely relational. It is not supernatural in the classical sense; it does not rest on an external metaphysics. Rather, it arises from the body's porous encounter with the world. In Helena's account, magic is not something believed in, it is something sensed, shifted, navigated. Energy is not abstract; it is tactile. Ritual, for her, is not a performance of inherited forms, it is an energetic recalibration, a tuning of presence. This understanding radically decenters the sacred/secular distinction that animates so much of Western scholarship.

What Helena offers is not a theory of magic but an ontology of relation, one that collapses the oppositional schema Bailey so carefully delineates in "The Meanings of Magic." In her world, religion, science, and magic are not mutually exclusive domains. Magic is not a failed science or irrational relic, nor a system of oppositional beliefs, it is a daily practice of responsiveness. Far from marginal, Helena's cosmology is primary. It is the condition of her knowing, her sensing, her engaging. It makes the world not just intelligible, but breathable.

Simon, a trans man and longtime practitioner, frames magic from a more tactical, realist perspective. "You shift the odds," he explained, referring to rituals he performs in support of his wife's job search. "She does the resume, the applications, the interviews, I do the magic. Maybe we do a sex magic ritual the night before. Not to make something happen, but to boost the chances." For Simon, magic is not a guarantee or miracle. It does not override effort or replace strategy. It amplifies intent, focuses energy, sharpens desire. His approach strips magic of mystification without rendering it inert. It functions as a partner to the mundane, not a substitute for it. Magic is part of the work.

Simon's account offers a challenge to both magical literalism and skeptical reductionism. He does not claim magic causes job offers. He claims it alters the field of possibility. It makes certain outcomes more available, not more certain. In this way, magic becomes a form of pragmatic relationality, a way of leaning into desire without assuming control. His rituals are not about changing the laws of the universe; they are about participating in them differently.

Here again, the concept of efficacy demands reevaluation. Magic is not effective because it produces visible change; it is effective because it allows practitioners to act within uncertainty, to cohere identity and intention in situations otherwise marked by precarity.

Both Helena and Simon, in different ways, dislocate magic from the epistemological anxieties that haunt academic treatments. Their practices bypass the question "Is it real?" and instead ask, "What happens when I do this?" In doing so, they displace belief as the central axis of religious or spiritual life. Magic is not something they adhere to, it is something they inhabit, perform, feel. It is a way of being in relation to energies, bodies, outcomes, and communities.

These accounts do not conform to classical models of magic, whether framed through Frazer's sympathetic laws, Mauss's mana, or Malinowski's anxiety-reducing function. Nor do they fit neatly within the binary structures that categorize magic as primitive, religion as moral, and science as rational. Instead, they articulate a mode of knowing that is embodied, iterative, and radically situated. Magic is not a belief system to be accepted or rejected. It is a repertoire, a tactical grammar of energy, story, gesture, and care.

Importantly, these views reveal that the category "magic" continues to function in the present, not as a discarded or superseded worldview, but as a flexible, adaptive, and critically alive site of engagement. Magic is not the other of modernity. It is one of its undercurrents, carried through queer bodies, minoritized practices, and ritual creativity that refuses assimilation. Bailey identifies magic's illegitimacy as an analytic marker. For queer practitioners, this illegitimacy is not merely endured, it is cultivated as sacred ground, a stance that redefines what counts as power in ritual life. They do not seek to rescue magic from its marginal status, they weaponize that marginality to build meaning, kinship, and power on their own terms.

In this, we are urged to reconsider the very frameworks that have shaped scholarly understandings of belief and ritual. Magic, as these practitioners use it, is not reducible to symbol, superstition, or therapy. It is a methodology, a lived, recursive, and unapologetically queer way of shaping the real.

## Queer Magic at Three Social Levels

The practices described above operate at three overlapping levels: individual, relational, and cultural. At the individual level, magic becomes a technology of identity, one that allows practitioners to inscribe coherence and power into lives often marked by erasure. Ritual acts offer a means of constructing the self, not as essence, but as process.

At the relational level, magic builds community. Groups like Lokkabrenna, a queer-inclusive Pagan kindred in Montréal, use ritual to articulate new forms of kinship. These are not symbolic gestures, they are lived commitments to recognition and care.

At the cultural level, magic serves as a tool of re-enchantment. Drawing from folklore, popular culture, memory, and myth, practitioners reclaim symbols and stories that dominant culture has rendered illegitimate. This is not escapism. It is reworlding.

Following Sabina Magliocco, we understand this as part of a broader refusal of disenchantment.⁷ Queer practitioners do not romanticize the past; they repurpose it. Their magic is syncretic, playful, and profoundly political.

## Magic, Use, and Academic Discomfort: Toward a Queer Use of Magic

Bailey's analysis of magic already gestures toward many of the dynamics foregrounded here. His sustained attention to magic as a category produced through acts of differentiation, exclusion, and power renders it analytically productive precisely because it resists closure. Magic, in his formulation, remains a category that never fully stabilizes, whose meanings proliferate across contexts, and whose boundaries are constantly renegotiated. What queer magical practice adds to this account is not a denial of analytic rigor, but a shift in perspective: from the scholarly management of slippage to its lived inhabitation. For the practitioners we encountered, ambiguity is not an analytic problem to be solved but a condition of possibility, one that enables ritual improvisation, identity repair, and speculative relation-making. If Bailey shows how magic operates as a boundary category within scholarly and historical discourse, queer magic demonstrates how that same instability can be mobilized as method, affective strategy, and form of embodied knowledge. The difference, then, is not whether magic is slippery, but who is allowed to use that slipperiness — and to what ends.⁸ If Bailey shows that magic has long operated as a boundary category, queer magical practice asks what happens when those placed near the boundary cease to appear only as its objects and become theorists and users of its instability.

Where Bailey treats illegitimacy as an analytic marker, queer practitioners claim it as sacred territory, turning the very sign of exclusion into a tool for re-worlding. They do not merely inhabit the margins; they make them generative. They do not seek to legitimize their practice through theological or scholarly recognition. Instead, they queer the category itself: undoing its expectations, aestheticizing its illegitimacy, and drawing sacred power from its instability.

This troubles academic method. Queer magic, as we encountered it, does not aim for doctrinal consistency or empirical legibility. It blends sincerity with irony, reverence with improvisation, and affective intensity with epistemic unruliness. For many scholars trained to expect belief as either orthodoxy or metaphor, this hybridity feels methodologically threatening. But perhaps it is our categories — "belief," "tradition," "religion" — that are showing their limits.

Rather than clarify what magic is, our fieldwork reveals what magic does: it repairs, resists, connects, and re-enchants. It functions not as a leftover relic of superstition, but as a method of relation, a tool of agency, and a site of world-building. To ask "Do they really believe it?" is to misrecognize the purpose of these practices. The question itself, so common in magical ethnography, imposes a false binary between authenticity and play, conviction and performance. For queer practitioners, ritual is not about doctrinal assent. It is about shaping experience, navigating trauma, and conjuring continuity where none is given.

What emerges is not a new definition of magic, but a provocation to reframe our approach. The queer use of magic foregrounds practice over belief, improvisation over doctrine, and ambiguity over coherence. In doing so, it unsettles the taxonomic drive in religious studies and adjacent disciplines. Bailey's boundary metaphor still serves, but not to signal limits. In queer hands, boundaries become porous stages for improvisation, a choreography of relation, not a line of separation.

To study queer magic, then, is not only to observe a cultural phenomenon. It is to confront the limits of our methods. It asks us to sit with uncertainty, to attend to the fragmentary and the felt, and to let go of the desire to make our subjects tidy. It is not merely about queering religion, it is about queering scholarship itself. Magic, in this context, does not just evade definition, it refuses to be domesticated. And scholarship must ask whose comfort is served by the desire to domesticate it. Perhaps our task, as scholars, is not to tame it, but to let it enchant the study.
