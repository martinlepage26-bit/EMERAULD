Abstract
Background: Schizophrenia is a complex neuropsychiatric disorder for which accurate and objective diagnostic tools remain limited. Advances in neuroimaging and machine learning have enabled the use of multimodal biomarkers, while eye-tracking has emerged as a promising behavioral indicator of cognitive and oculomotor dysfunction. Methods: A systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, and the Google Scholar database was conducted for studies published between 2015 and 2025. Eligible studies reported quantitative diagnostic performance of structural MRI, functional MRI, diffusion imaging, eye-tracking, or their multimodal fusion in identifying schizophrenia. Eight studies met the inclusion criteria. Data extraction included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC. Pooled estimates were calculated using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity, subgroup analyses, and publication bias were performed. Results: Across all included studies, multimodal approaches demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared with unimodal methods. The pooled diagnostic accuracy across all modalities was 89% (95% CI: 85 - 92%), with a sensitivity of 86% and specificity of 90%. Multimodal integration of neuroimaging and eye-tracking achieved the highest accuracy (91%), outperforming unimodal neuroimaging (85%) and unimodal eye-tracking (87%). The pooled AUC for integrated models was 0.94, indicating excellent discriminative ability. Heterogeneity was moderate overall (I² = 38%), and publication bias was not detected. Conclusion: Multimodal neuroimaging combined with eye-tracking provides a highly accurate and robust diagnostic framework for schizophrenia, outperforming single-modality approaches. These findings support the use of integrated neural and behavioral biomarkers as objective tools that may complement clinical assessment and enhance early detection. 
Keywords: Schizophrenia; Multimodal neuroimaging; Eye-tracking; Machine learning; Diagnostic accuracy; Meta-analysis.
Introduction
Schizophrenia is a long-term psychiatric condition considered to be one of the most debilitating disorders (1) because of its negative impact on one's cognition, perception, emotion, and behavior. Schizophrenia affects ~1% of the global population and is one of the top causes of disability among people in the young adult age range, and has a significant socioeconomic impact on the affected individuals, families, and healthcare systems(2, 3). While symptom-based criteria in the DSM-5 and ICD-10 provide the framework for a clinical diagnosis, these criteria are often qualitative, subjective, and lack biological contextualization, which is problematic because of the variability (4). Identifying and diagnosing the condition is particularly problematic in clinical settings involving the early stages of the condition (prodromal and first episode stages) because symptoms tend to be vague and/or nonspecific(4).
Neuroimaging has shown how to detect brain alterations caused by schizophrenia in the last two decades(5). Studies using structural MRIs have shown the negative brain alterations caused by schizophrenia, such as the thinning of brain tissues, enlargement of brain ventricles, and disruption in the integrity of the brain's gray and white matter(6). The brain's functional connectivity, activity in the brain's default mode network, and activity during task completion have also been shown to have abnormalities caused by schizophrenia(7). White matter microstructure damage due to schizophrenia has also been identified using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)(8). This has led to the conclusion that schizophrenia is a disorder that interrupts connectivity and is a dysconnectivity disorder that affects a network of distributed(9). However, with all the evidence of schizophrenia's neurobiological alterations, the evidence from the neuroimaging remains inconsistent with one another(10). This is due to variability in the methods used, differences in the samples used, and the disorder's complexity. 
Alongside the shift in neuroimaging, the field of eye-tracking has been appreciated as a potential objective biomarker for schizophrenia(11). Patients often demonstrate different eye movement control features, such as problems with smooth pursuit and fixation. Patients have also been found to have a high number of saccades and show strange gaze activity while participating in a free viewing task(12, 13). These actions demonstrate a basic breakdown of visual attention, sensorimotor integration, and cognitive control, all of which are patterns that are known to be disrupted in schizophrenia. Eye-tracking all of these movements is also non-invasive, easy to use, and inexpensive, which are features that make it well-suited for large-scale use and in practical clinical use(14).
Recent advancements have made it possible to combine machine learning and deep learning with various multimodal data streams, such as structural, functional, and diffusion MRI, in tandem with eye tracking, cognitive data, and transcriptomic data. These models are built to identify features across biological and behavioral domains, thereby enhancing classification in ways no single modality could reach. Early work combining imaging and eye movements demonstrates improved classification accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, providing initial empirical support for the validity of multimodal logits as objective diagnostic tools for schizophrenia.  
However, no previous work has holistically synthesized and quantitatively evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of multimodal neuroimaging with eye tracking. Existing systematic reviews have focused primarily on neuroimaging, while reviews of eye tracking have tended to be smaller and independent of neuroimaging(15). As a result, the field has a void in integrated reviews that have determined whether the new wave of multimodal fusion can substantively improve diagnostic accuracy over its unimodal predecessors.
Based on this gap, the present meta-analysis assesses the last decade's defense to determine what has been achieved in the diagnostic accuracy of multimodal neuroimaging models, eye-tracking models, and combined neuroimaging plus eye-tracking frameworks toward the detection schizophrenia to address: (1) the extent of pooled accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the AUC across the modalities; (2) the diagnostic performance of multimodal fusion; and (3) the assessment of methodological heterogeneity, publication bias, and the findings' robustness. The findings from this research offer an evidence-based premise to the quest for the development of objective, multimodal diagnostic tools to complement the clinical assessment for the early detection of schizophrenia.
Method
   1. Search Strategy
For this study, various studies concerning the diagnostic accuracy of the integration of various neuroimaging techniques along with eye-tracking technology in the case of schizophrenia, extending from the period of 2015 to 2025, were systematically and comprehensively evaluated, while also taking into consideration the most recent and pertinent advances made in the field of neuroimaging, as well as computational diagnostic tools.
The databases to be reviewed were PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, and Google Scholar. A search strategy involving MeSH together with free-text keywords in amalgamation was applied in an attempt to obtain the highest quantity of the target studies. Search terms were as follows:
("schizophrenia" OR "psychosis") AND ("multimodal neuroimaging" OR "MRI" OR "fMRI" OR "DTI" OR "neuroimaging integration") AND ("eye-tracking" OR "oculomotor" OR "gaze tracking") AND ("diagnostic accuracy" OR "classification" OR "machine learning" OR "deep learning").
The search was restricted to articles in English published between 2015 and 2025. To obtain relevant studies not included search databases, the reference lists of the studies identified were examined. The various studies were transferred to EndNote 21 to manage the citations, and prior to the screening, duplicates were removed. Two reviewers conducted the independent review of the titles and abstracts to ascertain that the focus of the study was, in fact, the diagnostic accuracy of schizophrenia with the incorporation of various neuroimaging and/or eye-tracking modalities.
To meet the criterion of methodological rigor, meta-analyses, systematic reviews, case reports, as well as studies published in any language other than English were excluded. Only original quantitative studies that included metrics of diagnostic performance (i.e., accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, or AUC) were included in the data extraction. 
2. Eligibility Criteria
The following studies were filtered and included based on specific PICO inclusion and exclusion criteria and following methodological verification to maintain studies included based on PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparators, and Outcomes). Studies were included that had adult populations who had a schizophrenia diagnosis and first-episode psychosis based on clinical criteria from Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV or DSM-5), or International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). Included studies were those that primary or uni/multimodal neuroimaging (sMRI, fMRI, or DTI), or eye-tracking technologies (used alone or in combination). The types of eye-tracking technologies may have included free-viewing, smooth pursuit, fixation stability, or saccade.
The included studies had to have a comparative component that included individuals with schizophrenia and healthy control participants or had to have some form of comparison that focused on differing diagnostic criteria (e.g., unimodal versus multimodal neuroimaging). This study only included quantitative reports on performance metrics about diagnosis to be considered for inclusion (classification, sensitivity, specificity, AUC). For quality of data and relevance, only primary empirical studies were included (cross-sectional, observational or experimental).
These searches were conducted from 2015 to 2025, focusing only on English publications to capture advances in methodology around multimodal neuroimaging and computational modeling. Non-English publications, as well as review or meta-analytic papers, conference papers, editorials, or letters to the editor, were excluded from the study. Non-schizophrenia psychiatric disorder studies, such as studies on bipolar disorder or autism spectrum disorder, were also excluded. In addition, research studies that focused on animals or children, studies that included pediatric populations, studies that did not include data on the quantitative diagnostic accuracy outcomes, and studies with duplicate data sets were also excluded from the study. The stated inclusion and exclusion criteria provided the synthesis with only original research that qualified as highly relevant to the diagnostic accuracy evaluation of multimodal neuroimaging and eye-tracking.
3. Study Selection
To ensure the selection of studies complies with the guidelines outlined by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) in terms of methodology and the ability to reproduce the results, duplicates were removed, and the titles and abstracts in all obtained records were screened by two reviewers independently to decide which studies pertained to the diagnostic accuracy of multimodal neuroimaging and eye-tracking in schizophrenia, After the individual screening, the studies which did not reach an agreement were discussed and, if necessary, they consulted a third reviewer.
After the first screening in which the reviewers did not reach an agreement, all potentially relevant studies were obtained in full text and reviewed thoroughly against the previously established inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies were included if they were original and quantitative and, in the case of neuroimaging, eye-tracking, and/or their multimodal integration, were about the diagnostic classification or discrimination of schizophrenia patients from healthy controls. Any reviews, editorials, proceedings from conferences, and works not in English were removed at this point.
4. Data Extraction and Management
Data were first preciously organized for extraction systematically to uphold the principles of accuracy, completeness, and reproducibility. The first stage of data extraction involved two independent reviewers for each of the primary studies, while disagreements were settled by a consensus. In an attempt to minimize biases and maximize systematic transparency, data were entered based on pre-defined extraction templates using Microsoft Excel. 
The following data were also extracted for each study: names of the respective authors, year the study was published, type of study (design), characteristics of the study population (which also included details on the number of sufferers of the schizophrenia cohort paired against healthy controls), what type of diagnostic tests were used, algorithm type or which machine learning model was used, and what were the metrics of interest regarding diagnostic abilities. In detail, data on classification success rates, sensitivity, specificity were captured, and for the primary instrument, the AUC, and kept for subsequent meta-analysis. In addition to the primary metrics, secondary metrics were captured, when available, precision, F1 scoring, and even cross-validation matrices were captured. 
Other than the diagnostic outcome metrics, other methodological features were also captured, the type of neuroimaging instrument (structural MRI, functional MRI, diffusion tensor imaging), inclusion of eye tracking, and cognitive/electrophysiological measures in the MOD. Studies that used a simple unimodal imaging were included only if they included in their frameworks a multimodal integration, or in the case of the parity of the research design, they used a unimodal imaging as the comparator respect. To maintain uniformity across the dataset, conversion of all accuracy metrics to percentage values was performed. Reported statistics were calculated using the data available from confusion matrices or ROC analyses. The integrity of the data was verified by the original articles.
5. Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis
To accommodate the methodological differences of studies, the DerSimonian-Laird method was employed to compile heterogenous studies of diagnostic performance that included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC as these performance metrics stacked up against other studies. In the case where several models were included, the findings from the best ranked multimodal technique were included in the study. Sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic odds ratios DORs were pooled and 95 percent confidence intervals were estimated. A summary receiver operating characteristic SROC curve, along with the AUC, was used to measure and summarize the overall diagnostic performance accuracy.  
Cochran's Q and I² whereby I² values above 50 % showed heterogeneity, and Subgroup analyses were designed to account for differences triggered by modality type, and classification algorithm, machine learning, and deep learning, to mention a few. Funnel plots and Egger's regression were used to measure and determine publication biases, and the trim and fill method was introduced in cases where bias was seen in the study. Analyses to determine the values of p less than and greater than 0.05 were done in RevMan 5.4 and R version 4.3 where the meta and mada were used.
Results
   1. Study Selection
In total, records that had been published in the span of 2015 to 2025, yielded 287 entries on the databases of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, and Google Scholar. Removal of 61 duplicates left 226 unique texts, which were progressively filtered by title and abstract. At this step, 184 records were discarded because they did not pertain to diagnostic accuracy, had no relation to the methodologies of multimodal neuroimaging and eye-tracking techniques, or studied countries other than schizophrenia.
A full-text eligibility assessment was conducted on 42 records. After completing this step, 34 articles were excluded for insufficient diagnostic data (n = 15), language other than English (n = 6), review or meta-analysis (n = 8), and duplication of databases (n = 5). In the end, a total of 8 studies were subjected to the final meta-analysis as they had all the required entry criteria (16-23). 
The studies published from 2018 to 2025 had as their main focus the evaluation of diagnostic performance from the integration of functional and structural MRI, diffusion tensor imaging, eye-tracking machine learning, and multimodal neuroimaging, along with data from cognitive neuroscience or transcriptomics. The selected studies were methodologically sound for assessing the diagnostic accuracy of integrating neuroimaging with eye-tracking in schizophrenia.

   2. Study Characteristics
This meta-analysis included all eight studies undertaken from 2018 - 2025, which assessed the diagnostic capabilities of multimodal neuroimaging; the neuroimaging studies focused on the use of both eye-tracking techniques and neuroimaging alone, singularly or in combination, to identify the presence of schizophrenia. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.  A range of sample sizes formed the basis of these studies, from the smallest  -  44 participants (Kacur et al., 2020) to the largest 747 participants (Lei et al., 2019), which included both patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls. A combination of studies used functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), structural MRI (sMRI), diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), and eye tracking, associated with transcriptomic data. A few of the studies employed artificial intelligence (AI) methods in the form of machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models, specifically support vector machine (SVM), convolutional neural network (CNN), Gradient Boosting, and ResNet attention models, to differentiate schizophrenia from controls based on the integration of multiple sources of information.
The diagnostic performance for the studies in this report ranged from 69% to 96%, with the most accurate being Dang and Wang's (2025) eye-tracking and cognitive model at 96.37%. The studies that analyzed the dynamics of the brain and the structure of the brain (fMRI + sMRI) also performed very well. Above 85% were the results for the studies by Lei et al. (2019), Salvador et al. (2019), and Shi et al. (2021). Studies that used structural MRI and integrated DTI (Liang et al. 2018) also performed reasonably well, with diagnostic accuracy of 75.05% and the eye-tracking unimodal models performed within the range of 75 to 79%.
Machine learning techniques also differed from one study to the other. Most studies adopted some forms of cross-validation, either 10-fold or leave-one-out cross-validation. There is also the study by Wang et al. (2024) that combined neuroimaging with transcriptome profiling and attained high accuracy (AUC=0.92), showcasing the benefit of integrating multiple biological datasets.    3. Diagnostic Accuracy of Multimodal Neuroimaging
The sensitivity of the diagnostic performance of multimodal neuroimaging techniques concerning the distinction of schizophrenia patients from healthy controls showed no variability in all the studies. With the combination of sMRI and fMRI in the analysis conducted, there was improvement in overall classification of the outcomes from the analyses of the studies when comparing them with analyses from studies that used a single modality.
The studies that used the combination of different modalities of MRI reported a range in diagnostic accuracy within the mean of 83.5% and 91% with a sensitivity range of 68% and 88% and a specificity within the 90% range, while the highest accuracies were from machine learning algorithms that were a combination of SVM, and M3 in the case of supportive models.    4.       Diagnostic Accuracy of Eye-Tracking Approaches
Several studies, including those that employed advanced techniques from machine learning and cognition, showed that eye tracking methodology and technologies demonstrated substantial capabilities, and the eye tracking models exhibited high diagnostic accuracy ranging from 75 to 96%. This is substantial as it demonstrates the eye tracking technologies' considerable ability to differentiate between those suffering from schizophrenia and those not.  
In studies carried out by Kacur and others (2020), focusing on the oculomotor indices, i.e., the fixation range, rate, and saccade characteristics as well as those of the healthy participants, the authors obtained 78.8% classification accuracy. These studies of a small sample size of 22 subjects and participants per group demonstrated the importance of eye tracking technologies.  
In Dang and Wang (2025) and other eye tracking studies, the authors employed larger sample sizes of 159 subjects and participants and used an RANet. In integrating the raw eye tracking data and analyzing gaze features, the authors achieved 96.37% classification accuracy, a 96.9% sensitivity, and a 95.9% specificity, which demonstrated the highest performing models of the studies, especially when algorithm data is combined with other behavioral data.
A mean diagnostic accuracy of 87% (95% CI: 82%-91%), sensitivity of 87% (95% CI: 82%-92%), and specificity of 88% (95% CI: 84%-92%) were attained by using the pooled random-effects model for the eye-tracking-based accuracy studies. The overall AUC was 0.90, demonstrating exemplary diagnostic performance across the studies. The overall heterogeneity between studies was low (I^2 = 32%), indicating a degree of coherence in the results, despite variance in the methodologies used in the studies (Table 3).

   5.       Combined Multimodal Neuroimaging and Eye-Tracking Models
The application of multimodal neuroimaging and eye tracking data achieved the highest diagnostic performance across all modelling techniques of this meta-analysis. Studies integrating both neurophysiological and behavioral data achieved significantly greater classification accuracies. This points to the value of employing both neural and oculomotor biomarkers in the diagnosis of schizophrenia.
Among the ensemble models, Dang and Wang (2025) achieved the highest results with 96.37% classification accuracy, 96.9% sensitivity, and 95.9% specificity through the implementation of a ResNet-based attention model (RANet-ET) that amalgamated eye tracking and cognitive performance features. Likewise, more than 90\% classification accuracies were achieved when behavioral features were integrated with MRI data due to a strong fusion foundation of static and dynamic features by Lei et al. (2019) and Shi et al. (2021).
The average diagnostic accuracy was 91% (95% CI: 87% - 94%), and the sensitivity and specificity were 89% (95% CI: 84% - 93%) and 92% (95% CI: 88% - 95%), respectively, due to the random effects model on the aggregated data. There was also a pooled AUC of 0.94, which indicates that the models performed extremely well on the classification task. Multimodal models always achieved at least 8-12% superior performance than the unimodal models incorporating neuroimaging and/or eye tracking.

   6. Heterogeneity Assessment
The actual variability in diagnostic performance due to study design, imaging technique, and analysis methodology was assessed in the included studies to determine heterogeneity. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed by Cochran's Q test and the I2 statistic, which indicates the proportion of the total variance due to heterogeneity between studies rather than variance due to sampling error. The average heterogeneity of the study was I2 = 46", and p = 0.08. A subgroup thematic analysis indicated that heterogeneity was largely caused by differences in the methodology of feature extraction and machine learning, as well as target sample size and composition. In particular, the studies that utilized deep learning architectures, including ResNet or varying CNN models, were correlated with greater diagnostic efficacy but also contributed to more heterogeneity among studies, which was due to differences in data prep and model training policies.
In the studies based on eye-tracking, heterogeneity was I2 = 32%, which was lower than the studies in the multimodal neuroimaging. This indicates that there is less variation in the behavioral protocol data compared to the imaging data. The studies that used both imaging neurotechnologies along with eye-tracking showed I2 = 22%, indicating low heterogeneity and more dependable outcomes. Based on the Forest Plot (Figure 4), it was possible to see that the effect sizes were mostly consistent without having any significant outlier that would distort the combined estimates of diagnostic accuracy.

   7. Publication Bias
Publication Bias is defined as the phenomenon of publishing only those studies that display favorable outcomes of a diagnostic test or a selective reporting of such studies. This publication bias was investigated by visually checking the symmetry of the effect sizes of the included studies against the errors of their standard deviation as depicted in the funnel plot. To Egger's regression test, a bias test from metric distance to standard errors was included.
Although there is some concern with the plot symmetry, some bias may exist with some of the studies, we can be more or less certain that there is negligible publication bias since most studies were represented around the average of the diagnostic accuracy metric, and studies with more extreme lower accuracy were equally likely to be included. Visual findings were corroborated with Egger's regression test, which indicated p = 0.241; a small p value is evidence of small study bias effect. With the absence of any estimated imputed studies from the trim and fill, which would hypothetically correct the plot symmetry, the variance diagnostic accuracy adjusted from the trim and fill for publication bias remained 88% statistically the same, which adds to the confidence in the findings.
Overall, statistical as well as graphical evaluations show that publication bias is not likely to have substantially impacted the conclusions drawn in this meta-analysis. The results of the distribution of the funnel plots are displayed in Figure 5.

   8. Sensitivity and Subgroup Analyses
Heterogeneity was derived from sensitivity and subgroup analyses assessing the robustness of the pooled estimates from the studies. It widened the field of study and the focus of interest to the type of modality, machine learning algorithm, and the samples of the studies in relation to the estimates of accuracy of the diagnosis across the various studies.
Analysis of the pooled accuracy demonstrated that no study predominated the influence on the overall pooled diagnostic accuracy. No significant change in the estimates of accuracy was derived from the removal of any study from the range of pooled estimates (from 87% to 89%), demonstrating the extreme homogeneity and the strength of the model. The differences in the models based on the type of modality used showed models that were integrated which used a combination of multimodal neuroimaging with eye-tracking were identified to have the highest predictor values in diagnosis with a pooled accuracy of 91% (95% CI: 87% - 94%), while models that were unimodal in eye-tracking 87% and unimodal with neuroimaging were 85% were on the second and third.
In the studies that adopted deep learning models, the increased accuracy was 91% in the diagnosis, in comparison to the traditional machine learning models, whereby the average accuracy stood at 84%. Both studies indicated that the models were effective in achieving the desired outcome of the studies. It was no surprise that the statistical tests indicated that the performance differences were of no major consequence (p = 0.11). A particular forest plot for subgroup analyses depicts the relative diagnostic performance of each algorithmic class and reflects the sustained superlative performance of the deep learning-based multimodal models. Discussion
The ability of eye-movement tracking and multimodal neuroimaging methods in diagnosing schizophrenia has been analyzed in this research. According to the aggregated analysis, the estimated diagnosis accuracy ranged from 87% to 90%. Along with that, AUC pointers indicated successful results of 93%. Of the gathered results, neuroimaging and oculomotor tracking in the measurement of schizophrenia objectively surpass all other methods (MRI and structural, eye tracking) in the field of diagnosis. The evidence that has been gathered is similar to other studies in the research field in the sense that MRI results suggest that the integration of multimodal structural and functional fMRI will also increase the accuracy of the diagnosis (LEG, 2019) to world-appropriate levels. Along with that, an improvement of over 90% has been observed in the accuracy of integration of multiscale imaging with predictive genetics, and this has been achieved in recent studies that have been conducted.
Previous studies using machine learning-based multimodal techniques to form a diagnosis and classification of schizophrenia have demonstrated similar findings. Studies by Lei et al. (2019) reported a classification accuracy of 90.83% using a support vector machine (SVM) on combined sMRI and fMRI, and Shi et al. (2021) using the M3 (Multimodal Multilevel ML) with 83.49% accuracy. Both studies noted that multimodal fusion captures intrinsic neurobiological information that remains covert with a single analytic mode (Shi et al., 2021). 
Moreover, the present study results support the findings of (24), who reported a profound of the traditional ML framework with automation of deep learning architecture (AutoML) in the analysis of multimodal brain features and proposed that automated deep learning could provide improved reproducibility across various diagnostics. The findings were noted in various fusion deep learning models (ML) that incorporated structural and functional MRI, with the Multimodal Vision Transformer (MultiViT) model emerging with over 0.83 in AUC (25). Of note is adding eye-tracking data, which strengthens the accuracy of the diagnosis. When the cognitive data of the eye tracker were fed to emulating a ResNet-based attention network (RAnet-ET), it was able to achieve a perfect score of 96.37% (Dang & Wang, 2025). This is consistent with previous work that describes the gaze metrics, fixation stability, and saccade movement as being useful for detecting visual processing deficits tied to schizophrenia (26).  
The integration of different kinds of imaging devices and eye trackers is likely to have a big impact on the personalization of diagnostic psychiatry. Multi-modal imaging offers the possibility to capture real-time changes and provide behavioral biomarkers that are indicative of cognitive or attention dysfunction. This combination of imaging with an eye tracker creates a psycho-biological bridge that connects the neurobiological dysfunction with traces of behavioral integration (dys)function. More notably, it allows the possibility for improving the early detection of schizophrenia and more specifically in the first episodes or during prodromal phases in which the diagnosis is primarily based on psychos symptoms. The incorporation of AI-based multi-modal classification algorithms in the clinic is likely to provide an added value to the diagnostic process. This will complement clinical practice and allow for a more data-driven risk stratification (27).
Every study has its challenges and sticking points. The current meta-analysis is no different and has its own challenges we must consider. For one, only a small number of eligible studies could be part of this investigation. Some of the studies that were included also only had small sample sizes. The small sample sizes could compromise the generalizability of the pooled diagnostic estimates. Tesla's studies also had their own methodologies, elements like imaging protocols, different eye tracking paradigms, varying pipelines in the preprocessing, and machine learning architectures. This made it very difficult for next to each other comparisons via subgroup analyses. The majority of the studies were also cross sectional in their design, so none of the studies could properly assess diagnostic instability over time, or the value of multi-modal biomarkers in the early or pre-symptomatic phase of schizophrenia. There was a wide range of eye-tracking tasks that were implemented in each of the studies as well. This is not helpful and makes comparisons even more difficult. The last problem we are examining is that some of the studies did not have external validation, or in some cases, there was no transparent reporting of how the models could be interpreted or explained. This is vital for clinical reliability and the models being able to be used in practice.
Future research should aim to develop standardized protocols for neuroimaging and eye-tracking, which should help minimize methodological inconsistencies and improve reproducibility between research teams. There is a need for larger, multicenter investigations to assess the diagnostic accuracy of various multimodal models within broader and clinically heterogeneous populations, notably individuals at heightened risk for psychosis and those in the early stages of the disease. Longitudinal studies will be key in assessing whether multimodal biomarkers can forecast the disease's progression, response to treatment, or outcome functionality. Recent developments in interpretable machine learning that focus on attention-based models and explainable AI could improve clinician trust and initiate the adoption of such tools in psychiatric practice. In addition, the construction of more complex multimodal models incorporating genetic, electrophysiological, and cognitive data is likely to help devise more accurate, biologically relevant diagnostic approaches aligned with precision psychiatry.
Conclusion
This meta-analysis demonstrates that multimodal neuroimaging combined with eye tracking contributes to a better understanding of the structural and functional neural correlates of schizophrenia. Within this approach, neural and behavioral markers are combined to achieve near clinical levels of reliability (90 - 95% in some cases). These findings are only the beginning of a shift toward data-driven psychiatry, where clinically relevant multimodal neural and behavioral markers are used in conjunction with clinical assessments.
