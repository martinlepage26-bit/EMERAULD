[stutter] [[Stuttering through the Institution — Academic Containment and Queer Knowing]][[ai-stutter-jobs]] [[Disability Epistemology and Institutional Critique]]
I have gone through multiple AI-mediated recruiting and interview processes, including with Aligner and Mercor. Some of these interviews were broad and general. Others were highly specialized and directly connected to my actual areas of expertise.

The interviews I was most prepared for were the specialized ones. These were the conversations where I could speak from experience, not from abstraction. I could draw on work I had done, problems I had studied, systems I had evaluated, and risks I understood. I gave substantive answers. I engaged seriously with the questions. I treated the process as real.

What I received back was silence.

No meaningful feedback. No explanation of what was missing. No indication of whether my expertise had been understood. No opportunity to clarify. No response when I raised a specific accessibility concern.

I have a stutter. For me, that can mean repetitions, hesitations, restarts, uneven pacing, or moments where my speech does not move as smoothly as expected. These patterns are not signs that I lack knowledge. They are not signs that I am unprepared. They are not signs that I am confused. They are part of how my speech works.

That distinction should matter in any interview process. It matters even more in an AI-mediated one.

When a human interviewer is present, there is at least the possibility of context. A person can notice that someone is searching for a word, repeating a sound, restarting a sentence, or speaking around a block. A person can understand that fluency and intelligence are not the same thing. A person can adapt.

An automated process does not necessarily do that. It may transcribe. It may score. It may summarize. It may infer confidence, clarity, communication ability, or professional fit from signals that are not neutral. A hesitation may become a weakness. A repetition may become noise. A restart may become incoherence. A transcript may flatten a person into text that does not reflect what they know.

During these AI interviews, I tried to make my access need visible. I asked the AI interviewer to take my stutter into account. I asked that the transcription process not treat repetitions and hesitations as evidence of poor communication or lack of competence. I tried to make the system aware that speech fluency and expertise are not the same thing.

Still, I was rejected from every position I applied for, from basic customer service roles to highly specialized ones.

I do not know the exact reason for each rejection. That uncertainty is part of the problem. I am not claiming that every rejection was caused by my stutter. I am not claiming to know what the system scored, what a recruiter saw, or whether a human reviewed my answers at all.

But when an AI-mediated hiring process provides no useful feedback, no accessible appeal channel, no accommodation pathway, and no human response to a disability-related concern, candidates are left to infer what happened from silence.

That silence is not neutral.

It is part of the system.

AI recruiting is often presented as efficient, scalable, and objective. The promise is that these systems can process more candidates, reduce friction, standardize evaluation, and remove some forms of human bias. In theory, that sounds like progress.

In practice, efficiency without accountability can become a barrier. Scale without accessibility can reproduce exclusion faster. Objectivity without transparency can hide bias behind process.

The ethical issue is not only whether AI recruiting tools can reach diverse candidates. The deeper question is what happens when diverse candidates actually enter the process.

What happens when a candidate stutters?

What happens when a candidate is deaf or hard of hearing?

What happens when someone needs captions, typed responses, extra time, a different format, or a human interviewer?

What happens when someone has an accent, a speech difference, a neurodivergent communication style, or an access need that does not fit the assumptions built into the platform?

What happens when the person has the ability, but the system is not designed to recognize it?

Too often, the answer seems to be that they disappear into the pipeline.

They are assessed, scored, rejected, and ignored.

That is not inclusion. It is automated exclusion with a polished interface.

A hiring system cannot claim to be fair simply because it treats everyone the same at the surface level. Equal treatment is not the same as equitable access. A process that assumes every candidate can perform in the same way, under the same conditions, through the same modality, with the same speech patterns, is not neutral. It privileges the people who already match the system’s preferred format.

That is especially dangerous when the system does not explain itself.

If a human interviewer misunderstands a candidate, the candidate may be able to clarify. If a recruiter has concerns, there may be a conversation. If an accommodation is needed, there may be someone to contact. None of those safeguards are guaranteed, but at least they are recognizable parts of a human process.

In an AI-mediated process, the candidate may not know what was evaluated, how their response was summarized, whether the transcript was accurate, whether disability-related context was preserved, or whether a human ever looked at the substance of their answer.

That creates a serious accountability gap.

For candidates with disabilities, that gap is not theoretical. It shapes whether they can compete at all.

Recruiting systems should not treat speech fluency as a proxy for intelligence, professionalism, communication ability, or expertise. They should not treat transcript cleanliness as a proxy for competence. They should not treat speed, smoothness, confidence, or verbal polish as neutral indicators of merit.

Those signals are socially loaded. They are also disability-sensitive.

A person who stutters may be precise, thoughtful, experienced, and deeply competent while also repeating sounds or restarting phrases. A deaf candidate may need a different communication channel. A neurodivergent candidate may answer in a structure that differs from the expected pattern. A candidate with anxiety, trauma, fatigue, or chronic illness may not perform well under automated pressure, even when they can do the job.

If the system cannot account for that, then the system is not evaluating talent. It is evaluating conformity to its interface.

At minimum, AI-mediated hiring processes need clear accommodation pathways before the interview begins. Candidates should know how to request captions, typed input, extra time, alternative formats, human review, or other adjustments without fear that doing so will count against them.

They need meaningful human review when disability-related concerns are raised. A candidate should be able to say, “I have a stutter,” or “I need another communication format,” and receive an actual response.

They need transparency about what is being assessed. Is the system evaluating content? Verbal fluency? Confidence? Tone? Pace? Transcript quality? Communication style? Job-specific reasoning? Candidates deserve to know which signals matter, especially when those signals may interact with disability.

They need a way to challenge or clarify an assessment. If a transcript misrepresents a response, if an automated summary misses the substance, or if a disability-related factor may have distorted the evaluation, there should be a real mechanism for correction.

They need feedback. Not necessarily a full scoring rubric in every case, but enough information to know whether they were rejected because of qualifications, availability, role fit, test performance, communication style, or something else. Without feedback, there is no learning, no accountability, and no way to detect patterns of exclusion.

Most of all, these systems need to be designed with disabled candidates in mind from the beginning, not patched later after harm has occurred.

Accessibility cannot be an afterthought. It cannot be a buried email address nobody answers. It cannot be a checkbox in a process that still assumes one kind of body, one kind of voice, one kind of communication, and one kind of candidate.

I am not asking for special treatment. I am asking for a process that can distinguish between how someone speaks and what someone knows.

That distinction is basic fairness.

If an AI recruiter cannot understand that a stutter is not a lack of competence, then it should not be trusted to evaluate candidates who stutter. If it cannot provide alternative formats for people who are deaf or hard of hearing, then it should not be treated as an accessible hiring tool. If it cannot respond when a candidate raises an access concern, then it is not a neutral system. It is a gatekeeper without accountability.

Companies using AI recruitment tools need to ask harder questions before deploying them.

Who is this system likely to misunderstand?

What forms of disability were tested?

What kinds of speech does it handle poorly?

Does it preserve disability context in transcripts and summaries?

Can candidates request accommodation before assessment?

Can candidates appeal or clarify after assessment?

Does a human review edge cases, access concerns, and rejection patterns?

Are rejection rates being audited across disability-relevant signals?

What happens when the system gets it wrong?

These are not minor operational questions. They are the difference between a hiring process that expands opportunity and one that silently filters people out.

The future of hiring should not be a system where candidates speak into a machine, disclose an access need, receive no response, and disappear. That is not innovation. That is bureaucracy with less accountability.

AI recruiting may make hiring faster. It may make screening cheaper. It may make pipelines easier to manage. But if it cannot handle human variation, it is not making hiring fairer.

It is making exclusion harder to see.

And for disabled candidates, that invisibility has consequences.

It means not knowing whether your expertise was heard.

It means not knowing whether your speech was misunderstood.

It means not knowing whether the system treated your disability as noise, weakness, or failure.

It means being rejected without explanation by a process that may never have been built for you in the first place.

That should concern anyone who cares about fair hiring, disability rights, or responsible AI.

AI systems are increasingly being used to decide who gets seen, who gets advanced, and who gets opportunity. If those systems cannot respond to access needs, cannot explain their decisions, and cannot distinguish between disability and incompetence, then they should not be allowed to quietly stand between people and work.

If AI recruiters are going to evaluate human beings, they need to be able to handle human variation.

Otherwise, they are not modernizing hiring.

They are automating the old exclusions and making them easier to deny.
