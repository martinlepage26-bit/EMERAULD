If you are constantly aware that our smallest move is being shorted, measured, and evaluated against the actions of millions of other people, and then used to judge us in unpredictable ways, there is a risk that we would turn into quivering necrotic beings. Outward harmony, masking inner misery is hardly an ideal state. Every act of kindness ceases to be an act of kindness and instead becomes an act of personal score maximization, and is perceived as such by the recipient. Or worse, the very concept of a voluntary act of kindness gladly becomes just a fading memory of something people used to do. Supporters of mass surveillance might say, we don't subscribe to the point of view that it's acceptable to let some terrorist attacks happen in order to uphold individual right to privacy. 
Opponents to mass surveillance would say that bulk collection of data has a chilling effect on a free society. Surveillance is harmful because it can shield the exercise of our civil liberties, such as free speech or association. Surveillance can also have the effect of making us constrain ourselves. If we become highly aware that our actions are being recorded and stored, scrutinized, and evaluated potentially on a second-by-second basis. That's not unlikely. Consider anomaly detection. People would soon learn that unusual movements or clothing would draw attention from their algorithmic watchers. 
Imagine walking down the street with an old friend, reliving fun things from school, suddenly you realize that the cameras on the street posts above you are evaluating your every move, and you find yourself worrying that they're going to alert the authorities that your behavior is anomalous. Will surveillance not only be looking at what we're doing but also understand the meaning of details about our social interactions with others. If future AI will be able to guess who are lovers, who are friends, who are enemies, who are business associates, who are on a first date, that's a little creepy, isn't it? But is control always a bad thing? 
Think about the cell phone apps for tracking the coronavirus that were introduced in the spring of 2020. Different apps were launched in different countries, but the main principle was that the mobile phones communicate with other mobile phones nearby and alert users that have been close to someone carrying the coronavirus. If you were infected, you could choose to upload this information to the app. But when the system was introduced in Austria, people without the virus also reported that they were carrying the virus, thereby obstructing the functioning of the system. The self-reporting system was changed to only include cases in official health registers. Misuse is of course a risk. Other risks of integrity violation. 
Health information and other personal information can be very sensitive. If you store a lot of personal data during a crisis, what will happen to this data after the crisis has passed? Should it be deleted? Should it be saved in order to help with the next crisis? If it's saved, who should have access to it? Will a government that has expanded their surveillance and capacities in response to a crisis, give up those powers after the crisis has passed? Critics warn of a slippery slope that the crisis can make us accept privacy violations we wouldn't advise accept, and a tracking solutions and other constraints introduced during the crisis could become permanent.

Module 1
- What hit me most in this lesson is that AI is not some clean, neutral judge. Even when it looks logical, it still carries values and it can still carry prejudice, because it learns from human-made data and it operates inside goals that reward certain outcomes over others.
The hiring example made that feel concrete. If a system is trained on a company’s past recruitment decisions, it can “learn” that white men were hired more often and quietly treat that pattern as a sign of merit. The bias does not vanish. It gets converted into a technical rule, scaled up, and then hidden behind the comforting story that “the algorithm is objective.”
The criminal justice example pushed the point further. A risk score can label people of color as more likely to reoffend, not because they are inherently more dangerous, but because the underlying data reflects unequal policing and unequal conviction patterns. What bothered me is how easily correlation becomes a moral judgment. A statistical pattern turns into a decision about one person’s sentence, parole, or surveillance, even when the pattern may be measuring social inequality more than “criminality.”
The Google example added a final layer: bias can be designed in on purpose. Search and recommendation tools optimize for engagement and revenue, and personalization can trap people in information bubbles that reinforce what they already think. The lesson, for me, is that dealing with bias is not only a technical fix. It is value work: naming what a system is optimizing for, checking what it leaves out, and building real safeguards like audits, transparency, and meaningful ways to challenge decisions.

- The most important insight I gained from this lesson is that AI does not just make surveillance “bigger.” It changes the type of surveillance we live under. In the past, states and companies could collect mountains of data, but they could not reliably make sense of it at scale. AI shifts surveillance from storage to interpretation. Pattern recognition, face recognition, voice recognition, and video analytics turn cameras and databases into systems that can flag, sort, and judge people in near real time. That is the difference between “we keep this information just in case” and “we actively watch and act on it.”
A second insight is that surveillance is not only about catching criminals or preventing terrorism. It can become a tool for behavioral control. Once monitoring infrastructure exists, the purpose can expand. Today it is framed as safety or efficiency. Tomorrow it can slide toward policing dissent, targeting activists, pressuring conformity, or rewarding and punishing people in subtle ways. Even when the intention is not authoritarian, automated “anomaly detection” builds a norm into the system, then treats difference as suspicion. That creates obvious risks of discrimination, because “unusual” can easily map onto race, class, disability, gender expression, or simply being out of place.
The third insight is why privacy matters even if you feel you have nothing to hide. Privacy is not a shield for wrongdoing. Privacy is the space that makes ordinary freedom possible. If people expect that their searches, friendships, health data, and group memberships can be tracked, linked, and re-identified, they start editing themselves. They speak less freely, organize less confidently, and take fewer social risks. This is especially stark in self-help groups, where trust and anonymity are part of the therapy. The lesson, for me, is that the cost of mass surveillance is not only personal discomfort. The deeper cost is a quieter society that learns to behave for the camera.
