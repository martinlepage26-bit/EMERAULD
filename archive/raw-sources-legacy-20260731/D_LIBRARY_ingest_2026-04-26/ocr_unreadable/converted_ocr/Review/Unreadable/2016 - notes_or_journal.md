Unnatural Selection: Queer Method and Posthuman Commons in *Jurassic Park*

Introduction: Queer Paleontology and the Failure of Design

Unnatural Selection: Queer Method and Posthuman Commons in Jurassic Park
 *Anonymized for Review*

### **Introduction: The Park Is Closed, But the Franchise Is Open**

There is something uncanny about a franchise that begins with extinction and refuses to die. *Jurassic Park* was never simply about dinosaurs—it was about the seduction of control, the fantasy of reversing time, and the belief that science could be both master and savior. From its first frame, the film stages a paradox: the resurrection of the past through the most future-oriented means imaginable. “We spared no expense,” Hammond boasts, conjuring not only financial excess but ontological hubris. But if the original film invited us to marvel, the franchise that followed has rendered that marvel increasingly ambivalent, even grotesque.

This paper emerges not from a position of critical distance, but from entanglement. Like the scientists in *Jurassic Park*, we are not outside observers but implicated participants. Any effort to analyze the franchise must begin by acknowledging the impossibility of objectivity. As Haraway reminds us, “we are not in charge of the world” (2016, p. 3). The viewer, like the paleontologist, the geneticist, or the cloned child, is caught in a loop of desire and disavowal. We want the spectacle, even as we mourn its consequences. We want the dinosaurs to return, even as we fear what they expose.

This refusal of objectivity is not a flaw in the critical method, but its condition. Feminist epistemologies and queer methodologies teach us that knowledge is always partial, embodied, and affectively charged. Subjectivity is not a contaminant, but a location of insight. Braidotti describes this as the “posthuman convergence of the technological and the organic, the informational and the embodied” (2013, p. 190)—a convergence nowhere more visible than in a cloned raptor’s eye or a child born without parents.

The franchise’s own structure mirrors this shift from control to recursive disorientation. Each installment is both a sequel and a theoretical recursion, reanimating questions that were never settled. As Hu notes, “the dinosaurs’ presence is less about paleontology than about the ideologies we bury beneath it” (2015, p. 641). The park collapses. The fences fail. But the story persists. Its persistence is not narrative laziness—it is philosophical insistence.

Across six films, the narrative expands from a privatized island laboratory to a planetary ecology of entanglement, mutation, and improvisation. Its central figures—cloned dinosaurs, hybrid raptors, resurrected scientists, recombinant children—are not merely plot devices. They are speculative provocations, staging the limits of legal recognition, the failures of human exceptionalism, and the ethics of living with the unnatural. Jones and Otto (2016, p. 112) remind us that recognition is not ethical relation; “true posthuman ethics begins where legal language fails.”

This article traces that trajectory. I argue that the franchise’s most enduring theme is not extinction, but epistemic instability: the collapse of certainty, the rise of situated knowledge, and the emergence of a posthuman ethics grounded in relation rather than mastery. Drawing on chaos theory, queer kinship, and feminist science studies, I read the *Jurassic* films not as coherent cautionary tales, but as speculative philosophy. Their value lies in disruption, not didacticism.

The following sections explore this evolving terrain: from the illusion of control (*Jurassic Park*), to the proliferation of recombinant bodies (*Jurassic World*), to the limits of legal subjecthood (*Fallen Kingdom*), and finally, to the speculative commons imagined in *Dominion*. At each step, I foreground the entangled nature of inquiry, refusing the fantasy of neutrality in favor of accountable witnessing. To write about *Jurassic Park* is not to map its ruins from afar, but to feel their edges—to stand inside the wreckage and ask, not what went wrong, but what remains possible.

### **2. The Illusion of Control: Fences, Logic, and Enclosure**

In *Jurassic Park* (1993), control is not simply a function—it is a worldview. The electric fences, motion detectors, and sterilization protocols are less about safety than about belief: belief in the legibility of nature, the primacy of design, and the coherence of human mastery. John Hammond does not build a zoo—he builds a simulation. “We spared no expense,” he insists, as if financial excess could substitute for ethical doubt. His park is not a tribute to nature, but to the fantasy that nature can be parsed, parsed again, and monetized.

The true counterpoint to Hammond is not the dinosaurs, but Ian Malcolm. When he says “Life finds a way,” it is not poetic fatalism—it is the axiomatic warning of a chaos theorist. In Nils Ekeland’s foundational formulation of nonlinear systems, which Crichton cites as direct inspiration, “every prediction becomes meaningless when initial conditions cannot be exactly known” (Ekeland 1991, p. 63). Chaos is not randomness; it is patterned unpredictability, the precise form of resistance to Enlightenment reason. The dinosaurs do not escape the system—they reveal that no system ever held them.

This epistemic failure is not merely thematic—it is structural. As Donna Haraway has argued, “objectivity is not about disengagement but about being accountable to what we learn how to see” (Haraway 2016, p. 3). The park fails not because nature rebels, but because the designers deny their own positionality. Fences are meant to separate observer from observed, but they also insulate the knower from responsibility. The illusion of control depends on the fantasy of disembodied science.

By *The Lost World* (1997), the franchise turns inward. Site B, imagined as a more “natural” sanctuary, recapitulates the same logic of containment: fences, tranquilizers, profit motives. The dream of neutrality remains intact, even as it collapses again. The franchise does not repeat itself—it recurses, like a failed experiment run through increasingly chaotic conditions. What begins as techno-utopia curdles into surveillance ecology. By the time we arrive at *Jurassic World* (2015), control has evolved—not abandoned, but digitized.

Here, Claire Dearing’s language of “asset containment protocols” lays bare the algorithmic turn: dinosaurs are now data, their behavior fed into neural loops and behavioral prediction systems. Yet even this precision fails. The Indominus rex, a creature born of corporate strategy and military ambition, cannot be predicted because it has no precedent. It is a species without ecology, a being without origin, “engineered not to fit into any ecosystem, but to dominate all of them” (Hu 2015, p. 644). Its rampage is not a malfunction—it is the revelation of the system’s ontology.

Control, we see, is never neutral. It is gendered, racialized, and capitalized. The scientists of *Jurassic Park* fail not because they feel too much, but because they pretend not to feel at all. Sandra Harding’s concept of “strong objectivity” suggests that science becomes more rigorous, not less, when it acknowledges its positionality: “The view from nowhere is a myth; the view from a body is a method” (Harding 1992, p. 567).

Moments of resistance flicker at the edges of spectacle: Ellie Sattler touching triceratops dung with reverence, Alan Grant’s face softening in the presence of a Brachiosaurus, Ian Malcolm’s chaotic monologues framed like liturgies of doubt. These are not minor gestures. They are epistemic ruptures—brief acknowledgments that knowledge begins with awe, and awe is never sterile.

Ultimately, the illusion of control is not limited to the characters—it extends to the viewer. We come to the franchise wanting mastery: explanations, resolutions, facts. But the films refuse to comply. Like chaos theory itself, they “fracture predictability from the inside” (Ekeland 1991, p. 72). What is left is not order, but affect: wonder, dread, melancholy. Not data, but sensation. The lesson is not that science fails, but that science—like cinema—must feel its own limits.

### **3. Recombinant Bodies and Queer Kinship**

The *Jurassic* franchise has always been about bodies that don’t quite fit. From the moment a Velociraptor learns to open a door to the unveiling of a chameleon-skinned hybrid engineered to evade thermal detection, the films stage embodiment not as a given, but as a problem. These are not simply prehistoric animals, but recombinant beings—neither purely ancient nor purely artificial. They are the outcome of ambition and error, shaped as much by marketing departments as by genome sequencing. Their bodies are unstable, excessive, and affectively charged. And it is precisely in this excess that they become philosophical.

To call these creatures “hybrids” is insufficient. They are not simple chimeras but speculative organisms—composites of evolutionary myth, aesthetic desire, military fantasy, and cinematic affect. They are, as Haraway might say, “monsters in the most generative sense: figures that reveal the seams of nature and culture, fact and fiction, science and spectacle” (Haraway 1992, p. 299). They do not break from nature; they reveal that nature was always already constructed. In their presence, the boundary between design and deviation collapses.

This collapse is not incidental—it is the very condition of queerness. Here, queerness is not merely a matter of sexuality or orientation. It is a rupture in continuity, a refusal of biopolitical coherence, a disorientation of futurism and fixity. As Muñoz writes, “Queerness is not yet here… we must always be on the lookout for its arrival—it is an ideality” (Muñoz 2009, p. 25). The cloned dinosaurs, birthed without ancestry, without natural history, are its embodiment. They are figures not of reproductive continuity, but of recombinant possibility—creatures whose very existence dislodges the taxonomies they are named within.

This speculative logic extends to human cloning. In *Jurassic World: Fallen Kingdom* (2018), we meet Maisie Lockwood—a girl cloned not for spectacle, but as a gesture of grief. Her body is not a miracle of science, but a wound. She is not a metaphor for technology gone too far; she is a child without a birthright, a subject formed in liminality. The film refuses to settle the question of her status: is she a daughter? A copy? A ghost? Instead, it holds the ambiguity. As Jones and Otto suggest, posthuman ethics “requires not just extending rights to others, but questioning the structures through which recognition becomes possible at all” (2016, p. 118). Maisie is unrecognizable by legal standards, but deeply recognizable to those she resembles: the dinosaurs.

Her decision to release the dinosaurs at the film’s climax is not framed as rebellion—it is framed as kinship. Recognition is extended not through species boundaries, but through shared recombinant origin. This is what Haraway means when she calls for us to “make kin, not babies” (Haraway 2016, p. 102). Maisie is not asserting dominance or identity. She is practicing an ethics of relation rooted in shared ontological instability.

This moment also explodes the myth of neutrality. There is no impartial position from which to judge Maisie’s act. The camera, the scientists, the viewer—we are all complicit. “Objectivity,” Haraway insists, “is about limited location and situated knowledge, not transcendence” (1988, pp. 581–583). Maisie’s knowledge is not scientific in the disembodied sense—it is born of captivity, of experiment, of shared trauma. Her perspective is not pure—it is painfully earned.

These recombinant kinships ripple outward. Blue the raptor mourns her sibling. Owen and Claire form an unorthodox triad with a child who is neither theirs nor entirely human. These are not families in the normative sense. They are affective assemblages, constellations formed through rupture, improvisation, and care. They enact what Braidotti calls “a posthuman convergence… grounded in relational ethics and embodied vulnerability” (Braidotti 2013, p. 190).

The *Jurassic* films do not offer resolutions—they offer rehearsals. Rehearsals for living with the unnatural, the unruly, the undesigned. Their speculative value lies not in their predictions, but in their provocations. What does it mean to care for a clone? To grieve a genetically engineered animal? To parent without lineage? These questions exceed narrative. They demand something of us—something intimate, messy, and unresolved.

In this way, the franchise queers not only bodies, but ethics. It offers a philosophy of life beyond categorization. Not cautionary tales, but invitations to cohabit with the monsters we made—and, perhaps, to recognize the monster in ourselves.

### **4. Juridical Limits and the Ethics of Recognition**

As the *Jurassic* franchise evolves, its ethical terrain sharpens. No longer content with visual spectacle or the thematic failures of containment, *Fallen Kingdom* (2018) pivots explicitly toward law: can dinosaurs be rescued from Isla Nublar? Should they be? A Senate hearing stages the question as if it were about resource allocation. But it isn’t. It’s about classification—and the violent exclusions built into systems that presume their own neutrality.

The hearing scene dramatizes the core crisis: are these creatures property, or persons? Do they deserve rescue, or is their very existence an existential error? The debate is less scientific than ontological. As Attwood argues, these films are uniquely suited for exploring bioethical questions because they collapse the boundaries between experiment, experience, and emotion: “The constructedness of the dinosaurs and their hybridity destabilize the very terms upon which ethics are decided” (Attwood 2008, p. 278). The Senate’s failure to act is not indecision. It is an act of legal cowardice.

At stake is the coherence of Western legal thought, which depends on binary distinctions: human versus animal, evolved versus created, subject versus object. The dinosaurs rupture every category. They are engineered yet sentient, brutal yet protective. They bond, grieve, remember. As Hu explains, “they are creatures of deep affect, but they are denied subjecthood because they fail to reproduce the familiar contours of human language and law” (Hu 2015, p. 645). Recognition, in this regime, is a gatekeeping operation. If the law cannot name you, it cannot protect you.

Maisie Lockwood exposes this logic even further. As a human clone, she is the legal system’s nightmare—technically human, but originless in a juridical sense. She is both child and artifact, kin and property. When she releases the dinosaurs at the film’s climax, it is not rebellion. It is jurisprudence. She enacts a form of care that exceeds the law, one rooted not in recognition but in relation. “They’re alive,” she says, “like me.” With this sentence, she dissolves the ontological scaffolding upon which the law depends.

Jones and Otto (2016, pp. 118–121) argue that posthuman feminist legal theory must go beyond liberal inclusion. It must interrogate the epistemological violence of recognition itself. “True ethical relation,” they write, “begins where legal language fails—where beings defy the criteria of voice, reason, or species boundary and still demand care.” *Fallen Kingdom* stages that failure with agonizing clarity. The dinosaurs are not heard. They are felt. Their worth is not argued. It is grieved.

Subjective inquiry becomes not a contaminant but a necessity. The court’s posture of objectivity is pure theater. As Haraway writes, “Responsibility requires response-ability… not the false hope of transcendence but the risky practice of staying with the trouble” (Haraway 2016, p. 34). There is no neutral position from which to legislate life. Every frame of the film implicates us: in Blue’s hesitation, in Maisie’s eyes as she watches a dinosaur suffocate behind glass, in Owen’s whisper to a dying creature. These are not scenes of moral instruction. They are confrontations with the affective terrain that law excludes.

Even science is not spared. Dr. Wu invokes intellectual property law to defend his synthetic monsters. The Indoraptor is patented, not born. And yet, it learns. It hesitates. It toys with its prey, not as instinct, but as performative curiosity. The creature exceeds its blueprint. It becomes legible only as affect—what Rosi Braidotti might call “a body whose boundaries are defined by flows of power, not static morphology” (Braidotti 2013, p. 89).

We are left, then, not with answers, but with the wreckage of epistemological certainty. The law cannot adapt fast enough. Science cannot control what it creates. The viewer cannot remain untouched. The film does not ask us to solve this—it asks us to stay in it. To witness without mastering. To recognize without assimilating. To care without claiming to know.

This is not merely a crisis of recognition. It is the ethical challenge of our time: to make kin with the unrecognizable, to cohabit with the beings we cannot classify, and to remain accountable in the absence of legal clarity. The park is gone. The fence is down. What remains is a call not to law, but to relation.

### **5. Broken Fences, Open Worlds: Toward a Posthuman Commons**

By the conclusion of *Jurassic World: Dominion* (2022), the central conceit of the franchise has shattered. There is no longer a park, no island, no fence. The very idea of containment—so central to the original film’s fantasy of scientific mastery—has failed spectacularly. Dinosaurs now populate forests, oceans, mountains, cities. They co-exist with whales and elephants, collide with traffic, nest atop skyscrapers. The spectacle of bioengineered life has outgrown its enclosures. We are no longer watching an experiment—we are living in its aftermath.

This narrative rupture is not a mere escalation. It is ontological. It signifies a shift from containment to entanglement, from taxonomy to entropic proximity. No longer exceptional, human life becomes one thread in a thick ecological mesh. The fences, both literal and epistemic, have fallen. As Donna Haraway insists, “It matters what stories tell stories” (Haraway 2016, p. 35). The *Jurassic* story no longer tells one of mastery—it tells of survivance, ferality, and kinship without clarity.

The chaotic dynamics once theorized abstractly by Ian Malcolm now saturate the narrative world. Ekeland’s chaos theory warned us: “every system, no matter how controlled, is sensitive to initial conditions and eventually unpredictable” (Ekeland 1991, p. 63). The mosquito in amber was never neutral. Dominion’s world emerges from that moment—not as culmination, but as ecological unraveling. This is not apocalypse, nor utopia. It is the muddled, recursive middle—what Haraway calls “staying with the trouble” (2016, p. 1). The question is not how to restore order, but how to inhabit disarray ethically.

The film refuses the logic of closure. Instead of a climactic showdown, it offers montage: a Mosasaur beneath an oil rig, Pteranodons atop glass towers, a raptor crossing snow beside an elephant. These are not reconciliatory images. They are scenes of tense proximity—uneasy, sublime, and unresolved. Coexistence here is not peace, but contact. Not harmony, but recognition without assimilation. This is the condition of the posthuman commons.

Rosi Braidotti articulates this as “an ontological shift grounded in relationality, vulnerability, and embeddedness” (2013, p. 190). The posthuman is not defined by AI or cybernetics—it is the recognition that control is gone, that life is improvised, and that survival is collaborative. In *Dominion*, this appears not in dialogue but in affect: Owen returning Beta to Blue, Maisie choosing proximity over power, the collapsing biotech facility overtaken by forest. These gestures are not symbolic—they are affective rehearsals for living otherwise.

Importantly, the film’s original heroes—Malcolm, Sattler, Grant—return not to fix the world but to witness its transformation. Their scientific certainty is now obsolete. They are spectral figures, haunted by the failure of the very methods they once championed. Their presence offers not comfort, but a reminder that knowledge, like life, must evolve. They do not restore control—they endure its loss.

This collapse extends to subjectivity. Who narrates this world? Who defines value? Who draws the line between natural and unnatural, native and invasive? Objectivity is impossible when your species is no longer at the center. Haraway reminds us that “situated knowledge is not a flaw; it is the only condition under which responsibility becomes possible” (1988, pp. 581–583). Analysis, here, must be felt. The collapse of epistemic distance is not a crisis—it is a call.

Anna Tsing names this condition a “feral commons”—a space where human and nonhuman lives intersect in contingent, unstable ways (Tsing 2015, p. 152). The dinosaurs are not a plague. They are kin of another kind: co-residents of a world disordered by hubris and stitched together by care, mistake, and improvisation. The commons is not shared property—it is shared precarity. It is the awkward, necessary coexistence of beings never meant to live together, yet doing so all the same.

Even the film seems to know it has written itself past redemption. It doesn’t pretend to offer closure. What it gives us instead is affective scaffolding: gestures of survival, scenes of ambiguous solidarity. It gestures toward a future it cannot name. A world beyond franchise logic. Beyond scientific omniscience. A world where living with what was never meant to return becomes not an act of containment, but of response.

In this broken world, we find a new epistemology. Not one of facts and certainty, but of implication, entanglement, and story. The fences are down. The protocols have failed. We are inside the enclosure now—with no way out, and no center to speak from. All that remains is the dangerous possibility that care can survive collapse. That knowledge, stripped of objectivity, might become something else: intimate, implicated, and alive.

### **Conclusion: Objectivity Ends at the Fence (Expanded)**

Across its three decades, the *Jurassic Park* franchise has staged more than the spectacular failure of technoscience. It has become a recursive philosophical inquiry into what follows design: what becomes of the world—and the human—when systems fail, taxonomies blur, and control evaporates. From the pristine illusions of Hammond’s original park to the decentralized, multispecies ecologies of *Dominion* (2022), the films dramatize the ontological instability of life once engineered, the affective ambiguity of relation without mastery, and the ethical challenges of cohabitation in a post-enclosure age.

But what makes this inquiry more than allegory—what makes it philosophical in a radical sense—is its refusal to secure the position of the observer. If scientific thought traditionally imagines itself from a distance—above the experiment, detached from the phenomena—*Jurassic Park* collapses this distinction. The scientist is inside the enclosure. The chaos theorist is in the jeep. The biologist flees her own creation. This embeddedness is not incidental; it is the narrative structure’s epistemic condition. The viewer, too, becomes complicit—not simply watching but reacting, flinching, sweating, fearing. Here, cinema does not illustrate a theory; it inhabits the collapse of objectivity from within.

This recursive staging of epistemic collapse plays out across multiple films, but it is most visceral in the jeep attack scene from the original *Jurassic Park*. When the T. rex emerges, the fence has already failed. The technology meant to restrain nature flickers out, and what follows is not a testable hypothesis but a visceral panic. Malcolm’s ironic detachment vanishes; Grant and the children become avatars for an audience unprepared for proximity. The scene is not only a moment of suspense—it is a lesson in affective knowledge. The audience does not learn *about* dinosaurs—they learn *with* their bodies what it means to be unprotected, overexposed, and abandoned by the systems meant to secure them.

This is where the franchise aligns with feminist and posthuman critiques of science that challenge the fantasy of neutral knowledge. As Haraway insists, “objectivity” is never from nowhere—it is always situated, partial, embodied (Haraway 1988, 581). The chaos of *Jurassic Park* is not random; it is patterned disruption, a reminder that control is itself a myth with a long colonial pedigree. What Crichton scripts as “chaos theory” becomes in cinematic form a phenomenology of failure, felt in flesh and screams. The violence of the park is not the dinosaur’s violence—it is the violence of pretending to know without consequences.

The affective resonance of these failures accumulates across the franchise, reaching a climax in *Dominion*’s gesture toward a “world without fences.” Here, the question is no longer whether control will fail, but how to live when it already has. This is not an apocalypse. It is a shift in epistemology. *Dominion* imagines an ecology where cohabitation is not an ideal but a given—messy, dangerous, incomplete. We see a Mosasaur swimming beside whales, a raptor moving through a snowy forest, a child releasing a cloned raptor into the wild. These are not narrative resolutions. They are experiments in relationality, affective glimpses into life without enclosure.

This vision echoes Anna Tsing’s articulation of “life in the ruins,” where survival is not grounded in restoration but in improvisation, in encounter (Tsing 2015, 4). *Dominion*’s ethics are feral. They resist resolution. Instead of offering catharsis or closure, the film lingers in scenes of unease—Blue watching Owen from the trees, not attacking, not returning, just observing. The franchise shifts its moral compass away from victory or defeat toward mutual recognition without guarantees. This, too, is a mode of knowledge—not rational, not extractive, but responsive, situated in presence, not prediction.

In this shifting terrain, cinema becomes not just a mirror of scientific failure but a method of epistemic rupture. The recursion of the franchise—its sequels, reboots, and legacy characters—is not a sign of creative exhaustion. It is a formal acknowledgment that the same questions return, refracted through different ethical and political conditions. There are no final answers because the conditions of inquiry keep shifting. The return of Malcolm, Grant, and Sattler in *Dominion* is not nostalgic closure but a recursive staging of epistemological failure. Their expertise is no longer predictive—it is anecdotal, fragile, haunted. They are not architects of order, but witnesses to its collapse.

These “ghosts in the system” mark a turning point in the franchise’s self-reflexivity. No longer are these characters the experts they once were. They are no longer called upon to restore order but to testify to its impossibility. They move through the ruins of their own designs, haunted by the knowledge they once wielded with confidence. This spectral return reinforces the franchise’s core philosophical claim: that mastery is a myth, and that survival requires the surrender of certainty. As Braidotti notes, “the posthuman condition urges us to think critically and creatively about who and what we are actually in the process of becoming” (Braidotti 2013, 12). *Jurassic Park* no longer asks what humans can create. It asks how they can live with what they’ve created.

And the story is not over. A new installment is already in production, slated for 2026. Its title has yet to be revealed, but the terrain it inherits is already mapped: a world without fences, without blueprints, without guarantees. The franchise has written itself into a paradox: how to make a sequel when the very logic of sequels—containment, return, resolution—has been dismantled. What remains is not narrative closure but epistemic openness, a kind of radical exposure to what comes next.

This is the franchise’s most unsettling and perhaps most urgent proposition: that knowledge itself must be rethought as a practice of vulnerability. The cloned girl and the cloned raptor do not reconcile species; they unsettle them. Their kinship is not symbolic—it is ontological. They do not know each other through categories but through gesture, breath, proximity. This is a speculative ethic, one that begins not in recognition, but in cohabitation. The lesson is not that we can fix the past, but that we must learn to survive with its living remnants. Not to control, but to remain accountable—to the ruins, to each other, to what was never meant to return but did anyway.
