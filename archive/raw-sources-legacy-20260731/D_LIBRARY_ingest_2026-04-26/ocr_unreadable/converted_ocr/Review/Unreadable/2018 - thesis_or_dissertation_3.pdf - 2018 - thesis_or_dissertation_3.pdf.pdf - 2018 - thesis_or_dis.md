Montreal, March 6th, 2018

Laverne Smith & Associates Inc.
Allan Slaight Chair for the Study of the Conjuring Arts
Faculty of Arts and Social Sciences
Carleton University
Ottawa, ON

Dear Dr. Laverne Smith,
I would like to be considered for the current open position within the Allan Slaight Chair
for the Study of the Conjuring Arts at Carleton University. I recently received a Ph.D. in
Religious studies at Université du Québec à Montréal, and I am convinced that my
research and teaching experience, as well as my interest in the different aspects of
academia have adequately prepared me for this position. In fact, I believe that my
expertise in contemporary religiosities surrounding magic, witchcraft, and what has been
dubbed the conjuring arts could uniquely and rigorously help bring to life this exciting
academic endeavor.
Doctoral project and research experiences
My doctoral dissertation is a study of identity negotiations in the Montreal Pagan
community, regarding gender and sexual orientation, and practices of witchcraft and
magic. Much of the research on the topic suggests that contemporary Paganism and
Wicca, or Modern Witchcraft, offer a very inclusive milieu where LGBTQ Pagans can
express the specificity of their identities through collective and individual practices and
performances whose gendered meanings and relations can be modified at will. But I
demonstrate, contrary to popular belief, that these religiosities still reproduce normativity,
and that identity and spirituality are more easily aligned when the individual is detached,
at least for a time, from the community.
To complete this research, I did an extensive exploration of the diverse relationships
between religion and sexuality, as they can be normative, as well as loci of agency.
1

Because certain issues around identity and power arose in my research, I also worked on
discourses around sexuality, marginalization, religious persecution, the history of the
Women’s and LGBTQ movements, and different approaches to gender and sex in society.
For the most part, my approach to research is influenced by queer feminist studies,
religious studies, cultural studies, and socio-anthropology of religion. I have explored
and successfully applied the use of the life narrative methodology with participant
observation, in order to contrast different power dynamics surrounding gender and
magical practices. Moreover, I have articulated a broadly useful queer perspective
allowing the analysis of power relations, within individual and collective experiences,
between identity and authenticity, belonging and social recognition.
I have spent six years doing fieldwork in the Pagan community surrounding Montreal
and some festival locations in Ontario. I have traveled to Toronto, Ottawa, and Quebec
City, interviewed experts on the topics of Paganism, magic and gender from Canadian,
American, and British universities, and created a diversified and comprehensive original
dataset from ethnographies and LGBTQ informants’ interviews. In addition, I have a
previous background in literary studies, and a basic training on Quebec’s Ethics and
Religious Culture curriculum. I have also served as a research assistant for projects on
sexuality, ageing and masculinities, on gendered school rites, on youth and sexuality in
Quebec, on marginalized populations in France, and on religious diversity in Montreal.
Although I have been focused on my graduate research for several years, I have been
actively involved in conversations with students and scholars from many universities
since 2011; through organization and participation in our annual Young researchers
conference; from Université de Montréal, with Groupe de Recherche Diversité Urbaine
(GRDU); from Université Laval, having been a part of conferences within ACFAS and
others; and from around the world, contributing to the Religious Studies Project, a
project sponsored by the British Association for the Study of Religion (BASR); to the
online network of researchers specialized in Pagan studies; with the International Society
for the Study of Religion (ISSR), and the American Academy of Religion (AAR). I have
also discussed my works in many different scientific peer-reviewed conferences. My
works were also published, both in French and in English, often after contributing to
such conferences, leading to four book chapters and peer-reviewed articles at Presses de
l’Université Laval, Routledge, Sciences religieuses/Studies in religion, Anthropologica,
Religiologiques, among others. Finally, I am currently working on the editing and future
publication of my doctoral dissertation as a monograph.
Teaching interests and philosophy
After completing a master’s degree in Literary studies, I entered the field of religious
studies because I wanted to work closer to those interested, at different levels, in the
2

complexity of the way culture and religion intersect. Scholars and religiously involved
people have different things to say about very diverse kinds of beliefs and practices. In
crossing over fields of studies, I needed to figure out, and very fast, how students were
taught fundamental concepts about those very omnipresent issues of life without them
thinking that these were religious truths. As such, I was wildly interested in the many
ways in which different narratives are constu(ct)ed in diverse contexts (religion, science,
magic, gender, education, identity) around objects of knowledge and power. It is a very
amazing thing to talk about religion as an aspect of culture, and it is a tremendous
challenge to bring students to that place where they can detach themselves from their
faith and collaboratively think like mature students and even future scholars.
My passion for learning is coupled with the desire to pass on what I have learned. In fact,
I have spoken and served as a teaching assistant in very diverse courses about religion
and sexuality, homosexuality and gendered identities, rituals, culture and Western
society, as well as Quebec culture, more specifically. It is a matter of pride for me to
teach, in a very short and effective fashion, what and how I have learned. University
courses and other speaking opportunities, during which it was crucial to communicate
social-scientific theories, methodologies, and necessary information in a short amount of
time, have allowed me to put this into practice using interactive methods and lively
discussions with and among the audience.
Something else came into this willful need to communicate knowledge while making
sure I was understood. Indeed, ever since I could talk, I always had a stutter. More
pronounced in my younger years, it has now become a very important element for me to
deal with whenever I face an audience, whether composed of students or scholars. It is
important because it allows me to be honest with the audience, as struggles may occur
during communication. Was I understood correctly? What can I do to make sure I was? I
found out with experience that it is always best to see students as part of the
communicative process. Certainly they are the ones being tested, but the exercise is also
mine, and, as such, it is my duty to make sure that every aspect of my classes engages
everyone in a learning relationship based on reciprocity. I also found out it was often
easier for me to communicate in English, as French pronunciation can sometimes be a bit
treacherous. That being said, I cherish my ability to navigate between two languages, as
I’m sure many students and scholars in Ottawa often have to do. It is also part of what
allows me to understand and adjust to different kinds of audience and levels of expertise.
For all of those reasons, I am continuously interested in looking into and talking about
issues of normativity, perception, interpretation, and representation. My doctoral thesis
led me to question whether or not certain topics deserved to be taught in a certain way,
and others in other ways. In fact, my goal is to bring students to ask questions like “How
can we speak of something without creating a norm?”, “How have our scientific
3

categories influenced the way we talk about certain matters in our schools today?”, or
“How do I take part in the stereotypes surrounding these important matters?”. Culture, as
well as magic, should not only be seen as mere entertainment, but mostly as a reflection
of our everyday “mundane” life, as a lens with which scholars and students can
understand the relationships between their knowledge and the world outside academia.
In sum, I always aim to develop pedagogical strategies dedicated to teaching several
principles from diverse fields of studies in a dynamic and concrete way. I am also
convinced that these strategies will equally help students in other areas of academia and
in a very day-to-day manner, inasmuch as they learn to encounter new experiences with
open eyes and critical but respectful words.
Vision for the programme and centre
My vision for the Programme and Centre for the Study of the Conjuring Arts commands
for a rigorous effort at bridging gaps between disciplines in social sciences and the
Humanities. As magic is deeply rooted in issues of power regarding knowledge, its
representation and transmission, the study of the conjuring arts requires a very broad
perspective allowing them to be put into a cohesive and comprehensive rationale. Magic
has always been a cultural phenomenon that resists reliable and all-encompassing
categorization. In terms of performance, it exists mostly in the margins, within that
which escapes the gaze of authority, conformity and expectations. Elusive as it can be,
magic always takes on the spirit of those who use it and those who are defined by it. As
such, it would be of interest to the instructors and researchers within the Programme and
Centre to always combine discourse and practice during their examination of a specific
topic, not as opposites, but as relational aspects of sociocultural life. For instance,
courses on the history of magic and witchcraft could not be separate from an exploration
of the material culture during a certain place and period of time; anthropological and
sociological approaches, taught at all levels, would include classical works on magic and
witchcraft, as well as more recent works, and even attendance to current practices or
performances, as they are very much present in Canada and in Ontario; and
psychological approaches would, much in the same way, always be interrogated in
regards to artistic, cinematographic, and literary productions in ways that appeal to both
methodology and theory.
For those reasons, it would be particularly important to create strong connections not
only with academics across the world who are interested in the conjuring arts, magic, and
witchcraft, but also with practitioners in Canada, the United States, and Europe who
identify as such, regardless of their culture, affiliations, or social positionings. The
Center would thus be the first in Canadian history to network internationally between
many scholars of magic, religion, and witchcraft from numerous disciplines. The
4

creation of an annual conference will also call on the expertise of prominent scholars in
the study of magic and witchcraft, its psychological, historical, and archaeological
aspects, those related to secular and non-religious definitions and practices of magic,
ritual performances and magical religiosities, contemporary Paganisms, Western
esotericism, Theosophy and Hermeticism, shamanism, neo-shamanism, as well as
Indigenous and First Nations practices and worldviews, among others. The Center would
also support scholars and students working with this approach, and many publications on
these themes already come to mind. They would be more than likely to see the light of
day as rapidly as scholars in those areas, from Canada and more, come together in
Ottawa or Toronto.
It can appear quite cavalier for a young scholar such as myself, who just finished their
doctoral studies, to aim for such an important position as Chair. Although this position
certainly calls for a more experienced and seasoned academic, I am greatly enthusiastic
about the possibilities, whether related to research or not, the Allan Slaight Chair for the
Study of the Conjuring Arts at Carleton University can offer. Furthermore, my previous
research has focused on contemporary Pagan rituals, but my expertise on the many facets
of magic reaches well beyond this specific context. I indeed encourage alternatives in
teaching and research when it comes to putting forward objects, like magic, that have
been neglected and looked down at. It is clear that this new chair will finally provide a
grand platform for these alternatives to take place. It would very much be a pleasure and
a passion to actively participate, any way I can, in making them a reality, and to work in
a stimulating community such as Carleton University.
Be assured that I will be happy to meet with you any time you deem appropriate, and to
answer your questions about my academic path and my professional endeavors.

Thank you greatly for your consideration,
