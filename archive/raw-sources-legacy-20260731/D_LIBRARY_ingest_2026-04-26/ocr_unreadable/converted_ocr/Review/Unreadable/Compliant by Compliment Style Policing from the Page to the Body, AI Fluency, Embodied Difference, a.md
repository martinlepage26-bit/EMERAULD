## **Compliant by Compliment: Style Policing from the Page to the Body, AI Fluency, Embodied Difference, and the Price of Legitimacy**

### **Abstract**

This paper merges two lines of inquiry: an experimental study of AI-assisted scholarly authorship and a critical autoethnography of institutional exclusion. In the first, I trained a generative AI model on my own research corpus, ethnographic data, theoretical frameworks, annotated bibliographies, and stylistic patterns, to produce full-length, peer-reviewable manuscripts. Submitted without disclosure of machinic mediation, these manuscripts were evaluated alongside my unaided work. In the second, I document my trajectory as a queer, neurodivergent, stuttering scholar whose work on contemporary Paganism and queer ritual practice was tolerated for its diversity value but strategically contained through the denial of authority, stability, and recognition.

The experiment revealed that peer review rewards the performance of fluency over the origin of ideas, granting legitimacy to AI-mediated texts so long as they conformed to disciplinary aesthetics. The autoethnographic account traced how the same aesthetic norms operated in hiring, teaching opportunities, and publication, where failure, or refusal, to perform fluency was punished. Together, these strands illuminate the shared infrastructure linking late capitalism, ableism, and heteronormativity in the governance of academic legitimacy.

By framing AI as an epistemic prosthesis rather than a threat, and by refusing the institutional pact that trades safety for assimilation, this paper argues for fugitive authorship: scholarship that remains alive, embodied, and resistant to the embalming of dead knowledge.

**Keywords:** prosthetic authorship, critical autoethnography, academic ableism, queer method, symbolic containment

### **Introduction**

The work of the scholar is supposed to be visible in the text they produce: the labour of thinking, researching, and writing crystallised into a coherent, self-contained object that can be evaluated on its own terms. This is the fiction on which much of contemporary academia rests, that the written artifact can stand as proof of intellectual merit, separate from the conditions of its making. The reality is less tidy. Scholarly legitimacy is not simply conferred through the content of ideas, but through their adherence to the stylistic, aesthetic, and affective norms of the discipline. These norms, despite being presented as neutral, are designed for and by particular kinds of bodies and minds: normatively abled, fluently articulate, emotionally controlled, and institutionally anchored.

This paper emerges from the friction between that reality and my own position as a queer, neurodivergent, stuttering scholar with no institutional affiliation. I hold a PhD. I do not hold a university post. My research on contemporary Paganism and queer ritual practice has been cited, invited into special issues, and praised for its originality, and it has also been sidelined, publicly rebuked, and denied the material conditions that would allow it to endure. I have lived the paradox of being visible without being recognised, credentialed without being entrusted, welcomed only so long as my presence remains decoratively diverse and structurally harmless.

This is not a story of misunderstanding, but of how institutions manage difference: they invite it in rhetoric, but contain it in practice. The episodes I recount here, in manuscripts, meetings, classrooms, and conference halls, are not aberrations, but examples of an ordinary logic at work. They reveal how the aesthetic of legitimacy is policed not only through what is said, but through how it is delivered. In my own case, the same arguments received radically different receptions depending on whether they arrived in my own cadence or through AI-assisted smoothing into disciplinary prose.

The project that follows draws its evidence from two entwined sites. The first is a methodological experiment: to test whether generative artificial intelligence can act as an epistemic prosthesis, translating my research and voice into the formal register of academic prose in ways that circumvent the communicational ableism embedded in scholarly writing norms. The second is a critical autoethnography of institutional betrayal, a tracing of the specific moments, gestures, and silences through which my legitimacy has been curtailed, contained, or withdrawn.

The AI experiment involved training a language model on my own intellectual corpus: ethnographic field notes, theoretical frameworks, annotated bibliographies, and stylistic samples from published and unpublished work. I supplied it with outlines, key concepts, and citations. The model returned drafts that I then edited line by line, checking accuracy, refining arguments, adding nuance. These were not AI-generated ideas; they were my ideas, mediated through a machinic collaborator whose capacity for fluent prose outstripped my own ability to consistently produce such language under the constraints of time, energy, and the embodied tempo of my speech.

The results were telling. Submitted without disclosure of machinic mediation, these manuscripts passed peer review with ease. Reviewers praised their clarity, sophistication, and argumentative structure, the very qualities whose absence, in my unaided voice, had been read as disqualifying in teaching interviews, departmental meetings, and editorial exchanges. The experiment thus became a mirror: it reflected back to me how much of academic legitimacy depends not on the vitality or originality of ideas, but on the ability to present them in a form that meets unspoken but rigid stylistic codes.

The autoethnographic strand documents the cost of those codes. It begins with an apparently innocuous lunch invitation from the director of my department, which became the occasion for an unspoken audition I did not know I was giving. It also includes the sharp dismissal I received when I asked a senior professor whether I might teach a course in my field. It recounts as well the footnote that appeared beneath my article in my own department’s journal, acting as a published disclaimer of legitimacy, printed alongside my work.

These stories are not detours from the argument, they are its core. They are evidence of what I call symbolic containment: the selective inclusion of marginalised scholars in ways that are visible enough to serve the institution’s image, but limited enough to prevent structural change. They are proof that the suspicion directed toward AI-assisted authorship is of the same species as the suspicion cast on queer, disabled, and otherwise unruly scholarship: both disrupt the fiction of the scholar as a solitary, self-sufficient, normatively embodied producer of text.

To name this connection is to shift the question from *Can AI undermine scholarly legitimacy?* to *What already undermines it, and for whom?* It is also to position this paper not as an abstract meditation on academic norms, but as an act of refusal. I am no longer inside the structures that once claimed the right to determine my intellectual worth. Without the bureaucratic tether of a department, I am free to think at the pace of my own methods, to let my stutter shape the rhythm of my writing, to feed my thought on the world rather than on the menu of institutionally sanctioned ideas. This works against the collectively demonstrated idea that both in academia and popular culture, stuttering is often misperceived as a sign of reduced intelligence or competence. This bias arises from both direct judgments (e.g., listener perceptions), systemic stereotyping (e.g., professors’ expectations), and cultural tropes (e.g., film representations) (Evans & Williams, 2015; Werle-Byrd, 2021; Amick et al. (2017)).

This is the only objectivity I recognise: one grounded in situated experience, reflexive awareness, and the refusal to confuse legibility with truth. The rest, rankings, metrics, impact factors, is an illusion of control over who decides what counts as knowledge. As a scholar in religious studies who also identifies as a witch, I deny that control. My refusal is not metaphorical; it is embodied. They kept me quiet once. I now speak, stutters, repetitions, and all, because even interrupted speech can break the seal of consensus.

In what follows, I braid together the AI authorship experiment and the autoethnographic account, moving between empirical findings, theoretical framing, and narrative scene. I argue that the real threat to scholarly legitimacy is not the presence of AI, but the persistence of an infrastructure that mistakes aesthetic conformity for rigor and compliance for merit. This paper claims space for fugitive authorship, scholarship that resists embalming, that carries its living pulse into circulation, that refuses to be made safe.

### **Literature Review: Writing, Embodiment, and the Epistemic Conditions of Legitimacy**

The academic article is often framed as a transparent container for knowledge, a neutral form into which ideas are poured and judged on their intellectual merit alone. This framing conceals the degree to which the form itself is political, the product of centuries of disciplinary consolidation and tacit consensus about how knowledge should sound, how it should be sequenced, and what affective register it should inhabit. To write in a way that feels “naturally” academic is to have internalised these norms—or, failing that, to have access to collaborators, editors, or tools that can reproduce them with convincing fluency. In situating both the AI-mediated authorship experiment and the autoethnographic strand of this project, four intersecting bodies of literature—academic ableism, symbolic containment, posthuman authorship, and crip temporality—reveal how legitimacy is constructed through aesthetic conformity and how the same criteria that gatekeep against AI-assisted writing also discipline embodied difference.

Jay Dolmage’s account of academic ableism insists that ableism is not a sporadic flaw in higher education but “its most enduring feature” (2017, 7), embedded in everything from the placement of ramps to the pace of committee meetings. Margaret Price extends this to the discursive sphere, describing the “kairotic space” of academia as dominated by unmarked norms of rationality, speed, and fluency—values “naturalised as universal” (2011, 27). Jonathan Sterne names this communicational ableism, in which speed, smoothness, and emotional neutrality are treated as inherent signs of credibility (2011, 340), a process echoed in Sara Ahmed’s claim that “the institution gives recognition only to those who do not cause trouble” (2012, 143). Disfluency is trouble; trouble is disciplined out of sight. Price and Sterne together make visible how the “proper” academic voice functions as an exclusionary filter, a point anchored by Dolmage’s emphasis on the physical and procedural infrastructures that enforce it.

Sara Ahmed’s concept of “non-performativity” (2012, 117) and Sirma Bilge’s critique of “palatable diversity” (2013, 410) shift the focus from pace and fluency to the aesthetic and affective management of difference. Institutions can celebrate diversity while ensuring it conforms to dominant norms, tolerating marginalised scholars long enough to benefit from their presence but not long enough to cede control over the terms of engagement. Such symbolic containment can take the form of overt rebuke, procedural delay, or the inclusion of disclaimers that undermine credibility. In all cases, the message is consistent: you may speak here, but only in the register we approve.

Posthuman theory complicates these dynamics by dismantling the fiction of the solitary, autonomous author. N. Katherine Hayles describes the posthuman subject as “a collection of heterogeneous components” whose boundaries are always in flux (2012, 3). Rosi Braidotti similarly rejects the fantasy of the mind as disembodied and self-contained, insisting on “embedded, embodied, and situated” knowledge (2013, 190). Read alongside Ahmed and Bilge, posthumanism reframes legitimacy: if all scholarship is already produced through human–nonhuman assemblages—archives, citation managers, reviewers, style guides—then generative AI is not a contamination but another actor in the network. The suspicion it elicits says less about its capacities than about the academy’s investment in maintaining the myth of the autonomous, normatively embodied author.

This opens directly onto Alison Kafer’s concept of crip time, which refuses the imperative to “catch up” to normative speed and instead insists on reshaping timelines to fit embodied needs (2013, 27). Where Sterne diagnoses the bias toward speed, Kafer offers a political refusal. In this frame, AI-assisted writing can operate as a crip technology, adapting to the scholar’s rhythms instead of demanding the scholar conform to institutional time. Mia Mingus’ notion of “access intimacy” (2011) adds a relational dimension, likening the tool to a collaborator who meets you where you are. Here, posthuman authorship converges with disability justice in a shared commitment to dismantling the aesthetic supremacy of smoothness and speed.

Abeba Birhane’s work on algorithmic injustice complicates this optimism, warning that algorithmic systems reproduce the epistemic violence of their contexts (2021, 17), embedding colonial and exclusionary logics into their operations. This echoes Ahmed’s critique of diversity language as a means of preserving hierarchy and Bilge’s warning that palatable difference is no difference at all. Yet, refusing prosthetic tools wholesale risks reinforcing exclusion for those who need them to participate at all. The suspicion directed toward AI-assisted writing mirrors that aimed at scholars whose bodies, voices, or epistemologies are already marked as deviant: both are treated as contaminations of scholarly purity.

Taken together, these literatures reveal that the debate over AI in scholarship cannot be isolated from the broader question of who and what academia deems credible. Price, Sterne, and Kafer expose the temporal and rhetorical norms that underpin legitimacy; Dolmage, Ahmed, and Bilge trace the institutional machinery that enforces it; Hayles and Braidotti dismantle the myth of the solitary author; Mingus and Birhane make visible the political ambivalence of prosthesis. Across these conversations, a shared recognition emerges: the infrastructures that render AI-mediated authorship suspect are the same ones that marginalise queer, disabled, neurodivergent, and otherwise unruly scholarship—not because it lacks rigour, but because it disrupts the institution’s preferred image of itself as orderly, autonomous, and untouched by the messy realities of mediation.

### **Methodology: Situated Inquiry Between Machine and Body**

This project unfolds across two interconnected terrains: an experimental engagement with AI-mediated academic writing and a critical autoethnographic account of my encounters with institutional gatekeeping and containment throughout and beyond my doctoral career. These terrains are not parallel lines but braided threads; each illuminates the other. The AI experiment surfaces the tacit aesthetic and affective norms embedded in peer review, while the autoethnographic strand documents how those same norms operate in embodied encounters, in hiring decisions, faculty meetings, and editorial processes. By holding these strands together, I aim to expose a shared infrastructure of judgment that operates across textual and interpersonal registers.

The decision to work in this doubled frame is methodological as well as political. It draws on the tradition of critical autoethnography as articulated by Holman Jones, Adams, and Ellis, who argue that autoethnography is not simply self-narration but “a way of using personal experience to describe and critique cultural beliefs, practices, and experiences” (2013, 22). Carolyn Ellis, Tony Adams, and Arthur Bochner insist that this method is both process and product, collapsing the divide between the act of research and the form of its presentation (2011, 273). In this project, autoethnography becomes a site of evidence: my experiences are not anecdotal interruptions but primary data that reveal the otherwise invisible affective economies of academic legitimacy. This aligns with Sara Ahmed’s assertion that citation itself is “feminist memory” (2017, 15), here, memory is not only in the references listed but in the lived archive of moments where legitimacy was granted, withheld, or marked as suspect.

The AI experiment was designed with an equally deliberate methodological grounding. I approached generative AI as a writing prosthesis, not as a substitute for intellectual labour but as a collaborator in rendering prose in the aesthetic key preferred by academia. This is consistent with N. Katherine Hayles’ account of the posthuman subject as “a material-informational entity whose boundaries undergo continuous construction and reconstruction” (2012, 3). If authorship is always already distributed across humans, tools, infrastructures, and norms, then AI is not an alien intrusion into scholarly production but another actor in this assemblage. Rosi Braidotti’s insistence that all knowledge is “embedded, embodied, and situated” (2013, 190) underscores this point: the question is not whether AI mediates authorship, but how such mediation is policed.

At the same time, my use of AI is informed by the politics of crip technoscience, as articulated by Aimi Hamraie and Kelly Fritsch (2019, 4), which examines how technologies can be reimagined and repurposed in the service of access and disability justice. Alison Kafer’s concept of crip time, “reorienting our notions of what can and should happen in time” (2013, 27), frames my practice of using AI not as a shortcut but as a temporal adaptation. The generative model allowed me to articulate ideas in ways that respected the rhythms of my own body and voice, particularly as someone whose stutter disrupts the smooth delivery prized by communicational ableism (Sterne 2011, 340). In Mia Mingus’ terms, this is a form of “access intimacy” (2011, para. 3), in which the tool adapts to me rather than forcing me to adapt to it.

The empirical material for this study comes from two distinct but overlapping sources. The first is a corpus of AI-mediated manuscripts submitted for blind peer review. Each was drafted in collaboration with a generative language model, with my prompts and editorial interventions documented in detail. Reviewer feedback, whether in the form of praise for clarity or critique of density, was retained as primary data. The second is a body of autoethnographic materials drawn from over a decade of involvement in and around academic institutions: contemporaneous journals, email correspondence, meeting notes, and reflective essays. These materials trace how legitimacy and exclusion operate at the level of institutional culture, affective interaction, and interpersonal judgment.

The analytic process was iterative and recursive. I read and re-read reviewer feedback alongside my own fieldnotes of institutional encounters, identifying patterns in the criteria used to assess credibility. I paid particular attention to the interplay between aesthetic judgment, terms such as “elegant,” “awkward,” “lucid,” or “overly abstract,” and substantive engagement with ideas. In this, I was guided by Margaret Price’s insight that the “kairotic space” of higher education is governed not only by what is said but by how and when it is said, in ways that privilege normative fluency and speed (2011, 27). This focus allowed me to see the deep structural homology between the mechanisms of peer review and the embodied practices of hiring committees, departmental leadership, and editorial boards.

My positionality is central to the methodology. As an unaffiliated scholar with a PhD, I am free from the bureaucratic circuits that often dictate what is thinkable within an institution. Donna Haraway’s concept of “situated knowledges” is useful here: objectivity, she argues, is not a view from nowhere but an accountable positioning within the world (1988, 590). My position is that of a witch, a queer person, and a disabled scholar whose refusal to be made “palatable” (Bilge 2013, 410) has led to both exclusion and a sharpened capacity to see how aesthetic governance operates. This refusal is not incidental; it shapes the very choice of methods. Ahmed writes that the “feminist killjoy” is one who “gets in the way of the happiness of others” by pointing to the violence concealed in their comfort (2010, 66). In this project, the methodology is killjoy work: it makes visible the exclusions that smooth scholarly prose and polished institutional narratives are designed to conceal.

Ultimately, this is a methodology of entanglement. The AI experiment and the autoethnographic narrative are not presented as separate studies but as interdependent inquiries. The experiment shows how the aesthetic codes of legitimacy can be reproduced on demand; the narrative shows how those same codes are enforced on bodies, voices, and careers. The point is not to determine whether AI is “good” or “bad” for scholarship, nor to offer a universal account of academic ableism, but to demonstrate how both machine-mediated and embodied departures from the aesthetic norm are disciplined through the same logic of symbolic containment.

**Findings: Fluency as Gatekeeping, Containment as Policy**

The results of this project emerge from two sites that might, at first glance, appear far apart in scale and register: the reception of AI-mediated manuscripts in the formalised setting of blind peer review, and the jagged, affectively charged terrain of my own encounters with institutional gatekeeping across doctoral study and professional life. One of these sites is draped in the language of neutrality and dispassionate assessment; the other unfolds in the unpredictable immediacy of meetings, invitations, and refusals that leave no paper trail. Yet when the two are placed side by side, their structural similarity is impossible to miss. In each, legitimacy is not awarded purely on the strength of an idea’s evidence, originality, or intervention, but on whether the work, and the body that bears it, performs in accordance with entrenched aesthetic and affective norms.

It was in the AI-mediated submissions that this became most visible. These manuscripts consistently attracted positive reviewer feedback, often in tones verging on celebratory. They were praised for their “clarity of expression,” “sophisticated argumentation,” and “well-structured engagement with the literature.” One reviewer enthused: “This article offers a rich and nuanced ethnographic account, supported by a deft integration of theory. Its prose is both elegant and precise, making complex concepts accessible without oversimplification.” Another commended it as “fluent, well-paced, and logically ordered,” with “admirable clarity and sense of purpose.”

What was absent from these reviews was as revealing as the praise itself. No suspicion was raised about the authorship, no curiosity about how the prose came to possess such polish. As Margaret Price notes, in the “kairotic space” of academia, fluency is treated as a universal good, “as if reason, evidence, and the self-contained writerly self were natural rather than cultural” (2011, 27). If the surface of the work matched the discipline’s stylistic template, the question of origin became irrelevant. Rosi Braidotti might read this as evidence of what she calls “the myth of the autonomous author” (2013, 190), a myth sustained only because the sound of the voice conforms to an already-legitimated key.

Where criticism did appear, it rarely engaged the underlying conceptual architecture or the strength of evidence. Instead, it targeted those features I had deliberately retained from my own unaided style: passages of dense theoretical weaving, recursive loops in the argument, paragraph structures that resisted the “topic sentence → evidence → conclusion” formula. These were flagged as “overly abstract,” “idiosyncratic,” or “unusual.” Sara Ahmed’s observation that “the institution gives recognition only to those who do not cause trouble” (2012, 143) acquires a textual corollary here: stylistic disfluency is trouble. It slows the expected pace of reading, interrupts the smooth consumption of knowledge, and risks making the reader work in ways the template has been designed to avoid.

When I placed manuscripts side by side, the unaided original and its AI-mediated counterpart, the mechanism revealed itself with clinical clarity. In the originals, the dense or recursive passages were read as “lack of clarity” or “awkward phrasing.” In the AI renderings, where the same arguments were recast into the cadences, transitions, and rhythmic predictability of disciplinary prose, they were recoded as “lucid” and “elegant.” Dolmage’s point that ableism is embedded as much in communicative practice as in physical architecture (2017, 15) was confirmed in real time. The experiment became a mirror, showing that peer review rewards not the vitality or daring of thought, but the ability to dress that thought in the costume of legitimacy.

This same logic, visible in the fate of manuscripts, also operated in the arc of my academic life. Here it appeared as what I have called **symbolic containment**, closely related to Sirma Bilge’s notion of the “palatability of difference” (2013, 410). Symbolic containment describes the selective tolerance of marginalised scholars long enough to showcase their presence, while withholding the permanence or influence that might allow them to reshape the institution’s foundations.

One winter in Montréal, I was invited to lunch by the departmental director. The meeting felt, at the time, almost like mentorship: warm enough to feel encouraging, attentive enough to seem like recognition. We spoke about my research on contemporary Paganism, about methodology, about the intersections of ritual and politics. I left thinking we had found common ground. It rather became the occasion for an unspoken audition I did not know I was giving. I stuttered, as I often do in high-pressure, one-on-one settings. Weeks later, I learned that in a faculty conversation he had said, without qualification: “He will never teach at this university.” No formal review, no dossier, no teaching observation, just an unmarked audition whose real criteria were affective and aesthetic. Ahmed describes such moments as the “brick walls” of diversity work (2012, 26): the institution’s smile hardening into refusal once the real decisions are made.

Years later, I approached a senior professor during a graduate conference I had organised. I asked, plainly, whether I might be considered to teach a course in my area of expertise. I had syllabi ready; similar courses were being offered by less specialised instructors. The response was immediate, and sharp: “You’re paranoid… You don’t know what you’re talking about. Let me do my job.” Bilge’s description of the institution’s “affective economy” (2013, 412) resonates here: my request did not follow the choreography of deference and timing the institution expects. In asserting my own value outside its sanctioned channels, I disrupted the comfort of its hierarchy, and was swiftly disciplined back into place.

Even my doctoral defence condensed the paradox I have been tracing. The first minutes felt like wading through wet concrete: each sentence heavy, each word pulled from my mouth as though against resistance. I stumbled often enough to sense the room’s collective breath holding, not in curiosity about the argument, but in anxious anticipation of whether I would “make it through.” Their eyes followed not my reasoning but the mechanics of my delivery, the embodied disfluency that marked me before I even reached my claims.

Yet when the questioning began, something shifted. The words came without hesitation, the arguments were alive in my mouth. I spoke of beauty standards as regulatory regimes, of the disciplining of sexual norms, of the dismantling of prejudicial narratives about homosexuality and transgender identity, without euphemism, without softening the edge for comfort, because the conditions I was naming demanded an unsanitised truth. The air in the room recalibrated. Eyes lifted, bodies leaned forward. Friends and family, who until then had witnessed me in a defensive posture, now saw the research move as it was meant to move, embodied, reasoned, unapologetic. The shift was not just in my delivery, but in the atmosphere: a recalibration of who was presumed to hold authority in that room.

The jury mirrored the spectrum of my academic life: a supervisor who had backed my work with steadiness and care; a feminist theorist who had challenged it rigorously and respected its force; an older gay professor whose interest had always stopped at aesthetics, who eroticised the novelty without grasping the politics; and an absent fourth whose silence hovered as a reminder that recognition can also be withheld without words. In that exchange, even the most sceptical faces seemed, for a moment, to register the seriousness of what they had been trying to domesticate.

I have felt that shift before, in spaces far less formal. When I spoke in classrooms about Wicca and contemporary Paganism, I sometimes sang the chants I had heard during fieldwork, ritual songs passed through circles, covens, and queer spiritual gatherings. In those moments, my voice, so often read as unstable, became resonant, rhythmic, powerful. I was not just speaking about magic. I was invoking it. The atmosphere would change; students would lean in. Theory gave way to embodiment. The chant was a method, a citation, a situated epistemology. And for a brief moment, the classroom became a ritual site. I remember those moments not as performance, but as clarity: I knew exactly what I was doing. So did the students. Whether the faculty did, or wanted to, was another question.

Both the defence and the classroom moments revealed how legitimacy could be reconfigured, not by conforming to the institution’s cadence, but by shifting the terms of engagement entirely, forcing the space to respond to a rhythm it did not set. These instances make visible how fluency operates as a gatekeeping device: determining not only how work is read, but whether it is read at all. It is less about the clarity of ideas than about their compliance with an aesthetic norm. In my own case, the same arguments received radically different receptions depending on whether they arrived in my own cadence or through AI-assisted smoothing into disciplinary prose.

I have never watched the recording. I do not need to. To replay it would invite the institution’s gaze back into the memory, offering one more occasion to doubt. What mattered was not the permanence of the moment but its occurrence: they saw me, I saw myself, and the work stood, not in spite of my voice, but through it.

Even publication was not immune. After my doctoral defence, I was invited to co-edit a special issue of the department’s journal. I contributed both the editorial introduction and an article extending my dissertation. The review process was tense; one reviewer misread key elements of my field, yet their authority shaped the editorial demands. I revised, clarifying where I could, adding citations, but the schedule was tight. When the issue appeared, my article alone bore a footnote: “The author, unfortunately, chose to maintain their article as is.” Ahmed’s concept of “non-performativity” (2012, 117) describes this perfectly: the public gesture of inclusion, accompanied by the subtle but unmistakable signal that my inclusion did not signify endorsement.

The first terrain, grounded in autoethnography, shows how the ordinary moments of academic life reveal the institution’s governance of time and form. Each moment unfolded not as an isolated encounter but as part of a system that evaluates bodies and voices according to their conformity with unspoken aesthetic and affective norms. In my own case, the act of speaking as a queer scholar who treats sexuality as a central object of inquiry, rather than an optional subtopic, already marked my presence as excessive. When I spoke about beauty standards as regulatory regimes, the disciplining of sexual norms, or the dismantling of prejudicial narratives about homosexuality and transgender identity, I did so in ways that refused euphemism or sanitisation. In classrooms filled with twenty-year-old urban students raised in a hypersexualised culture, this was not provocation for its own sake, but a direct engagement with the conditions shaping their own subjectivities. The department, however, responded not to the accuracy or urgency of these interventions but to their form: too embodied, too unfiltered, too resistant to being reframed within the tonal decorum of academic propriety. Here, the temporal politics of the institution, the pacing of speech, the modulation of affect, intersect directly with the politics of embodiment and address.

The second terrain, the AI-versus-alive manuscript experiment, tested these politics under controlled conditions. Identical arguments, citations, and ethnographic grounding were presented in two versions: one mediated by AI to fit the academy’s unspoken stylistic template, the other left alive, with its seams visible, its cadences shaped by the rhythms of my own stuttering voice and queer sensibility. This alive version did not merely carry the trace of disfluency; it carried the tonal and rhetorical imprint of a scholar unafraid to confront sexual normativity in blunt, embodied terms. Peer review did not evaluate these features as epistemic strengths; rather, it rewarded the smoothed version that could be absorbed quickly and without friction. The suspicion directed toward AI-assisted writing here mirrors the suspicion toward voices and discourses already marked as deviant: both are seen as contaminations of scholarly purity, both are policed not for lack of rigour but for divergence from the affective template of “serious” scholarship.

Across both terrains, the findings converge on a shared recognition: that the politics of academic time, the policing of form, and the disciplining of sexual discourse are part of the same machinery. Factice objectivity demands that the scholar appear disembodied even when speaking from the most embodied of positions; academic betrayal occurs when institutions invite marginalised voices only to reframe or erase them; dead knowledge is what remains when the living charge of queer, embodied critique is stripped away for palatability; fugitive circulation is what happens when such critique finds ways to move outside the authorised channels, at its own pace and in its own form. These are not separate phenomena but interlocking mechanisms, and the solitary witch, without the protective circle of departmental allegiance, must learn to work within and against them simultaneously.

That resistance was not simply a matter of pacing or rhetorical form. It was also lodged in the body from which the words emerged, and in the kinds of truths those words carried. My queerness was visible, uncontained, and I spoke about sexuality without the hedging that keeps such discussions within the bounds of polite academic discourse. I used images that did not veil their force, examples drawn from contemporary queer and trans life that resisted translation into the sanitised idioms of theory.

In classrooms and conference rooms alike, I met twenty-year-old urban students who had grown up saturated with hypersexualised media, their everyday lexicon already threaded with references to sex and desire. My teaching met that fluency head-on, framing it not as a problem to be corrected but as a resource to be redirected toward critical analysis. What the department saw, however, was not intellectual engagement but an excess, too free, too embodied, too unregulated. They read the strength of my voice as a threat precisely because they had decided, in advance, that I did not have one. I have wondered since whether the same blunt precision, offered by a heterosexual man speaking of normative reproductive sexuality, would have been reframed as authority rather than impropriety, as scholarly candour rather than transgressive exposure.

To speak in this register, to occupy that space with the full force of one’s voice and presence, was to step further outside the institutional circle than any lapse in pacing or format could explain. It marked me not simply as different but as deviant from the institution’s own self-image, a solitary witch in a faculty meeting, refusing the quiet containment of the coven, unwilling to renounce the rituals and registers that had kept my work alive.

These patterns did not dissolve when I stepped outside academia into clinical research. My doctoral title was omitted from documents; authority was assumed for colleagues whose bodies, voices, and demeanours fit the unspoken ideal. Meetings followed a familiar script: those whose fluency matched the dominant aesthetic carried weight without question, while my contributions required repeated demonstration to be recognised at all. The disciplinary boundaries had shifted, but the infrastructure, ableism, aesthetic gatekeeping, symbolic containment, remained in place.

Read through the lenses of Dolmage, Price, Sterne, Ahmed, Bilge, and Braidotti, the symmetry is clear. In both peer review and lived institutional experience, legitimacy hinges less on intellectual contribution than on the ability, or willingness, to perform fluency and align affect, comportment, and style with disciplinary norms. Where that performance falters, or is consciously refused, the result is not debate on the merits but containment. The suspicion aimed at AI-assisted writing and the suspicion aimed at unruly embodiment are not simply parallel tendencies; they are two instances of the same phenomenon. Both threaten the fiction of the scholar as an autonomous, unaided, normatively embodied mind producing knowledge in isolation. Both reveal that all authorship is already mediated, distributed, and contingent, and both are disciplined precisely for making that mediation visible

**The Aesthetic Governance of Legitimacy**

The findings from this study, drawn from both the AI-mediated manuscripts and my lived academic trajectory, reveal a structural pattern: legitimacy in scholarly work is conferred not solely through intellectual merit, but through alignment with aesthetic and affective norms that have become naturalised as the markers of credibility. This section examines that pattern as a form of aesthetic governance, in which style, tempo, and affect are mobilised as disciplinary tools. It is in this nexus that communicational ableism (Sterne 2011, 340), symbolic containment (Bilge 2013, 410), and the posthuman politics of authorship (Hayles 2012, 3; Braidotti 2013, 190) intersect.

At the most immediate level, the AI experiment demonstrates that fluency itself operates as a primary currency in the peer review economy. When manuscripts bore the marks of my unaided style, recursive argumentation, occasional syntactic density, paragraph structures that resisted the linear formula, they attracted criticism framed in aesthetic terms: “awkward,” “unclear,” “overly abstract.” When the same ideas were rendered into the smooth, transitional cadences of disciplinary prose by the AI, they drew praise for “elegance” and “clarity.” Price’s account of the “kairotic space” of academia explains this dynamic: the ability to produce the right style of writing at the right tempo is read as evidence of competence, while deviation from these norms is recoded as intellectual weakness (2011, 27).

Dolmage pushes this further by framing such preferences as the enactment of academic ableism, an “enduring feature” of the institution that is as much about discursive architecture as it is about physical space (2017, 7). In his terms, the demand for a frictionless scholarly voice is structurally homologous to the demand for architectural inaccessibility: both are designed around an imagined “ideal body” and “ideal voice” that move without obstruction. Sterne sharpens this point by showing how communicational ableism turns smoothness into an unquestioned proxy for credibility (2011, 340), making it almost impossible to disentangle aesthetic preference from epistemic judgment.

This has direct implications for understanding symbolic containment. Ahmed’s work on non-performativity makes clear that the institution can display a commitment to diversity without altering its aesthetic codes (2012, 117). Bilge specifies the affective dimension of this process: difference is acceptable only when it is “palatable,” when it can be incorporated without altering the institution’s self-image (2013, 412). In my experience, both as a scholar submitting manuscripts and as a body in academic space, the refusal or inability to produce “palatable” fluency is precisely where containment takes hold. This is the logic at work in the lunch meeting that ended with the quiet sentence, “He will never teach here.” It was not a matter of scholarly deficiency but of affective and aesthetic dissonance.

The peer review results illuminate how this containment operates at the textual level. AI mediation made my writing conform to the aesthetic profile that reviewers rewarded, and in doing so, revealed that the boundary between inclusion and exclusion is not determined by intellectual originality but by a text’s ability to perform normative voice. Braidotti’s posthumanism is critical here: if all authorship is already distributed, across archives, citation managers, style guides, editors, and now AI, then the policing of AI use is less about preserving rigour and more about preserving the fiction of the autonomous, normatively embodied author (2013, 190). Hayles makes a parallel point in arguing that the posthuman subject is always “a material-informational entity” in flux (2012, 3). What the AI experiment exposes is not a rupture in authorship, but the institution’s discomfort with making visible the mediations it already relies upon.

Kafer’s notion of crip time offers a temporal counterpoint to this aesthetic governance. If the demand for fluency is a demand for normative pace, crip time insists on reorganising the terms of production around embodied realities (2013, 27). The AI, in my case, became a tool for producing text in alignment with my rhythms, not the institution’s. Yet, as Birhane warns, any technological prosthesis can reproduce the biases of the system that built it (2021, 17). The fact that AI could be trained on the very stylistic norms that enact exclusion means that its liberatory potential depends on how it is used, and by whom.

This tension, between the possibility of access intimacy (Mingus 2011, para. 3) and the reproduction of bias, is at the heart of the aesthetic governance of legitimacy. The AI experiment suggests that mediation can enable participation without assimilation, but only if the user retains agency over which aspects of their style are adapted and which are preserved as acts of refusal. In my own practice, I used AI not to erase disfluency entirely, but to make deliberate choices about when to let it surface, to trouble the text in ways that were legible on my terms, not as errors to be corrected.

Taken together, the experiment and the autoethnographic record show that aesthetic governance operates as a form of infrastructural power. It is not reducible to individual prejudice; it is embedded in the timelines of review, the templates of style, the performance expectations of committees, and the unspoken consensus about what “good scholarship” should feel like. It is maintained by the conflation of fluency with intellectual worth, and by the refusal to name this conflation as a political choice.

By situating this analysis within both posthuman theory and critical disability studies, I argue that the aesthetic governance of legitimacy is a hinge point where the politics of embodiment and the politics of mediation meet. It disciplines bodies and texts in the same motion, rendering both safe for circulation only when they conform to the preferred image of the autonomous, unaided, normatively embodied scholar. Both AI-assisted writing and unruly embodiment threaten that image, not because they undermine knowledge, but because they make visible the collective, mediated, contingent nature of its production.

**Conclusion: Refusing Containment, Reclaiming Mediation**

This project began with an observation that has long lingered in the margins of my work but was sharpened here into a central claim: legitimacy in academia is not conferred on the basis of ideas alone. It is granted or withheld through a dense matrix of aesthetic and affective norms that masquerade as neutral markers of quality. These norms dictate not just what counts as credible knowledge, but how that knowledge must sound, move, and feel to be recognised at all. The AI experiment and my autoethnographic record, distinct in method but united in object, demonstrate that this governance operates with equal force on the page and in the room, on the body and in the text. Whether in the turn of a sentence or the tempo of a stuttered reply to a departmental chair, the demand is the same: conform to the aesthetic code, or accept your containment.

To name this process is to see how deeply it is woven into the infrastructure of scholarly life. As Dolmage argues, ableism is not a flaw that can be patched over; it is “the most enduring feature” of higher education, inscribed in its spaces, schedules, and communicative expectations (2017, 7). Sterne’s account of communicational ableism (2011, 340) and Price’s theorisation of kairotic space (2011, 27) make clear that fluency, smoothness, speed, affective neutrality, is not an innocent preference but a technology of governance. It is the criterion by which ideas are judged legible and their authors deemed trustworthy. This governance is at once intimate and systemic: it is embedded in reviewer comments, hiring deliberations, hallway encounters, and the unspoken sense of who belongs.

The AI experiment crystallised this with uncomfortable clarity. Manuscripts whose prose bore the marks of my unaided style, recursive argumentation, syntactic density, deliberate moments of disfluency, drew aesthetic critiques: “awkward,” “unclear,” “overly abstract.” Yet when those same arguments, with identical evidence, were recast by AI into the cadences of normative academic prose, reviewers praised them for “elegance,” “clarity,” and “sophisticated argumentation.” In this transformation, the content was untouched; only the performance of voice changed. That this performance alone determined the tone of the review is perhaps the clearest evidence of what I call the aesthetic governance of legitimacy.

Here, Ahmed’s concept of non-performativity (2012, 117) and Bilge’s critique of the “palatability of difference” (2013, 412) sharpen the frame. The institution will accept difference when it arrives dressed in its own image, when it is translated into the dominant affective key. But difference that refuses translation, that insists on speaking in its own rhythms, is contained. My experiences, from the lunch meeting that ended in the quietly definitive “He will never teach here,” to the footnote marking my article as suspect, are not exceptions. They are the ordinary operations of containment. They show how legitimacy is granted conditionally, as long as it does not trouble the institution’s aesthetic order.

The AI experiment reveals that this order is not simply a matter of human preference; it is codified and reproducible. Generative AI, trained on vast corpora of disciplinary prose, can perform the aesthetic requirements of legitimacy on demand. This should unsettle any claim that these requirements are organic or inevitable. As Braidotti notes, the autonomous author is a myth, maintained through the erasure of the collective, mediated processes that make scholarship possible (2013, 190). Hayles’ vision of the posthuman subject as “a material-informational entity” (2012, 3) drives the point further: authorship has always been mediated, by citation managers, style guides, peer reviewers, and institutional templates. The policing of AI is not about preserving intellectual rigour; it is about keeping that mediation invisible.

For disabled, queer, and otherwise unruly scholars, this policing is already familiar. The demand for fluency is also a demand for normative embodiment and comportment. Kafer’s crip time (2013, 27) offers one mode of refusal, shifting the conditions of scholarly production to fit the realities of disabled lives rather than the other way around. In my case, the AI became a crip-technoscientific tool (Hamraie & Fritsch 2019, 4), enabling participation on my terms. Mingus’ notion of “access intimacy” (2011, para. 3) resonates here: the tool adjusted to my rhythms rather than forcing my body and voice to bend to its tempo. Yet, as Birhane cautions, these same tools can reproduce the epistemic violence of the systems that built them (2021, 17). AI’s stylistic smoothing can be a prosthesis, but it can also be a coloniser, training the unruly into docility if used without care.

This is why the question is not whether AI should be embraced or rejected. Such binaries obscure the deeper issue: the suspicion directed toward machinic mediation and the suspicion directed toward embodied difference are the same suspicion. Both unsettle the fiction of the scholar as a self-contained, normatively embodied mind. Both make visible the messy, collective, contingent conditions of knowledge production. And both are disciplined, contained, or erased when they threaten the institution’s preferred image of itself.

From my vantage point outside academia, I can say this without the hedging that affiliation often demands. Haraway’s insistence on “situated knowledges” (1988, 590) is not just a theoretical anchor here but a description of my practice. My objectivity comes not from detachment but from accountability to my own embodied, political position. I am accountable to the archives of trouble, the moments of exclusion, the refusals, the mediated collaborations, that make this work possible. I am not required to package them into a narrative that reassures those who benefit from the current order.

To refuse containment, then, is to refuse to perform fluency as the price of legitimacy. It is to embrace, as Ahmed’s feminist killjoy does, the work of causing trouble (2010, 66), not for the sake of disruption alone, but to open space for other rhythms, other voices, other styles of knowledge. This project is a record of such refusal, but also of the mediations that make refusal sustainable: the human collaborators, the machinic tools, the temporal adaptations that keep the work alive outside the sanctioned timelines and spaces.

If the institution cannot make room for such work without neutralising it, then the work must flourish elsewhere. It must take root in unaffiliated scholarship, in queer and disabled knowledge networks, in communities where mediation is not hidden but celebrated. The paper may not exist in the form academia finds most comfortable. It may stutter, loop back on itself, defy the clean progression from problem to solution. But it exists in a form truer to the conditions of its making, and that is the only legitimacy worth claiming.

The lunch with the department head, the doctoral defence, the student conference presentation, each scene offered a small aperture into the larger machinery. These were not aberrations but routine points of contact where legitimacy was conferred or withheld according to unwritten criteria. They stage openness while designing for containment.

Fluency signals compliance to the unmarked norm, more than clarity itself. In the peer review process, conformity in cadence and form inoculated manuscripts against suspicion, while in lived encounters, the same conformity determined access to opportunity.

The work of exclusion is often in the rationing of presence, not its elimination. Symbolic containment remains an enduring strategy, offering visibility without permanence. The stories remain bound to the moments in which they occurred, retaining their texture as a refusal to be smoothed into the generalities institutions prefer.
