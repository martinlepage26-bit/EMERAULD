# Chapter One — Willow Rosenberg: Recursion and Saturated Grief

## Extracted Text

Chapter One — Willow Rosenberg: Recursion and Saturated Grief
It begins again in the kitchen.​
Not the Rosemont one from the introduction, though it could be — the geography of these
spaces blurs when the architecture of the working is the same.​
This time it’s in Parc‑Extension, winter pressing in tight around the building.​
The heat is uneven; near the stove it’s warm enough to shed your coat, but along the outside
wall you can feel the draft curling in around your ankles.​
The windows sweat against the night air.
On the table: an altar‑in‑progress.​
Half the objects are there because someone carried them in with intention — a jar candle, a
small dish of salt, a bone wrapped in red thread.​
The other half were scavenged from the kitchen itself — a battered teaspoon, a chipped
enamel cup, a plate of clementines.​
And at the centre, a Bluetooth speaker, already wrapped in the now‑familiar loop of red yarn.
The sound is unmistakable once you’ve heard it in enough rooms: Willow’s resurrection
chant from Bargaining, slowed and thickened so it moves less like speech and more like
weather.​
It doesn’t just sit in the air; it presses into it, bending the atmosphere slightly, altering how
breath moves through the room.​
You can stand in the doorway and feel the difference between the kitchen’s charged air and
the unmarked air of the hallway.

Why Willow
Willow’s arc in Buffy the Vampire Slayer is one of the longest, most intricate, and most
destabilising in the series.​
She begins as the shy, hyper‑competent best friend — the one who can be counted on to
research the demon, hack the computer system, hold the centre when others falter.​
But across seven seasons, she becomes the most magically powerful character in the series,
and also the one most prone to overreaching.​
Her magic is rarely “pure tool”; it is saturated with her emotional life, particularly grief,
love, and rage.
The Bargaining resurrection is a hinge point.​
It is both a plot necessity — bringing Buffy back for Season Six — and a profound narrative
gamble: to begin a season with a magical act that is, in the logic of the show, morally and
magically suspect.​
The spell is framed as dangerous, even reckless, but Willow drives it forward anyway.​
The moment is uncomfortably saturated: with love, with fear of abandonment, with the need
to undo what cannot be undone.

Recursion as glitch
In the episode, the resurrection is staged as a sustained, multi‑component working.​
Willow’s chant repeats in a near‑constant loop, punctuated by other ritual actions: cutting her
arm for blood, invoking Osiris, pouring the essence of the demon’s egg into the cauldron.​
The scene is structured so that repetition is part of its form — each cycle pulling the working
further along, but also deepening the risk.
This is where the glitch lives.​
In the moral economy of Buffy, magic is supposed to be a finite act: invoked, deployed,
closed.​
Willow’s spell resists closure by design.​
It must keep running until Buffy returns — and there is no backup plan for if she doesn’t.​
The repetition becomes the work, the loop the form.

Post‑canonical life of the chant
Lifted from its canonical context, the chant retains this recursive structure.​
In the field, it is rarely played once.​
It is looped, either manually or digitally, until it merges with the sonic fabric of the ritual.​
It is not a “track” to be enjoyed; it is a temporal anchor.
In the Parc‑Extension kitchen, the chant runs for just under an hour, long enough for time to
start flattening.​
Conversation ebbs and flows around it, but the chant remains steady, like a metronome for
the working.​
At moments, someone hums along unconsciously; at others, it drops below conscious
hearing entirely, only to re‑emerge when the loop clicks slightly as it restarts.
Here, Willow is not “present” as a fictional character.​
She is present as a set of operational qualities: sustained vocalisation, tonal gravity, and the
charge of overreaching love.​
These qualities are executable in any number of contexts — grief vigils, healing circles, even
protection workings — without having to narrate their origin.
Recursion as Magical Technique
Recursion is often treated in media studies as a formal property — the loop, the repeat
viewing, the meme template re‑captioned indefinitely.​
In programming, recursion is a function that calls itself until a condition is met — or, if
improperly constrained, until the system crashes.​

In ritual magic, recursion appears as repetition: the mantra, the chant, the cycle of breath, the
circling of the compass points.
But recursion is not just repetition for its own sake.​
It is repetition as an engine for transformation.​
Each cycle through the loop is not identical to the last; it carries the residue of what has
already been done, the incremental shifts in energy, the micro‑adjustments of attention.​
The form is circular, but the movement is spiral.

In Buffy’s narrative economy
Within Buffy the Vampire Slayer, most spells are episodic: perform the incantation, complete
the gesture, receive the result, close the working.​
Even large‑scale rituals, like the Slayer essence transfer in “Primeval,” have a built‑in
end‑point.
Willow’s resurrection spell in Bargaining refuses this episodic neatness.​
Its structure is more like a siege: keep the chant alive, keep the components in motion, keep
the working going until the barrier breaks.​
It is not a spell with a clear exit strategy; it is a spell that will run as long as the caster can
sustain it.
That structural refusal to end is part of why the scene is so unsettling.​
It is narratively “wrong” in the Buffyverse’s moral geometry — not because resurrection is
inherently evil, but because the scale of Willow’s commitment leaves no room for
withdrawal.​
She has removed her own capacity to choose to stop.
This is the narrative glitch that becomes so potent in ritual life: the moment where the only
option is to continue.

In queer and trans ritual life
In my fieldwork, recursion works less like siege and more like heartbeat.​
The loop is rarely aggressive; it is persistent.​
The repetition holds the room in a particular state, making it harder for outside time to
intrude.
In the Parc‑Extension kitchen, for example, the chant’s low, thick rhythm became a kind of
anti‑clock.​
People arrived late, left briefly to smoke, returned, and found the chant unchanged.​
The work was still happening, still holding its shape, still anchored to that loop.

One participant later told me, “It’s like the sound was holding the door open. You could come
back through it at any time.”​
That “door” is the ritual opening: the fragment’s capacity to keep the liminal state alive until
the work — whatever it is for each person — has had time to unfold.

Why recursion matters here
In queer ritual life, especially in precarious urban contexts, rituals are often brief, improvised,
and subject to interruption.​
Neighbours knock, roommates pass through, a phone pings, the cat jumps onto the altar.​
Maintaining ritual time under these conditions requires tools that can absorb disruption
without collapsing.
Recursion is one such tool.​
It does not depend on a flawless, uninterrupted arc; it depends on persistence.​
The loop can survive distraction.​
The loop can pick up again after a break.​
The loop is forgiving in a way that many ceremonial forms are not.
This makes recursion particularly well‑suited to queer and trans ritual worlds, where
participants may arrive late from work, step out for a cigarette, or slip in and out of the circle
depending on physical or emotional capacity.​
Recursion keeps the work available for re‑entry.

The loop as refusal
There is also a political charge to keeping the loop alive.​
In mainstream ritual discourse — whether liturgical, New Age, or pop‑spiritual — closure is
treated as a virtue.​
You “ground” the energy, you “close” the circle, you “release” the work.​
This narrative of proper ritual hygiene mirrors therapeutic narratives of grief “completion”
and social narratives of “moving on.”
Recursion refuses this closure.​
It says: we are not done.​
We will not be done until we decide otherwise — and maybe not even then.​
The loop becomes a tactic for sustaining grief, desire, rage, or joy beyond the point where
normative time would declare them excessive.
This is why Willow’s fragment matters so much: her recursion in Bargaining carries the risk
and the promise of refusing the end.​

When that fragment is run in a real‑world ritual, it carries that refusal into the space, making
it available to others as a shared stance.
The Post‑Canonical Mechanics of Willow’s Chant
When Bargaining first aired in 2001, the resurrection scene was already structured for
memorability.​
It was not filmed as a quick‑cut montage but as a sustained sequence in which the chant
anchors the action.​
The repetition gives it weight.​
The camera keeps returning to Willow, mouth moving in steady rhythm, body swaying
slightly with the cadence.​
The visual grammar tells the viewer: this is the work, and the work is ongoing.

From scene to sample
In the field, that sequence is rarely invoked in its entirety.​
Instead, a particular sonic loop — four lines of incantation — is lifted from the scene and
isolated.​
Sometimes the source is a fan‑made audio rip, imperfect and fuzzy, pulled from an old DVD.​
Other times it’s a clean clip extracted by someone with better editing skills.​
But regardless of fidelity, the transformation into loop form is the first step in its
post‑canonical life.
This is a technical choice with ritual consequences.​
Looping removes the original scene’s forward motion toward a single climactic event
(Buffy’s resurrection).​
It detaches the fragment from its resolution and turns it into a temporal machine that can
run indefinitely.​
The loop no longer points forward to a specific plot outcome; it points sideways, into
sustained ritual time.

Context‑agnostic deployment
Once looped, the chant becomes portable across ritual contexts.​
I have recorded its use in:
●​ Grief vigils, where it holds the space for unscripted speaking.​
●​ Healing circles, where it anchors the rhythm of laying‑on‑of‑hands.​

●​ Protection workings, where it is played low enough to be felt more than heard.​
●​ Seasonal rites, especially Samhain and Beltane, where it undergirds long stretches of
collective trance.​

What’s notable is that in many of these contexts, not everyone present recognises the chant’s
source.​
Recognition is not required for it to function.​
This is crucial: the fragment’s operational capacity is not dependent on shared fandom but
on its sonic and affective properties.

Modulation and adaptation
In some spaces, the chant is run “straight” — the original loop played without alteration.​
In others, it is slowed, pitch‑shifted, or layered with other sounds.​
One group in Mile End runs it under a field recording of wind through dry reeds.​
Another overlays it with a low drone from a singing bowl.​
In a winter solstice livestream, it was mixed with a sample from Once More, With Feeling,
creating a tonal bleed between two very different episodes.
These adaptations are not decorative.​
They are ways of tuning the fragment to the emotional frequency of the working.​
Slowing deepens trance; layering thickens the texture of the space; overlaying with another
sample changes the emotional colour without losing the loop’s anchoring function.

Archiving the loop
Over time, the chant accumulates a shadow archive.​
In one Discord server I observed, the audio file lives pinned in a private channel alongside
other “ritual resources” — PDFs of invocations, images for altars, soundscapes for trance
work.​
It is passed between groups via Google Drive links, WeTransfer files, or even AirDrop in
person.​
The file name is rarely “Willow Chant”; more often it’s something oblique like Loop‑03.wav
or dooropen.mp3.​
These naming practices both obscure and normalise its use: it becomes one more item in the
toolkit, alongside salt jars and candle‑holders.
This shadow archive is a key feature of post‑canonical life.​
The fragment is no longer just “a scene from Buffy” — it is in the system.​
It circulates alongside, and on equal footing with, other ritual technologies.

Why this counts as infrastructure
If a ritual element is portable, repeatable, adaptable, and archived for future use, it is
infrastructure.​
The Willow chant meets all of these criteria.​
It can be dropped into a working on short notice, tuned to fit the mood, passed between
practitioners, stored for later.​
It is no longer an event; it is a tool.
And because it originates from a glitch — a moment in the Buffyverse where magic refuses
closure — it carries with it a latent refusal each time it runs.​
Even stripped of its narrative context, it retains the operational stance of “keep going until
the door opens.”​
This stance is what makes it so valuable in queer and trans ritual life, where closure is often
premature and staying in the work is a political necessity.
Three Vignettes: The Chant at Work

1. Parc‑Extension — Grief Vigil
It is late January, snow pressed hard against the edges of the sidewalk.​
The apartment is warm, dim, the light pooled low around the table.​
The Bluetooth speaker is wrapped in red yarn again, the chant already playing as people
arrive.​
It has been running for twenty minutes by the time the room fills, though there’s no formal
start.
The purpose is simple: to hold space for the absence of someone who should be here but
isn’t.​
People drift in and out of conversation.​
One person stands at the window, tracing the outline of frost on the glass.​
Another keeps their head bent over a mug, steam curling toward their face.
The chant threads through it all.​
It is not background noise; it is scaffolding.​
The loop keeps the room from tipping into awkward silence or busy chatter.​
It creates a third space — neither mute grief nor noisy distraction — where speech can
happen if it needs to.
Analysis:​
Here the chant functions as emotional containment.​
Its repetition gives the vigil a steady pulse, allowing participants to enter and leave speech

without breaking the shared mood.​
The loop also keeps grief present without forcing it into words, resisting the social pressure
to narrativise the loss too soon.

2. Mile End — Beltane Rooftop
It’s early May, dusk edging into night.​
On a downtown rooftop, fairy lights criss‑cross between two chimneys.​
A table in the centre holds candles, a plate of strawberries, and a pitcher of something
vaguely sparkling.​
The air smells of lilacs and barbecue smoke drifting up from the street.
Someone has brought a portable speaker.​
The Willow loop plays softly under laughter and conversation, just audible enough that when
the group shifts into more focused ritual — circling the table, joining hands — the chant feels
like it’s been there all along, waiting.
Later, as a small blessing is spoken over the strawberries, the chant is layered with a hand
drum, the beat following the rhythm of the loop almost exactly.​
It’s as if the chant has pulled the drumming into its orbit rather than the other way around.
Analysis:​
In this context, the chant works as a temporal bridge.​
It holds the space between casual social time and ritual time without requiring a hard break.​
Because the loop has no sharp beginning or end, it smooths the transition, allowing
participants to slide into the working without abrupt ceremony.

3. NDG — Online Samhain Gathering
It’s late October, the sky already black at 5:30 PM.​
On a Zoom call, boxes flicker with faces lit by candles, fairy lights, laptop screens.​
One host shares audio through the platform: the Willow chant, slowed and pitched down
until it is almost unrecognisable.​
The audio quality over Zoom compresses the chant into something grainier, more distant —
like it’s being broadcast from another room in a house you can’t enter.
As people take turns naming their dead, the chant hums behind them.​
When a name catches in someone’s throat, the loop seems to fill the gap, carrying the
moment until they can speak or pass.​
After the final name, no one speaks for several minutes.​
The chant keeps running.

Analysis:​
Here the chant functions as digital anchoring.​
It overcomes the fractured temporality of a Zoom call, creating a sonic through‑line that
connects each person’s isolated room into a shared auditory field.​
The lo‑fi compression even enhances its liminality, making it feel like something moving
between worlds.

Common Core: Recursion as Stability
Across these contexts — in‑person grief vigil, rooftop Beltane, online Samhain — the chant’s
role changes:
●​ Containment in the vigil.​
●​ Transition on the rooftop.​
●​ Anchoring online.​

But in all three, the loop’s recursive nature is the constant.​
Its repetition builds a continuity that resists rupture, whether from emotional overwhelm,
social chatter, or the lag of digital space.​
The glitch it carries from Buffy — refusal of closure — becomes the technique that holds
these disparate rituals in motion.
Recursion, Queer Temporality, and Ritual Time

Queer temporality as refusal
José Esteban Muñoz’s Cruising Utopia (2009) reminds us that queerness is not only an
identity but a temporal orientation.​
It lives in the “not‑yet” — an active horizon that resists the pull toward normative endpoints.​
This is not simply the absence of fulfilment; it is the refusal of fulfilment on terms that are
already decided by straight time.​
Straight time offers a rhythm: birth, coupling, reproduction, retirement, legacy.​
Queer time interrupts that rhythm, stretching and bending it, inserting loops where linearity
demands forward motion.
Willow’s resurrection working in Bargaining performs that interruption twice over.​
First, narratively: by refusing to accept Buffy’s death as a natural endpoint.​
Second, formally: by structuring the ritual itself as an open‑ended loop with no built‑in stop.​
It is an insistence that the “proper” endpoint is not only negotiable but rejectable.​

It is queerness as method — not merely resisting the given timeline, but building a different
clock entirely.
Elizabeth Freeman’s Time Binds (2010) offers language for this: queer time is made of
attachments, repetitions, and re‑bindings that draw us into rhythms at odds with the
normative.​
Willow’s loop binds the Scoobies to her own grief‑driven tempo, a tempo that ignores the arc
pacing of television drama and imposes its own: stay here until this works.​
Her loop is not just a sonic event; it is a temporal demand — a demand to inhabit her
refusal.

Queer ritual temporality: an everyday practice
In my fieldwork, queer temporality rarely appears as a grand philosophical gesture.​
It emerges in the texture of ritual life: the way a Samhain vigil runs past midnight without
anyone declaring it over; the way a rooftop Beltane lingers in conversation long after the
formal circle has been closed; the way a looped chant plays for hours, anchoring a kitchen’s
atmosphere in a present that refuses to expire.
These are small‑scale but decisive acts of temporal sovereignty.​
They refuse to let the state, the workday, or even the social script of “how long a ritual
should last” dictate the clock.​
Recursion is one of the tools that makes this possible.​
Because a loop has no hard beginning or end, it can stretch time elastically, letting
participants stay as long as they need without the anxiety of being “off‑schedule.”

Ritual time as elastic time
Catherine Bell (1992) frames ritual as a strategic suspension of ordinary life.​
It is not that ritual happens outside time, but that it happens in a different register of time —
one that allows for symbolic compression, expansion, and reordering.​
Victor Turner (1969) called this liminality: a threshold state in which transformation
becomes possible because the sequence of ordinary events has been disrupted.
Recursion intensifies this liminality by flattening temporal markers.​
If there is no obvious “start” to the chant and no natural point at which it should “end,” the
entire working becomes a continuous middle.​
This was palpable in the Parc‑Extension vigil: people drifted in late or stepped out for a
smoke and re‑entered without breaking the spell.​
The loop absorbed the movement; time inside the space did not fracture.

Ronald Grimes (2014) distinguishes between scripted ritual time — tightly structured, often
with a fixed duration — and improvised ritual time, which adapts to circumstance.​
The Willow loop produces a hybrid.​
Its sonic content is scripted — the same incantation repeated — but its duration is contingent
on the needs of the room.​
This blend gives the ritual both stability and flexibility: the loop is the fixed axis, the timing
is the improvisation.

Loop aesthetics in media culture
Jonathan Sterne’s The Audible Past (2003) draws attention to how sound reproduction
technologies alter our relationship to time.​
A loop, unlike a one‑time performance, creates the sensation of continuous presence.​
It is not merely a replay; it is an occupation of the now.​
The Willow chant, looped, doesn’t point toward an upcoming climax.​
It holds the moment in suspension, delaying the release.
Alex Gitelman (2014) describes loops as temporal dwellings — structures we inhabit rather
than pass through.​
In digital culture, loops are ubiquitous: GIFs that animate the same two‑second moment
forever, TikToks designed to restart without perceptible seam, playlists that cycle ambient
tracks indefinitely.​
These loops vary in intent: a meme loop amplifies affect, a video game loop trains skill, a DJ
loop builds tension.
The Willow loop’s function is distinct: it is ritual infrastructure.​
It doesn’t call attention to itself as content; it operates as temporal architecture.​
You don’t listen to it; you move through it.​
Its presence is felt most in its absence: when the loop finally stops, the room changes
instantly, like air pressure shifting before a storm.

Queer recursion as ritual technology
Pulling these threads together, we can see recursion as a queer ritual technology.​
It is queer because it resists normative closure, staying in the “not‑yet” even when resolution
beckons.​
It is ritual because it changes the temporal texture of the space, suspending linear sequence
in favour of elastic time.​
It is a technology because it is portable, repeatable, and adaptable — a tool that can be
inserted into any number of contexts to achieve a predictable temporal effect.

Willow’s loop is exemplary because it carries its origin’s refusal in both form and content: a
spell designed to run until the impossible happens.​
In post‑canonical life, this form can be detached from its original purpose and re‑coded to
hold open any kind of liminal space — for grief, for joy, for solidarity, for risk.

Field resonance: loops as survival
In queer and trans ritual contexts, staying in the loop is not only an aesthetic preference; it is
a survival strategy.​
Mainstream grief culture pushes for closure — to “heal,” to “move on.”​
Mainstream activism often demands progress narratives — “we are winning,” “we are
moving forward.”​
Even progressive communities valorise closure as a sign of maturity.
The loop resists all of this.​
It says: we’re not ready to close.​
It holds the door open for those who cannot yet — or will not — step into the next chapter.​
It makes time for those who are late, those who are tired, those who are not done.
This is not indulgence; it is infrastructure for staying alive in hostile worlds.​
When closure would erase pain, the loop keeps it in play.​
When closure would dissolve solidarity, the loop keeps the group tethered.

From glitch to method
The leap from Buffy to the rooftop, the kitchen, or the Zoom vigil is not merely cultural
borrowing.​
It is methodological translation.​
The glitch in Willow’s resurrection — her refusal to build a clean exit into the spell —
becomes a repeatable method: keep going until the door opens, and maybe keep going after
that.
This method can be applied to any ritual that needs to resist premature closure.​
It can hold a grief vigil in motion until the words that need to be spoken finally surface.​
It can keep a celebration alive through the awkward transition from feast to dance.​
It can anchor an online gathering in a sense of shared presence despite digital fragmentation.
In this sense, Willow is not a model for identity but for system design.​
Her recursion is executable code.​
Her loop is a spell.
