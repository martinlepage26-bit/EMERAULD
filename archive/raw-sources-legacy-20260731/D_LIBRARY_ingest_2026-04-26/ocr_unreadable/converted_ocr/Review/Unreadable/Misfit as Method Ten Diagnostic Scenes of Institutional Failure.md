**Misfit as Method: Ten Diagnostic Scenes of Institutional Failure**

**Introduction**

Failure in contemporary institutions rarely arrives as a single, legible event. More often, it accumulates through small withdrawals of attention, vague reschedulings, micro-corrections delivered through tone, and the growing sense that decisions are being made without being named. In retrospect, this kind of trajectory is commonly explained as poor fit, individual limitation, or misaligned expectations. Organizational research, however, has long shown that failure is frequently produced by systems that distribute knowledge, authority, and accountability unevenly, then render their contradictions administratively invisible (Perrow, 1984; Reason, 1990; Vaughan, 1996).

This article argues that failure is better understood as an institutional process of diagnostic sorting than as an outcome of performance. Misfit is often generated early and stabilized through mechanisms that do not resemble formal evaluation: role opacity, procedural pedagogy without rationale, affective thresholds of belonging, and silence as a mode of judgment. The argument is developed through a scene-based autoethnography of a 90-day probationary period following a hire intended to be ongoing. The empirical material consists of ten field-note scenes written during entry and the final week preceding termination. Identifying details have been omitted, generalized, or altered to protect anonymity while preserving sequence and analytic integrity.

The analysis draws on queer phenomenology and critical institutional scholarship to describe how organizations function as orienting environments. Following Ahmed (2006), institutions are treated not only as task allocators but as devices that produce legibility: they shape what kinds of bodies, affects, and communicative styles can appear “easy,” competent, and legitimate. Misfit emerges not only through deviation but through disorientation, particularly when institutional directives are followed too literally and their incoherence becomes visible. Queer theory and critical disability studies sharpen this account by foregrounding norms of intelligibility: difference is often tolerated most easily when it remains implicit and privately managed, while pedagogical clarity can be read as disruptive because it redistributes interpretive authority (Butler, 2004; McRuer, 2006).

The article also builds on scholarship on affective labor and organizational control to specify how judgment can operate without articulated standards. Institutions regulate not only outputs but tone, restraint, and the capacity to absorb ambiguity without naming it (Hochschild, 1983; Gregg, 2011; Gill, 2014). Under conditions of contraction and overload, organizations often rely more heavily on informal, deniable mechanisms of evaluation that allow judgment to operate without accountability (Crozier, 1964; Kühl, 2015; Vaughan, 1996). This lens is extended to contemporary efficiency and automation discourse, where narratives of optimization often function as managerial futurity: they reward initiative that confirms institutional coherence and penalize initiative that makes infrastructural limits explicit (Beer, 2019; Zuboff, 2019).

Treating scenes as diagnostic events rather than illustrative anecdotes, the article shows how misfit can be produced and finalized through low-intensity mechanisms: calibrated politeness, postponed meetings, rerouted communication, and procedural closure without narrative. Although the empirical setting is corporate employment, the argument is not restricted to the private sector. Similar dynamics have been documented in academic environments, where evaluation often proceeds through opaque criteria, delayed recognition, and retrospective narratives of fit (Ahmed, 2012; Berlant, 2011). Read together, these contexts illuminate how contemporary institutions can preserve legitimacy not by correcting failure, but by sorting it quietly and sealing the sorting inside procedure.

**Background and Context**

The field notes analyzed here were written from within a large corporate organization whose built environment is designed to communicate containment: controlled quiet, curated light, minimal visual strain, and a choreography of movement that suggests nothing leaks. The opening scenes unfold in the wake of major organizational cuts. This matters because contraction alters how newcomers are read: speech narrows, calm becomes performative, and ambiguity becomes costly. In such conditions, coherence is maintained less through explanation than through regulation of what becomes speakable.

I entered as a newly hired employee expected to remain beyond probation. The probationary frame is analytically central because it enables evaluation without narration. It creates a temporal asymmetry in which the institution can observe while keeping criteria partially latent, and can end the relationship without producing a substantive account. For the person being assessed, this produces a distinctive uncertainty: one is asked to perform fit without being given the terms by which fit is judged.

Early interactions made clear that legibility was not only about completing tasks but also about naming oneself correctly within an organizational grammar of acronyms, categories, and tacit hierarchies. When a role lacks stable shared meaning, that instability is easily localized in the worker, producing a fragile start that is not reducible to competence. Informal reassurances in the first week further clarified how security was being narrated. Protection circulated as a systems story (continuity, indispensability, budget logic) rather than as an offer of mentorship or developmental accompaniment. Belonging was framed less as incorporation into a relational infrastructure than as installation into workflow continuity.

Training practices reinforced this orientation. Instruction arrived in short, fragmented windows under visible overload and emphasized procedural reproduction across multiple tools. Requests for rationale were met with habitual formulations that normalized the absence of explanation. In such contexts, competence becomes legible as compliance and memory rather than understanding, and sense-making becomes private labor rather than an institutional responsibility.

Managerial expectations during probation intensified this bind. The role was framed in terms of autonomy and self-sufficiency while managerial availability remained limited. Autonomy thus operated less as support than as demand, and the absence of guidance could later be recoded as a failure of ownership. As the workflow stabilized, the role’s infrastructural character became clearer: much of the work involved maintaining continuity across systems that did not fully align through attention and verification. This labor is indispensable precisely because infrastructure is fragmented, yet it is often treated as low status because its success is defined by invisibility. During probation, this configuration creates a specific vulnerability: when systems fail, accountability can land on the human interface while infrastructural conditions remain unspoken.

Finally, the context is affective as much as procedural. The scenes unfold in an environment where difference is easiest to tolerate when it remains quiet and privately managed. When difference or critique becomes explicit and pedagogically framed, interpretive authority shifts, and in settings where ambiguity functions as a resource, that redistribution can be read as excess. Probation, in this sense, tests not only performance but the management of presence: visibility, literalism, tone, and the degree of clarity one produces in a space that depends on certain things remaining ambient.

This is the ecology within which the analysis reads the ten scenes as diagnostic events. Failure can be generated early through orientation, stabilized through deniable evaluative mechanisms, and finalized through procedure without ever needing to appear as formal judgment.

**Field Notes: Ten Diagnostic Scenes**

The following scenes are presented here as analytic data rather than retrospective narrative: each scene records a moment in which institutional expectations, affective norms, and evaluative silence became legible in practice.

*Scene 1. Week 1, Day 1, Monday morning: Entry after the cuts*

Monday morning. Glass lobby, soft lighting, controlled quiet. The badge printer hums. Faint disinfectant and coffee, that hygienic promise of control. People move with practiced efficiency, coats folded neatly over forearms, eyes forward, voices low.

I arrive early, the way you do when you are new and trying not to take up too much space. The lobby is designed to soothe. It is rich in the aesthetic sense: clean lines, minimal clutter, no visible strain. Nothing looks overtly wrong. The mood is not calm, though. It is braced, as if calm were being performed under supervision.

I introduce myself at reception and try to warm the room with ordinary friendliness. The receptionist smiles politely, says “One moment,” and types, stops, types again. Her tone remains pleasant, but her face has the stillness of someone who has been asked too much recently. It registers as procedural kindness, not welcome.

In the elevator, a woman in business casual glances at my badge and asks, “You’re starting today?” I say yes. She nods once, then adds, almost reflexively, “It’s been a week,” and stops. No elaboration. The doors open, the sentence ends, and we walk out into a floor bright enough to film a commercial.

A sentence offered and withdrawn. Calm is performed as compliance. What is most striking is not hostility but the management of what can be said, and what must remain ambient. The building reads as a machine that has recently removed people and is trying to function as if removal left no residue.

I feel the institution deciding my tolerable shape before anyone knows me. I had been told the role was essential, insulated, protected. Walking in, I feel the opposite of protection. Not rejection, something colder and more ordinary: a sorting that occurs before anyone says my name twice.

*Scene 2. Week 1, Day 1, Monday midday: The acronym that does not exist*

Onboarding room. Long tables, identical laptops, welcome kits, identical pens. A slide deck cycles through values, compliance, and belonging. Someone says, “We’re happy to have you,” with the exact emphasis of a phrase learned for unstable times.

Silence feels cruel in a room like this, so I talk. Not to dominate, but to make the situation human. Other new hires respond to me with an odd deference, as if ease reads as authority. For a moment I wonder, with a faint laugh inside my head, do they think I’m a manager.

A senior leader walks in, cheerful in the calibrated way of someone who knows they are being watched. He claps his hands once and says, “Okay, quick hello.” He moves down the table offering brief greetings that feel like scans. When he reaches me, he smiles and asks, “So what’s your role?”

It is an easy question, except it is not. I answer too quickly, then not quickly enough. I reach for the acronym that should make me legible. I say something like, “I’m a CTC.” The syllables land wrong in the air. He blinks. The smile does not disappear. It tightens.

He says, “A what, sorry?” I correct myself, but the correction is messy. I can hear myself searching in real time. He nods with professional patience that feels like a verdict delayed by courtesy. “Got it,” he says, and moves on.

After he leaves, one of the other new hires whispers, “What does that stand for?” I laugh lightly and say, “That’s a great question.” My laugh is real. So is the heat in my face. The moment is small, almost comedic. It does not feel small afterward.

The manager’s expression calibrates: the smile tightens without vanishing. A small procedural question behaves like a legibility test. The institution asks me to name myself in its grammar, and my answer arrives with an accent it cannot, or will not, translate. The scene reads as personal uncertainty, but it functions as something else: an exposure of structural opacity.

The room cools around my chair. I realize the role exists on paper but not in shared institutional meaning. I didn’t merely stumble. I exposed vacancy. Later, I understand the sharper point: the scene did not reveal my ignorance, it revealed that the institution had hired into a category it could not coherently name, then treated my inability to stabilize that name as evidence about me.

*Scene 3. Week 1, Day 2: “You’re safe” as an accounting story*

Kitchen corner, low voices, the small domestic theatre of a workplace trying to look normal. People stir tea like it is work. Someone rinses a mug with an intensity that reads like displacement. The room is bright and quiet in a way that suggests the building has been engineered to absorb emotion.

In early conversations, people reassure me. “Don’t worry,” someone says, stirring tea. “Different budget.” The word different is offered like armor, like a charm you can wear under your shirt. Another person adds, “Your role is essential.” Essential. Protected. Insulated. The story is repeated often enough that it begins to sound like policy, even though no one cites a document.

I nod in the right places. I say, “That’s good to know,” with the tone of a person trying to be easy. At the same time, my mind starts doing that quiet work it always does, listening for what is being avoided. The reassurance is too smooth. It arrives too quickly. It does not attach to any human commitment, only to structure.

Later that day, I ask a basic question about where a certain task “lives” in the organization, who owns it, who would be impacted if it goes wrong. The person pauses and says, “We just… do it this way.” There is no departmental logic offered, no rationale, only habit. I hear myself reply “Okay” too brightly, as if brightness could compensate for the absence of a map. The person smiles with relief, like my acceptance has restored order.

A check-in occurs that never becomes a real check-in. My supervisor says, “We need you to be dependable. People should come to you for answers.” The sentence is framed as empowerment. It lands like an expectation without scaffold, a demand for centrality without the conditions that would make centrality possible.

Safety is narrated in system terms: continuity, budget lines, workflow necessity. Care is displaced into accounting. The promise is not “we will support you,” but “the machine requires this function.” In that sense, yes, the role is essential. It is essential the way a missing screw is essential. You only notice it when it is gone. The institution does not protect people; it protects smooth surfaces.

I begin to hear the distinction I’m not supposed to hear: I am being installed as continuity, not welcomed as a person with a learning curve. I also feel the first pressure to perform gratitude for this “protection,” as if recognizing the story is itself part of belonging. The moment I confuse budget insulation for care, I become vulnerable to disappointment. The moment I see the difference, I become vulnerable to something else: clarity.

*Scene 4. Week 2: Training by clicking, without “why”*

Training is scheduled, then moved. Scheduled, then shortened. It happens in fragments, whenever my assigned trainer has time. It feels like borrowing a person from a disaster, like catching someone in the hallway while the building burns quietly behind them. When we finally meet, it is via screen share: the modern ritual of proximity without presence.

“Okay,” she says, “so you click here.”
She clicks.
“Then you copy this.”
She copies.
“Then you save it here. Also here.”
She toggles between systems with the speed of someone who has had to learn to survive.

I write everything down like I’m preparing for an exam. My notes become choreography. Click, copy, paste, save, log, confirm, repeat. The steps are clean. The purpose is fog.

Every few minutes, I want to ask: what is this for? Who reads it? What happens if it’s wrong? What does this protect? But the pace discourages questions. If I interrupt, I feel like I’m stealing oxygen. She is generous with her time, but her time is also clearly not hers.

I try anyway. “Just to understand, why do we record it in both places?” She hesitates. “Because… we always have.”
 “Okay,” I say, and underline the step in my notebook as if underlining can create meaning.

The more she shows me, the clearer something becomes: what is missing is not information, it is framing. No overview of the system. No conceptual map. No rationale that would let me improvise if something changes. The training assumes comprehension while withholding the conditions for it. I am being taught to execute, not to understand. I am being positioned as an interface rather than an interpreter.

When the call ends, I stare at my notes and realize they describe a ritual more than a job. The sequence is precise. The “why” is ambient. I am being trained to reproduce continuity, not to know what continuity is for.

“Because we always have” functions as a governance formula. It forecloses inquiry without forbidding it. It keeps the system intact by keeping its reasons unspoken. Understanding is treated like a personal preference, not an organizational requirement. Asking “why” risks making the infrastructure visible, and visibility is expensive.

My confidence becomes a performance of memory, not competence. I can do the steps, but I cannot explain them. I begin to anticipate future scenes where this absence will be read as a flaw in me rather than a choice made by the institution’s pedagogy. The ritual is handed to me without meaning, and I am expected to inhabit it as if meaning were obvious.

*Scene 5. Week 2, Thursday: “Be a one-person show”*

A short managerial exchange that arrives like a directive, not like a relationship. It happens between other demands, as if my existence is a tab left open in a browser full of emergencies.

My supervisor says it again, as if repetition will create infrastructure: “You need to be a one-person show.” The phrase is delivered with a tone that suggests admiration, even trust. Then she is gone. Meetings. Deadlines. Invisible pressures. Days where I hear nothing unless I initiate. When she does respond, her replies are short, functional, slightly delayed, like a system processing requests rather than a person accompanying another person’s learning.

I try to perform the role she described. I make myself available. I write messages that are careful and non-demanding. I ask for clarification without sounding like I need help. I try to become the dependable node the system claims to need.

No one comes. People route around me, not because they dislike me, but because the relational map never included me as a node. The “one-person show” becomes literal: me, alone, performing competence for an audience that does not gather.

In one meeting, someone mentions an issue with a process and I offer a suggestion, gently. The room goes quiet for half a beat. Then the conversation slides away as if I had introduced a smell. No one argues. No one engages. The suggestion simply does not attach to anything. I watch my own words fall to the floor and be stepped around politely.

Autonomy is demanded without authority, access, or shared meaning. Centrality is invoked rhetorically while withheld structurally. “One-person show” reads as empowerment, but functions as isolation. It converts organizational absence into an individual trait: if you struggle, it will be framed as your inability to be autonomous, not the institution’s choice to withhold mentorship.

The contradiction becomes my posture: radiate certainty while still learning the grammar of the place. When I cannot inhabit that contradiction smoothly, the contradiction begins to look like my personality. It begins to look like “fit.”

*Scene 6. Week 3: The work is email, over and over*

Repeated subject lines, repeated templates, multiple systems that do not align. The day acquires a rhythm that is not quite workflow and not quite trance: open one system, copy a number, verify another, paste into an email, save a record, repeat it elsewhere. The same courtesy language loops until it begins to feel like a mask that breathes on its own.

The job narrows into repetition. I send the same email structure again and again. I move data manually between tools that do not speak to each other. The work requires vigilance more than creativity, and it relies on my eyes to reconcile discrepancies that the infrastructure cannot reconcile. One database says one thing, another says something slightly different, a third requires a manual note that will never be read unless something goes wrong.

I become careful in a particular way: careful not only about accuracy, but about trace. If I make an interpretive choice, I write it down somewhere private first, as if the act of deciding is itself risky. I develop micro-rituals: check three times, then send; cross-reference before replying; re-open the attachment after saving, just to be sure it is still there. The work is not cognitively empty. It is cognitively narrow. It funnels my attention into a corridor with padded walls.

At some point I catch myself thinking: the work is not “low skill,” it is low status. It depends on tacit judgment, on noticing the small mismatch that machines cannot reconcile. Yet the organization treats that human judgment as an inconvenience rather than expertise. It wants me precise, but it does not want me interesting.

I mention, casually, that the role feels different from what the title suggested. Someone smiles sympathetically and says, “Yeah, it’s a lot of admin.” The word admin lands like a dismissal of the mind. I nod and feel my own brain trying not to protest.

Human judgment is required as glue, but not recognized as expertise. Success is measured by invisibility: the better I am, the less anyone should notice I was needed. The institution depends on my attention to keep surfaces smooth while denying that the surfaces are cracking.

My brain feels both underused and overpoliced. I begin to understand the odd humiliation of “indispensable but thin”: necessary to keep the machine from tearing, yet denied the dignity of being called skilled. I start to sense how this kind of work produces a special vulnerability: if the infrastructure fails, the human interface becomes the site where blame can land.

*Scene 7. Week 3: Disclosing my stutter*

A group meeting where introductions are also calibrations. That early social moment where the institution gathers bodies and asks them to become legible to one another. The atmosphere is polite, faintly anticipatory, as if everyone is listening for what will count as normal.

As usual, when I meet a new group, I make my disclaimer. Not because I want attention, but because I have learned what silence invites. I do it lightly, pedagogically, with humor, a small pre-emptive translation.

“Just so you know,” I say, “if you ever think I’m confused or that the line is cutting, I’m not. I’m not high, I’m not drunk. It’s just my stutter.”

People laugh warmly. Someone says, “I love that.” Another person nods with a kind of respect that feels almost tender: relief, perhaps, that there is a frame, that misunderstanding has been disarmed before it can form. For a minute, clarity functions as care. The room loosens. I feel the difference between being tolerated and being understood, and for a second I imagine this could be a place where those two overlap.

Then I look toward my supervisor. Her response is not aligned with the room. A flicker of shock, a tightening, an expression that registers not discomfort with my speech but surprise at my choice to name it. The look is brief. It stays with me longer than the laughter.

I realize later what the moment did. By naming the stutter myself, I removed a power the institution often keeps for itself: the power to interpret difference silently. I made the difference collectively intelligible, shared, and therefore less available for private diagnosis. I interrupted the institution’s right to decide what my difference “means.”

Management registers differently because the issue is not the stutter; it is that I framed it first. Institutions tend to tolerate difference best when it remains ambient and privately managed. Once difference becomes explicit and pedagogical, it redistributes interpretive authority. That redistribution can read, in some environments, as generosity or leadership. In others, it reads as excess, as a breach of the tacit agreement that workers should remain decipherable only on institutional terms.

I sense a rule taking shape: difference is acceptable as long as it stays quiet, individualized, and affectively contained. Once it becomes shared, it becomes political, even if no one uses that word. I begin to wonder what else, in this environment, is permitted only in private: illness, confusion, grief, even curiosity.

*Scene 8. Final week, Monday daytime: Team building as sorting*

Field event. Polished venue, curated agenda, “culture” talk, smiling as performance. A bright room full of people wearing the correct version of themselves. Someone high up speaks about innovation and resources and how the organization must be agile, efficient, modern. The language is uplifting in the way corporate language is uplifting: it floats above concrete conditions.

We are sorted into instrument groups for a playful performance. The framing is collaboration, connection, joy. The structure is classification. I am placed in a group where the task is musical. I have a few years of violin lessons in my past, not enough to claim expertise, but enough to recognize the body-logic of practice: listening, counting, trying again. I lean in.

I engage quickly. I ask questions. I try to animate the exercise, to bring people into the play: “Wait, are we entering here? Are we supposed to accent that part?” I laugh. I encourage. I make room. Most people remain restrained, dutiful, minimal, as if enthusiasm were a liability. They comply without inhabiting. They follow instructions with the affect of someone completing mandatory training.

I notice how their restraint is not the absence of personality so much as a disciplined neutrality. They know how to disappear into the correct tone. I, meanwhile, am visible. I am having fun. I am human about it. I am asking why the room is not more alive.

The exercise functions like an affective bandwidth test: who can stay within the tonal limits of the room. Informality is staged so evaluation can happen without being named. A “team building” ritual becomes a soft diagnostic apparatus: it asks who can participate without making participation noticeable.

I feel myself becoming too visible. I begin to read restraint not as shyness but as literacy: they know what kind of aliveness the institution can tolerate, especially now. My mistake, if it is a mistake, is not poor performance. It is a surplus of presence.

*Scene 9. Final week, Monday evening: The joke, the eye roll, the silence*

Outside the restaurant, waiting for taxis. Cold air, streetlight glare, coats and small talk. A brief moment of real laughter that is not scheduled, not branded, not attached to a slide deck. For a minute, the group feels almost ordinary.

I make a joke, mildly irreverent, the kind of line that lands among friends. It is a small defiance, not against the institution exactly, but against the idea that we must always remain professionally smooth. A supervisor rolls her eyes. She replies with a clipped half-sentence, then stops.

“Well, that’s not…” she starts, then trails off.

She does not confront me. She does not ask what I meant. She does not correct me. She simply withdraws. The rest of the evening, she speaks to me minimally. The next day, she does not greet me. The silence expands, not dramatically, but procedurally, like access being slowly revoked.

No reprimand, no meeting, no feedback. Only withdrawal. The discipline is social, not formal, and precisely for that reason it is deniable and powerful. It produces a verdict without a tribunal, a sanction without paperwork.

The silence becomes loud in my body. I learn what it means to be managed by absence: a judgment that does not announce itself, but reorganizes the relational field around you. I replay the moment not because the joke matters, but because the eye roll does. It is a micro-verdict delivered in public.

*Scene 10. Final week, Tuesday–Thursday: AI discourse, then termination by procedure*

Post-event days. Quiet calendar shifts. Emails and meetings that move without explanation. The building is no longer around me, but its logic is: the logic of scheduling, postponing, rerouting communication, turning relationships into channels.

At the event, a senior leader spoke about AI tools as an ethical imperative of efficiency: if you’re not using them, are you really using your resources well? I hear it as permission, almost as a challenge. I take it literally, the way I often take institutional speech literally when I still believe the institution wants to mean what it says.

I spend hours trying to automate my workflow. It fails because the work depends on cross-checking across inconsistent systems. It requires context, verification, a human eye. AI cannot pull coherence out of incoherence. I report this as initiative: “I tried. It doesn’t work. Here’s why.” I add, lightly, as reassurance and pride: “Good to know we can’t be fully replaced.”

I want it to read as competence. I want it to read as commitment. I want it to read as alignment with leadership’s discourse. Instead, the next part of the week feels like doors closing quietly.

A meeting with my supervisor is postponed. It moves to Monday. The postponement sits in my stomach like a first hint, not because rescheduling is unusual, but because it comes with a particular vagueness: no new agenda, no context, only delay. Then the call that happens is not the call I expected. HR is there. An unfamiliar intermediary joins, calm, almost disarmingly pleasant. I am told I am not allowed to reach out to anyone directly. I am only allowed to communicate with him. Contact is transformed into procedure.

That night, I write him to thank him and to ask about retrieving personal belongings I left behind. Soon after, I receive a mail delivery failure notice: address does not exist. Either I am blocked or the system itself refuses my message. The effect is the same. I stare at the notification as if it were absurd, then feel the absurdity settle into something colder: erasure.

Access disappears. The ending arrives as procedure: clean, polite, empty of explanation. I am not given a story, only a channel. The institution closes like a door that makes no sound, leaving me with the sensation that the decision was enacted long before it was spoken.

The organization welcomes initiative only when it confirms its narratives. When initiative exposes infrastructural incoherence, it becomes a risk. Termination operates as procedural erasure rather than evaluative conclusion: judgment is dispersed across postponements, intermediaries, and vanished contact, appearing nowhere while already fully effective.

The ending is administratively complete and narratively hollow. I am left with a particular kind of grief: not only the loss of the job, but the loss of legibility, the sense that my presence has been converted into a problem without ever being spoken as such.

**Literature review**

Work and organizational scholarship has repeatedly challenged individualized accounts of failure and “underperformance” by showing how misfit is produced at the level of organizational design, evaluative practice, and environmental condition rather than isolated incompetence. In complex systems, breakdown is often a predictable outcome of how knowledge, responsibility, and authority are distributed, especially where roles remain ambiguous and infrastructures are fragmented or loosely coupled (Perrow, 1984; Reason, 1990). From this vantage point, failure is less a deviation from clear standards than an effect of systems that make certain errors likely and certain forms of explanation unavailable.

These dynamics sharpen under restructuring, downsizing, and managerial overload. Classic organizational studies describe how institutions under strain both narrow their tolerance for ambiguity and increase reliance on tacit judgment (Crozier, 1964; Vaughan, 1996). In such contexts, evaluation frequently proceeds without explicit criteria, and “fit” becomes an administrative shorthand for the organization’s limited capacity to absorb uncertainty. Legibility itself becomes an evaluative value: workers are assessed not only on what they do, but on whether they can be read quickly, smoothly, and without producing interpretive cost.

This focus on legibility converges with scholarship on affective and emotional labor. Hochschild’s foundational account shows that organizations regulate tone, demeanor, and the presentation of self as conditions of legitimacy, not merely as interpersonal style (Hochschild, 1983). Later work on precarious and post-fordist work cultures emphasizes that such regulation is intensified when risk and disruption are displaced onto workers: affective restraint, flexibility, and self-containment become requirements of employability rather than optional virtues (Gregg, 2011; Gill, 2014). In these regimes, evaluation often operates atmospherically through attention, micro-signals, delayed response, and withdrawal rather than corrective feedback.

A further strand of organizational theory describes a shift from development to diagnosis. Kühl (2015) argues that organizations frequently stabilize early impressions into durable judgments without providing meaningful opportunities for recalibration. Misfit in this diagnostic mode becomes a trajectory: an accumulation of small, deniable calibrations that precede any formal decision. The appearance of individualized deficiency can therefore function as a managerial solution to uncertainty: sorting is faster than accompaniment.

Although this article’s empirical focus is corporate, parallel analyses of institutional life in academia illuminate the broader relevance of diagnostic governance. Ahmed’s work on institutional inclusion demonstrates how organizations can adopt the language of diversity while maintaining normative lines of belonging, producing conditional recognition that tolerates difference only when it remains manageable and non-disruptive (Ahmed, 2012). Berlant’s notion of cruel optimism clarifies how attachment to institutional promises can be sustained through deferred clarity: participation is maintained by narratives of future belonging that are resolved only after the relationship has already been foreclosed (Berlant, 2011). Taken together, these accounts support reading corporate and academic institutions as distinct settings that nevertheless share evaluative logics organized around opacity, deferral, and the privatization of difficulty.

Queer theory and critical disability studies sharpen the analysis by foregrounding norms of intelligibility that structure institutional assessments. Butler’s account of normativity clarifies how standards work most effectively when they appear natural, rendering deviation legible as personal failure rather than structural constraint (Butler, 2004). McRuer’s concept of compulsory able-bodiedness shows how competence is often defined against unspoken expectations of fluency, productivity, and comportment, allowing difference to be tolerated only when it remains individualized and privately managed (McRuer, 2006). Halberstam reframes failure as a diagnostic lens that exposes the normative assumptions embedded in institutional definitions of success and coherence (Halberstam, 2011). On this view, failure can reveal an institution’s limits: what kinds of presence it can absorb without acknowledgment, and what kinds must be quietly removed to preserve the appearance of neutrality.

Finally, scholarship on datafication, surveillance, and managerial futurity contextualizes how optimization and automation discourse functions within contemporary governance. Beer’s analysis of the “data gaze” and Zuboff’s account of surveillance capitalism show how efficiency narratives often operate ideologically, promising coherence while obscuring infrastructural fragmentation and shifting responsibility onto workers who must act as adaptive interfaces between non-integrated tools (Beer, 2019; Zuboff, 2019). When workers test such narratives literally and report their limits, the problem can become not failure to innovate, but the production of unwanted legibility: making incoherence explicit.

Across these literatures, a shared insight emerges: under conditions of contraction and saturation, institutions often govern through diagnostic sorting rather than corrective accompaniment. Failure becomes less a discrete event than a process assembled through early legibility tests, affective norms, and procedural deferral. This is why a scene-based, queer phenomenological approach is analytically useful: it treats disorientation, silence, and misrecognition as sites where institutional power becomes visible, rather than as merely private anecdotes.

**Theoretical Framework and Method**

This article approaches institutional failure as a process of diagnostic sorting rather than a retrospective judgment about performance. It combines queer phenomenology, affective labor, and critical organizational analysis to show how institutions orient bodies and attention toward “proper” lines of conduct while keeping key evaluative criteria tacit. Following Ahmed (2006), institutions are treated as orienting devices: they make certain forms of presence feel natural and “in line,” while other forms register as disoriented, excessive, or difficult to place. Disorientation is analytically productive here because it makes norms perceptible at the moment they resist being inhabited.

Queer theory and critical disability studies sharpen this account by foregrounding intelligibility as a condition of belonging. Norms of competence and professionalism often assume fluency, affective containment, and self-management; difference is easiest to tolerate when it remains implicit and privately managed, whereas pedagogical disclosure can redistribute interpretive authority and become readable as disruption rather than resource (Butler, 2004; McRuer, 2006). Scholarship on affective labor further clarifies how evaluation can operate through tone, restraint, and atmosphere rather than explicit criteria, especially under overload and precarity (Hochschild, 1983; Gregg, 2011; Gill, 2014). Organizational accounts of complexity and “normal” failure situate these dynamics within systems where responsibility is displaced onto individuals who function as human interfaces for fragmented infrastructures (Perrow, 1984; Reason, 1990; Vaughan, 1996; Kühl, 2015). Work on datafication and automation discourse contextualizes managerial futurity as a narrative that rewards initiative when it confirms institutional coherence and penalizes it when it makes infrastructural limits explicit (Beer, 2019; Zuboff, 2019).

Methodologically, the article uses a scene-based autoethnographic approach. The empirical material consists of field notes written during a 90-day probationary period in a large corporate setting, with identifying details redacted to protect anonymity. Rather than treating scenes as illustrative anecdotes, the analysis reads them as diagnostic events in which institutional orientation, affective tolerance, and evaluative silence become observable. The scenes are sequenced to track how misfit is assembled over time, often prior to any formal evaluation, and how judgment is communicated through deferral, withdrawal, and procedural mediation rather than corrective feedback (Ellis, 2004). The aim is analytic description rather than confessional narrative: to document how institutional power becomes legible in micro-events, and how “failure” is produced as an administratively resolvable outcome without being explicitly named.

**Analysis and Findings**

The analysis reads the ten field-note scenes as a single diagnostic arc: misfit is not “found” at the end of probation through performance review, but assembled early through orientation, stabilized through silence, and finalized through procedure. What matters across the scenes is less what I do than how the institution learns to read my presence, and how it withdraws interpretive generosity once that reading hardens.

The first mechanism is atmospheric pre-diagnosis. Scene 1 begins with a built environment that performs containment, while the social atmosphere performs bracing. The crucial cue is not hostility but the regulation of speech: “It’s been a week” appears and is immediately retracted. This small withdrawal is an early demonstration of institutional discipline: after disruption, the organization manages coherence by restricting what becomes narratable. The new hire enters into a space where the acceptable affective range has narrowed. In such contexts, belonging is not conferred through welcome but through calibration: your “tolerable shape” is decided before anyone learns your work.

Scene 2 makes the process explicit through a legibility test disguised as casual onboarding. “So what’s your role?” operates as a demand to name oneself inside the institution’s grammar. The error is not simply that I produce an unstable acronym; it is that my hesitation exposes that the category I’m meant to inhabit is itself unstable. The manager’s tightened smile and quick move-on matter because they show how judgment can be delivered without confrontation. What reads as my personal stumble also functions as the institution’s self-protection: ambiguity is relocated from role design onto the worker’s body. The organization keeps its categories intact by treating the person as the site of instability.

Scenes 3 through 6 show how this early misrecognition is reinforced structurally: the organization offers “safety” not as mentorship but as an accounting story (“different budget,” “essential role”), then trains through procedures without rationale, then demands autonomy without support. This produces a second mechanism: responsibility without interpretive authority. Scene 4’s “Because we always have” is not just a time-saving phrase; it is a governance formula. It teaches that asking why is unnecessary, possibly risky. The worker is positioned as interface, not interpreter: reproduce continuity without claiming conceptual ownership. Scene 5 intensifies the bind. “Be a one-person show” frames isolation as empowerment and converts managerial absence into a test of disposition. The result is a particular vulnerability: if the role is incoherent and relational infrastructure is missing, the worker who cannot inhabit contradiction smoothly becomes the problem. In other words, organizational design failures are transformed into personality narratives (“not a fit”).

Scene 6 clarifies the labor that this system actually requires: repetitive email work and manual reconciliation across non-integrated systems. Here a third mechanism appears: indispensability as low status. Human judgment is required as glue, but the institution refuses to recognize it as expertise. Success is measured by invisibility; the better the work, the less it should be noticeable that a human was needed. This is a central contradiction for diagnostic sorting: the organization depends on human attention to compensate for infrastructural fragmentation, but it prefers that fragmentation remain ambient. The human interface becomes both necessary and blameable, which makes probationary vulnerability structural rather than personal.

Scene 7 marks the turning point where misfit becomes explicitly about interpretive control. The stutter disclosure, offered pedagogically and with humor, briefly produces collective ease. But management’s different registration signals something more diagnostic than “bias against disability” in a narrow sense. The issue is that I frame my difference first. I preempt institutional guessing. I make meaning shared. That redistribution of interpretive authority is what reads as excess in a system that prefers difference to remain privately managed and silently interpretable. This is where the queer phenomenological logic is most visible: the problem is not deviation from a rule that is stated; the problem is disorientation of an unstated norm about how legibility should be produced and who gets to produce it.

The final week (Scenes 8 to 10) shows the institution moving from early diagnosis to closure through ritual and procedure. Scene 8 frames team-building as play, but functionally it acts as an affective bandwidth test. In a post-cut environment, enthusiasm is risky. Restraint reads as literacy. My visible engagement does not “fail” the exercise; it reveals the exercise’s function. Informality is staged precisely so evaluation can occur without being named. The institution can observe who stays within tonal limits, who becomes too present, who asks too many “why” questions in the wrong register. The sorting happens under the cover of cohesion.

Scene 9 shows how judgment is communicated in its most deniable form: micro-sanction by withdrawal. The eye roll and the clipped half-sentence do not matter because the joke is objectively inappropriate; they matter because they signal the shift from inclusion to managed absence. Silence becomes a disciplinary tool. There is no feedback to respond to, no criteria to contest. The worker is governed by a relational reorganization that leaves no paper trail. This is diagnostic power at low volume: it achieves compliance and removal while preserving the institution’s neutrality.

Scene 10 functions as the hinge between futurity discourse and termination. The AI talk frames optimization as an ethical imperative. I take that discourse literally, attempt automation, discover it fails because the work is precisely the reconciliation of incoherent systems, and report this as initiative. This produces a fourth mechanism: initiative is rewarded only when it confirms institutional fantasy. The organization can celebrate AI as narrative while depending on human judgment as infrastructure. When a worker tests the narrative empirically and returns with limits, they render incoherence speakable. That is the threat. Replaceability is almost secondary. The primary danger is legibility: naming the system’s dependence on human glue exposes the institution’s cracked surfaces.

Termination then arrives as the culmination of the entire arc: procedural erasure instead of evaluative narrative. Meetings shift without explanation. An intermediary appears. Direct contact is prohibited. Access disappears. Even retrieval of personal belongings becomes a dead channel. The institution ends the relationship without a story. This is not an accident; it is a governance method. A narratively empty ending prevents contestation because there is nothing to dispute except procedure, and procedure is designed to appear neutral. The result is an administratively clean exit paired with an affectively unresolved experience.

Taken together, the scenes yield a compact set of results:

1. Failure is diagnosed before performance: misfit begins with orientation and role legibility, not with measurable task outcomes.
2. In precarious environments, evaluation becomes affective: tone, restraint, and invisibility replace articulated standards.
3. Difference is tolerated when it is private: pedagogical clarity redistributes interpretive authority and becomes readable as excess.
4. Initiative is conditional: innovation is welcomed when it reinforces narratives, punished when it exposes infrastructural incoherence.
5. Termination functions as erasure: closure is achieved through procedure and withdrawal, not through explanation, producing inevitability after the fact.

This is why the probationary period reads less like a trial of competence than like a diagnostic machine: it sorts by legibility, affect, and interpretive control, then finalizes the sorting through silence and administrative closure rather than correction or repair.

**Conclusion**

This article has argued that failure in contemporary institutions is less an outcome of inadequate performance than a diagnostic process through which misfit is identified, stabilized, and resolved without being explicitly named. Drawing on a queer phenomenological, scene-based autoethnography of a 90-day probationary period in a multinational pharmaceutical corporation (anonymized), the analysis showed how misalignment emerged through role opacity, affective governance, and procedural silence rather than through articulated criteria or corrective feedback. Failure, in this account, is an institutional accomplishment that protects organizational coherence by relocating contradiction into the worker (Perrow, 1984; Vaughan, 1996).

Methodologically, the paper demonstrated the value of treating scenes as diagnostic events rather than illustrative anecdotes. Across entry after cuts, legibility tests in onboarding, procedural training without rationale, demands for autonomy under managerial absence, repetitive infrastructure-glue work, pedagogical disclosure of speech difference, team-building as affective sorting, automation discourse as futurity ritual, and termination by procedure, a consistent pattern became visible: institutions do not primarily fail to recognize competence; they fail to tolerate clarity. Initiative is rewarded when it affirms institutional narratives and neutralized when it renders infrastructural incoherence speakable (Kühl, 2015). Under such conditions, judgment does not require explicit articulation to be effective; it is conveyed through narrowed bandwidth, delayed responses, withdrawal, and the rerouting of relation into procedure.

Theoretically, the article contributes to queer phenomenology by specifying misfit as disorientation rather than deviation. Following Ahmed (2006), the analysis showed how people who follow institutional speech literally, by seeking role coherence, making difference intelligible, or empirically testing efficiency discourse, encounter the limits of institutional tolerance. These moments expose norms that remain hidden when one can move smoothly “in line,” and the friction produced by those norms is then attributed to the body that encounters it.

The paper also extends scholarship on affective labor by detailing how evaluation is enacted through tone, restraint, and silence. Affective neutrality emerged as an unspoken threshold of belonging, while visible engagement, pedagogy, and literalism were read as excess (Hochschild, 1983; Gregg, 2011; Gill, 2014). Crucially, the regulation of this excess occurred through deniable micro-sanctions rather than explicit correction, producing a form of accountability that operates without leaving a stable archive. Queer theory and critical disability studies sharpen this point: difference is most tolerable when it remains individualized and privately managed, whereas pedagogical disclosure redistributes interpretive authority and can be diagnosed as misfit rather than accommodated (Butler, 2004; McRuer, 2006).

Finally, the analysis complicates popular contrasts between corporate dehumanization and academic refuge by arguing that similar diagnostic logics operate across domains. In both settings, opaque evaluation, withdrawal of care, and retrospective narratives of “fit” can transform institutional incoherence into individualized deficiency, while institutional promises sustain attachment until clarity arrives too late to matter (Ahmed, 2012; Berlant, 2011). The broader implication is that contemporary failure is increasingly built into institutional design in contexts of precarity, fragmentation, and managerial saturation: diagnostic judgments stabilize early, travel through silence, and become administratively inevitable by the time they are enacted.

This article does not offer a reform program. Its contribution is diagnostic: to show how institutions manage difference and uncertainty by converting evaluation into procedure and making judgment deniable. What appears, in administrative language, as probation or fit functions less as development than as sorting, accomplished through the micromechanics of everyday organizational life: postponed meetings without agenda, narrowed affective bandwidth, social withdrawal in place of feedback, communication rerouted through intermediaries, access revoked as a technicality. In these sequences, procedure becomes the decision’s most durable disguise. The scenes end where institutions often end: not with an explanation, but with a closed channel. The silence is not the absence of judgment; it is one of its most reliable forms.
