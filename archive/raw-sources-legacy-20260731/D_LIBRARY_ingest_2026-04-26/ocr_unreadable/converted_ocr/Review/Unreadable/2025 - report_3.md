I believe my employer dismissed me in circumstances that raise serious
concerns of discrimination under the Québec Charter of Human Rights and
Freedoms, based on disability and health, combined with a failure to
accommodate and an abusive exercise of probationary authority. I have a
speech disability (stuttering), which constitutes a protected ground under the
Charter. During my probationary period, I disclosed this disability proactively
and pedagogically in order to prevent misinterpretation of my communication
and to facilitate understanding in the workplace. This disclosure was made
openly and calmly. My colleagues responded positively; however, my
supervisor's reaction was noticeably different in tone and demeanor. Following
this disclosure, managerial engagement decreased rather than increased, and
no discussion occurred regarding accommodation, clarification of expectations,
or support. My disability was acknowledged but not addressed through
appropriate measures. During the same period, I experienced significant health
issues that were known to management. I contracted a streptococcal infection
that caused severe physical symptoms, including widespread hives, followed by
laryngitis that resulted in a near-total loss of voice for several weeks. These
conditions directly affected my physical comfort and ability to speak. Despite
this, I remained engaged in my duties, continued learning the role, and
communicated transparently about my limitations and recovery. Despite having
explicit knowledge of these health-related constraints, my supervisor repeatedly
insisted that I be physically present in the office for training purposes. This
insistence occurred without any individualized assessment of accommodation
needs, even though my duties consisted almost entirely of email-based
administrative work performed through digital systems. The limited training I
received was procedural and could have been conducted remotely without loss
of effectiveness. No clear operational necessity for mandatory in-person
attendance was articulated, and no alternative arrangements were explored. At
one point, my supervisor sent an email to my manager emphasizing my
obligation to attend the office. I responded by clearly explaining my health
situation, the temporary nature of my limitations, and my continued commitment
to my work and training, noting that the constraints I was experiencing were
outside my control. I received no response, no clarification, and no adjustment
of expectations. Following this exchange, supervisory engagement diminished
significantly, reinforcing a pattern in which health-related disclosures were met
with silence rather than accommodation. Throughout the probationary period, I
was evaluated without meaningful supervision, feedback, or communicated
performance criteria. Although a supervisor was designated as responsible for
my evaluation, engagement was minimal. No performance concerns were ever
communicated to me, and I was not given any opportunity to adjust, improve, or
benefit from accommodation measures. Evaluation appears to have occurred

informally and retrospectively, through withdrawal and procedural closure rather
than through articulated standards. I was ultimately dismissed with the
explanation that I was "not a good fit," without examples, documentation, or
explanation. In the context of Charter jurisprudence, this rationale is particularly
concerning, as it is vague, subjective, and capable of masking differential
treatment. The timing of the dismissal, following disclosures related to disability
and health, combined with the absence of accommodation, feedback, or
transparent evaluation, supports the conclusion that protected characteristics
may have negatively influenced the employer's assessment. During the
termination meeting with Human Resources, I was informed that I was no longer
permitted to communicate directly with my supervisor or colleagues and that all
communication was to be routed through a single intermediary whom I did not
previously know. When I attempted to contact this individual in writing to
retrieve my personal belongings from the office, my message was returned as
undeliverable. As a result, I was effectively cut off from all channels of
communication with the employer immediately following termination. This
restriction on communication, combined with the absence of a functioning
contact person, reinforced a pattern in which engagement and clarification were
replaced by silence and procedural barriers, including in basic post-termination
matters. Taken together, these facts align with the elements of discrimination
recognized by the CNESST: protected grounds (disability and health), employer
knowledge, an adverse measure (dismissal), differential treatment, lack of
accommodation, and a clear temporal link between disclosures and termination.
