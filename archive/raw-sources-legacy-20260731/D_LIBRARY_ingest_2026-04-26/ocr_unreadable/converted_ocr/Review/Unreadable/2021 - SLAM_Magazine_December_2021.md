Are fandoms a modern kind
of religion?
written by Professor Carole Cusack,
Department of Studies in Religion

At first glance religions
and fandoms have little
in common. Religions are
traditionally venerable,
being founded centuries or
millennia ago, where fandoms
are contemporary, emerging
from modern capitalism
and pop culture. Religious
scriptures are respected
as the source of divine standards for human life, while fandom texts are
entertainment and fiction.
What if this perspective on religions and fandoms was wrong?

the mid-twentieth century pop cultural texts (literary
and filmic) have for many expressed what the Christian
theologian Paul Tillich called ‘ultimate concern’, that which
matters most. This shift in values enabled fiction to fulfill
the functions formerly unique to religious texts. In the
twenty-first century, religion is in retreat in the West, and
individual, imaginative personal spiritualities are on the
rise. Activities and sources that are primarily aesthetic
(visual and performing arts, reading and watching films
of fictions, immersive online games and anime) act as
alternative meaning guides that some people believe
meet requirements that were previously unique to
religion. Fictions, after all, reflect human concerns and
provoke contemplation, so individuals can create personal
meanings from the story. The most common genres that
attract passionate fans who create strong communities
are science fiction and fantasy novels and films. It is
likely that this is because they introduce audiences into
new worlds that are experientally rich and aesthetically
beautiful, so much so that they seem more meaningful
than ‘ordinary’ life.

The cult television series Star Trek attracted fans who
devotedly re-enacted scenes from the various series and
communed together at science fiction conventions where
they cosplayed characters and experienced authentic joy
in the presence of Star Trek actors. The invented language
Klingon was developed to a high degree by linguists and
fans, and deep immersion in the culture of Star Trek
facilitated the level of active participation needed for a
popular cultural event to become collectively meaningful,
a philosophy or way of life worth pursuing, preferable to
the barren late capitalist world. This fervent fandom is
similar to the approach Zell and Christie took to Stranger in
a Strange Land; they recognised that Heinlein’s novel spoke
directly to their place in the world, their understanding of
life, and also to their hopes for the future. Fiction is not
usually so impactful, but while unusual Zell and Christie’s
response was not unprecedented.

Since their origin, humans have constantly produced,
consumed, and accorded aesthetic value to fictions
(which include religious texts). Similarly, humans constantly
produce new religious forms. Religion and fiction overlap
with aesthetics and art-making, and in the secular, multifaith modern West people seek attractive and entertaining
experiences, and religious and spiritual meanings,
wherever they choose. Immersed in fictional environments,
these individuals and communities find ultimate realities
and discover profound emotional relationships within
popular culture. The religiously-laden terms “devotee”
and “cult” are often applied to fandoms, and with good
reason; imagining oneself in another world, with another
life among spiritually and technologically superior beings
with alternative philosophies engages the same cognitive
abilities that enabled humans to develop religions in the
first place. The sacred has slipped its moorings in religion
and now can be found anywhere; religion has become only
one possible source of the sacred, which now manifests in
a multitude of areas of life formerly regarded as profane,
such as sport and music, film and television, novels and
comic books.
