"Reboot": Performance, Gender, and the Computing of Identity in Postmodern Society

# Abstract

This article examines the Canadian animated series ReBoot (Mainframe Entertainment, 1994–2001) through the lens of performance theory, gender studies, and digital culture. As one of the first fully computer-animated television series, ReBoot offers a remarkably prescient allegory for identity in the networked, postmodern condition. The show's narrative architecture—centered around "games" imposed by a mysterious User, and the adaptive performances required to survive them—renders visible the mechanisms through which identity is produced, contested, and reprogrammed. This article argues that ReBoot dramatizes the cultural logic of identity as computation: iterative, relational, and contextually activated. Special attention is given to how gender roles and expressions are encoded and subverted within the series, particularly through characters like Dot Matrix and AndrAIa. Ultimately, ReBoot presents identity as neither essential nor stable, but as a programmable, performative response to power, difference, and survival within digital and social systems.
