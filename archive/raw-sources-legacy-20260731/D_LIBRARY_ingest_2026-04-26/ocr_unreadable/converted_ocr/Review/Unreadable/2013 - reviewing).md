Drawing the researcher into data: drawing as an analytical tool in
qualitative research
Abstract

In qualitative research, visual methods often entail engaging with images

as the subject of analysis. Yet, images may be of value also as a means of analysis. This
article reflects on this analytical value in relation to drawings. To this end, the authors
explore drawings made by researchers in various phases of qualitative research. Drawings
made ‘in the margin’ are put centre stage to better understand their role in data analysis.
They allow revisiting situations; and they supplement the audio-to-text act of transcribing.
Actively drawing involves and stimulates a sensory engagement with the phenomena
under study and the data. Drawings furthermore play an important role in arranging and
re-arranging concepts when formulating conclusions. Examples highlight how researchers
may explicitly incorporate drawing in data analysis to harness the potential of a
multisensory skill set and engage with transcribing in new ways.

Keywords: drawing, multisensory engagement; qualitative analysis; sketching; theory
development; transcribing; visual methods

2

Introduction
In qualitative research, visual methods often entail an engagement with images as the subject of
analysis. Yet, images may be of value also as a means of analysis. Pauwels (2010, p.561) points
out: “Visual scientific literacy manifests itself as a form of visual thinking and doing throughout
the complete research process”. The researcher(s) embrace a visual approach in the inquiry in a
way that influences the collection and analysis of empirical material. As our research pertains to
the built environment and how it is experienced we also acknowledge that drawings are
important in architecture and other design disciplines to represent artifacts before they are
constructed and mediate between world and concept “on the way toward, or away from
empirical reality” (Medway 1996, p.474). In this article we call attention to drawings made ‘in the
margin’ during research activities, including observations and analysis.
During the analysis phase, various qualitative research approaches rely heavily on
transcriptions of audio-recorded interviews. There are different transcription styles and there are
choices a researcher makes when ‘composing’ these transcripts. O’Dell and Willim (2013) put
forward the idea of the transcription as sensory. Drawing too is sensory: image-making is related
to the senses more directly than words are, accessing a deeper level of cognition(Edwards 1979
in Aarts et al. 2013); or when understood as verb and ‘trace of a gesture’ (Ingold 2013, p.128),
rather than picture or image. Similarly to how transcriptions often play a central role in a
researcher’s engagement with empirical material, we propose considering drawings as useful in
the analysis. A researcher’s drawings may be considered an expression of their relationship to
their data: their closeness to or immersion in them. The process of drawing is explored here as
leading the researcher into unexplored analytical themes and territory, a tool to connect the
collection and analysis of empirical material. Parallels may be drawn with how Cross (2006)

3

considers sketching a way (for a designer) to find unintended consequences and with drawing
employed as ethnographic method (Pink 2013; Causey 2017).
The questions guiding our study were aimed at better understanding spatial aspects in
the lived experience of people affected by cancer with the intent to (better) inform architectural
design process. As architectural researchers we were interested in the various types of spaces
patients encountered for examinations, consultations, treatments and other (health/cancer) care
and the interactions participants in our study had with hospital buildings (Authors 2019). Physical
encounters with architecture are considered (multi)sensory experiences that are integrated
through the body (Pallasmaa 2005). We subsequently align with Pink’s (2009) work that
considers sensing a whole body perception and we consider visual (or aural) recordings of built
research environments important to better understand their sensory qualities.
In this study we initially engaged with patient-made photos as visual method (Authors
2018). To investigate interactions with built care environments we looked to previous studies
employing photovoice (Wang and Burris 1997; Frith and Harcourt 2007; Catalani and Minkler
2010; Maratos et al. 2016) and photo-elicitation (Radley and Taylor 2003; Radley 2010).
However, revisiting the process of analysis made us realise that there was more to the visual
method employed. For the first author – henceforth ‘the researcher’ – drawing was perceived as
a side activity (covert and personal) in a process oriented toward reporting and producing
scientific publications. Participant-made photos were incorporated in both data collection and
dissemination, while drawing as a visual method was not an explicit part of the study design and
drawings were not an intended outcome of the research process. Drawings were made in
various situations: alongside ‘field notes’, sometimes in sensitive contexts where taking
photographs seemed inappropriate, was curbed by feelings of discomfort, or was formally not

4

allowed; also during the analysis phase, while the researcher was listening to audio-recorded
interviews and when developing materials to disseminate findings.
Drawings were made by the researcher throughout and as an integral part of the
research process. The design orientation of the study, the aim to inform the design of (cancer)
care facilities, and the first author’s personal interests and abilities led to making drawings. At
the time (over the course of four years of PhD research) it remained unclear what they did and
how they worked. Although drawing(s) play(s) an important role in architecture and other
design disciplines, the way we discuss drawings and the intent to highlight their role in
enhancing embodied understanding is a new direction of exploration. The drawings we reflect
on in this article were made in the context of a qualitative analysis roughly based on the
QUAGOL method (Dierckx de Casterlé et al. 2012) and we aim to shed light on their potential
as a tool in such analysis. We make more explicit how drawing can help explore (new)
connections and, literally, provide lines into subject matter and out to audiences/readership.
This highlights the dual potential drawing holds to complement textual coding processes and
further disseminate findings of interdisciplinary research to relevant audiences.

Context
Knowing through drawing?
The analysis of an image is commonly linked to its producer’s account of it (Harrison 2002 in
Mitchell 2006), for example, when participant-made photos serve to (co-)determine the topics
of an interview (Frith and Harcourt 2007; Maratos et al. 2016). In research with adults, drawing
has been considered a novel way to explore patient experiences of care, illness and treatment
(Guillemin 2004; Cheung et al. 2016; Eggleton et al. 2017). Drawing is also employed in

5

research with children, for example, to explore living conditions and self-image (Mitchell 2006),
care and interpersonal relationships (Eldén 2012), or children’s encounter with the urban
environment (Ramioul et al. 2020). With a critical look at the supposed child-centredness of
visual methods in general, and drawing specifically, Mitchell (2006) concludes that drawing
methods may reveal as much about the adults/researchers as about children’s drawings.
Although Mitchell’s point aims to shift thinking about analysing children’s drawings and
illustrates how ‘an adult gaze’ may contrast with the outcomes of the drawing activities, it leads
to the question what insights can be gleaned from a researcher’s own drawings.
An example of the use of researcher-made drawings is James’ (2018) use of a ‘sketch and
talk’ method in research with incarcerated women. The method supports the focus in, what is
referred to as, an ‘odd type of conversation’, zooming in on specific details of the interior and
talking about how they are experienced (James 2018, p.156). Since James is interested in
designed objects and how they relate to control of the body in prison, references to the body
and personal details are important. For James, sketches allow these references and details and
can be incorporated in research reports without sacrificing confidentiality. From an ethical
standpoint this characteristic is a basic strength of researcher-produced drawings or illustrations
employed with the intention to tell (personal, human) stories and open up new possibilities for
understanding (Haapio-Kirk 2020).
A scoping review conducted by Cheung et al. (2016) investigating how in 32 studies
drawings were used to explore patient perspectives on illness and treatment, concluded that
much remained unclear about how drawings were interpreted. Our exploration here then relates
to the possible roles drawing has in an emergent process of qualitative analysis, possibly
pinpointing instances where a researcher develops new angles and relationships to theory. When

6

qualitative research involves an ‘analysis of abduction’ as described by Alvesson and Karreman
(2011), drawings may be agents uniting applications of established theory and imaginative
articulations of observed phenomena. To focus on the analysis and the experience of doing
research is also a way to share and increase understanding about varied ways of knowing in
interdisciplinary research (Kilbourn 2013; Mackley and Pink 2013).

‘Making’ transcriptions
In qualitative analysis, transcribing interviews – putting audio into text – is often a central
activity. It is not unusual to generate and process large amounts of text in this way (Heath et al.
2016). Individual researchers make choices between literal and exact or more ‘natural’ ways of
transcribing. A literal transcription (more phonetically oriented) may include background noises,
and unfinished words or sentences, grammatical or pronunciation mistakes. A so-called natural
transcription may claim to ‘edit lightly’ in order to create a naturally flowing text without
sacrificing words or meaning (in linguistics this is considered an orthography oriented
approach).
A distinction more commonly made in qualitative research is between naturalist and denaturalist approaches to transcription (Heath et al. 2016). More in line with the literal
transcription described before, the naturalist approach aims to move beyond what was said,
emphasising how understandings are articulated. Where de-naturalist approaches do not include
idiosyncratic elements of the interview, the naturalist approach encourages researchers to find
ways to notate pauses, sounds (background or interruptive), and gestures or actions (e.g.
emphasis by tapping on the table, laughter or increased volume of speech) that are considered
relevant or important. The notation symbols used to do this are often based on the Jefferson
Transcription System (Heath et al. 2016, p.112) although researchers also develop their own.

7

This ‘putting into text’ may be seen as a practice of line making rather than an assembling words
(Gunn 2009) and as such, it is a compositional act. According to O’Dell and Willim (2013),
previous work on transcriptions has tended to stress the semiotic and cerebral exercise of
converting sound to text. They argue that attention also needs to go to transcriptions as
compositional pieces that “pull in the senses” (O’Dell and Willim 2013, p.319).
The aforementioned authors investigate the role played by the body and its senses, and
technology in the act of transcribing, and consider the outside world (the subject of research) as
moving through the scientist/researcher and onto the paper. They describe two important
factors that underlie their approach. Firstly, they illustrate that qualitative methods and theory
need to be understood as intertwined. If methods are not distinctly separate from theory, this
suggests also, that no strict distinction (in time) can be made between the methods used to bring
together data and a process of analysis that can contribute to theory development. For the
purpose of the reflection presented in this article we align with this as we simultaneously
process empirical material and draw on literature, discussions as a research team and the
researchers’ own sketch-notes, mind maps, schematics, and infographics. Secondly, the
aforementioned authors point out that researchers cannot stand outside of their research and
are “corporeally entwined in it” (O’Dell and Willim 2013, p.316). This contrasts with how
positivist-leaning researchers may question the extent to which a ‘true’ state of affairs is reflected
with findings (Guba and Lincoln 1994), and whether other researchers would achieve similar or
even identical results. O’Dell and Willim’s view on transcription foregrounds the bodily activity
and allows us to blur the distinction between texts and drawings, furthering the notion of
research as craft (Alvesson and Kärreman 2011) and placing researchers “as participant in

8

amongst a world of active materials” (Ingold 2013, p.21). Ingold (2013, p.31) describes a
collaboration or “correspondence” with materials as follows:
In the act of making the artisan couples his own movements and gestures – indeed his
very life – with the becoming of his materials, joining with and following the forces and
flows that bring his work to fruition.

In doing so, Ingold articulates an interest in the interaction with material to understand
what it can do, in contrast to a scientist’s desire to know what it is. Ingold’s description may
also be interpreted as a way to express that the artisan is completely emerged and ‘one’ with
their material. What is becomes an ontological question, as the material and the artisan are
not separable. Also, when referring to a correspondence between researcher and empirical
material, this raises for us the question of, not so much what the material does or is, but
what the interaction means, and what becomes in the relationship.

Methods
To better understand the role of drawings in the analysis of data, we put drawings made by the
researcher center stage here.. They were not incorporated as a method in the study design and
the reflexive exercise to look more closely at them was initiated retrospectively. In the study
participants were people affected by cancer, and interviews were held with patients and their
informal and professional caregivers to better understand spatial aspects in their experience of
healthcare facilities. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and, as mentioned, analysed roughly
following the QUAGOL method (reported on elsewhere, see Authors 2019), which consisted of
an iterative process of coding, category development, memo-writing, and discussions within the
research team.

9

Subsequently, research notebooks are scanned for drawings. Graphic field notes were
made during and immediately after visits to the different hospitals where patient participants
were treated and the settings where interviews were conducted. Sketched transcriptions were
made while the researcher was (re-)listening to 30-90 minute audio recordings of interviews.
This generally resulted in an A4 sheet of sketched transcriptions per interview (e.g. Figure 1).
Other types of drawings (sketch-notes, plans, schemes, mind maps, doodles etc.) were made
whilst reflecting on, processing and analysing the data, and alongside the presentation of
findings.

Figure 1

We do not attempt to ‘extract knowledge’ from the drawings that were made. Rather
than looking into a ‘field of inquiry’ from the outside, what we attempt involves a turning
towards the world to learn from the activities we engage in. The aim of presenting (a selection

10

of) drawings is not to visually present research participants’ narratives. The quality of the
drawings is also not pertinent (see Hannah Gravestock’s (2010) discussion on the contextdependency of the required standard of drawings employed in research). Rather, by exploring
the researcher’s perspective and acknowledging the pleasure she had in producing drawings
while engaging in the research process, we identify and develop a number of themes, and frame
them in relation to experiences that exist beyond her own.
In order to enhance the reliability of this reflexive and interpretive exercise the
perspective of the researcher is continuously checked with the other authors, who as supervisor
and co-supervisor of the PhD research were nearby (in physical and substantive ways) and
actively involved throughout the research. This article being a result of a collaborative effort is
also the main reason that the researcher is referred to in the third person.

Findings
Made by a researcher with a background in geography and interior architecture, the drawings
shuttle between the logic and accuracy of spatial representation and architectural plans on the
one hand, and investigative and interpretive meanings on the other. They shift the researcher’s
focus away from the word-based analysis. In this section we present various ways in which the
drawings can shed light on the process of analysis. First the focus is on the role they played in
note-taking. Here we consider the sensory nature of noticing and how drawings were used to
record observations at different stages of engaging in research activities. This puts forward the
way the researcher’s subjectivity takes in place on the paper in the analysis, and the value of
multisensory qualities the drawings capture. Then, theory development and communication are
touched on as roles that are more directly linked to the spatial nature of the research topic.

11

Note-taking
In interactions with participants and the places where interviews were conducted, distinctive
situations or objects were recorded as non-verbal ‘alerts’. The researcher sketched situations
when taking a photo was not deemed appropriate and/or when something could be more
accurately articulated through drawing. Combined with circumstance (intention, accident,
pragmatism, ambition) the act of drawing itself advanced the researcher’s thinking. The
drawings were used to record observations layered with sensory information. Due to the
selective nature of the process some things were highlighted and others underplayed.
Afterwards, these graphic field notes could jog a memory, reconnect the researcher to a specific
situation or bring to mind details.
Some drawings were ‘reference points’ as they amplified insights while serving as
‘touchstone’ during the analysis. They were often made on location immediately after an
interview had been conducted. In an example of a big, comfortable chair on wheels (Figure 2),
the drawing ‘lift it out’ of the room. A photo would not have shown as clearly what is
synthesised with the drawing. While talking to the head nurse of this particular outpatient clinic,
the researcher learned that the chairs need to meet various criteria. The chair was drawn
alongside notes made immediately after this conversation. The drawing elevated an idea: no
matter the choice of chair, they need to be comfortable for patients receiving chemo and they
need to be moved regularly in order for cleaners to be able to do their work. As the analysis
progressed, examples in different healthcare facilities seemed to suggest that the comfort and
experience of different types of users lead to design considerations that may be challenging to
combine.

A particular group of drawings consisted of many small sketches (see e.g. Figure 1).
Alongside making more typical transcriptions of audio-recorded interviews, these sketched
transcriptions, made while listening to audio-recordings, was another way the researcher
engaged with what participants said, building an embodied connection with them. Multisensory
details included in the pictograph-style drawings illustrate comments participants made about
how they were affected by sounds, smells, lights, objects and/or walking distances or routes. A
description of a reclining chair (Figure 1, lower left) included details of the direction of
movement, the resulting sound and the patient’s surprise at this chair’s positioning, such that it
would always bump against the wall. These sketches seem to complement the text-only
(naturalist) transcripts. As such, the drawings link to other empirical material.
Participants talked about their experience changing as familiarity with the hospital
increased but also as their physical condition declined. The examples presented in Figure 4
highlight issues of mobility and motility at different scales, as expressed in interviews with three
patients and their relatives. The researcher’s abstractions of the routes spoken about suggest a
certain inefficiency or indirectness that can be linked to the physical or mental challenges of the
experience. Many spatial aspects of importance in the experience of people affected by cancer
were related to the themes of first encounters and routes and transitions (see also Authors 2020).
Taken together the drawings and these developing themes led to an understanding of
interactions with the built and material environment changing over time as they were mediated
by patients’ changing bodies.

Language-related aspects added a personal dimension too. Interviews were held both in
English and in Dutch (also reflected in Figure 1), with the Flemish Dutch spoken by most
participants (in Belgium) differing from the mother tongue of the Northern Dutch researcher
(from the Netherlands). The task of coding incorporated these linguistic differences, reflecting
them in the resulting annotations and themes. Visualizing ideas during a ‘desk phase’ of the
research was then also a way to find common ground and transcend linguistic differences
between participants’ and researcher’s language use in an iterative process of integrating
concepts into a response to research questions. The focus was on gaining understanding of the
role of the built environment in the lived experience of people affected by cancer. In this

15

process, drawing, by being somewhat separate from particular language use, supported abstract
thinking.

Theoretical development and the 'relatives' of drawings
Arranging and re-arranging codes in ‘coding trees’ as a kind of mind map is a common analytical
strategy, particularly when a conceptual framework is generated systematically to produce an
inductive theory as is the aim with Grounded Theory (Glaser and Holton 2004; Dierckx de
Casterlé et al. 2012). In our research, paper and digital environments were used in parallel to
draw and develop relationships between concepts. Depth was added to schemes showing
conceptual relationships and in some cases initial relationships first drawn in pencil were
overlaid with a revised version in ink, the paper retaining something of the development over
time.
For an example that relates directly to the spatial nature of the research topic, we turn to
a step in the analysis where findings were related back to existing literature and theory. This
phase may be referred to as ‘abductive’, suggesting the new angles and relationships that may be
added to interpretations (Alvesson and Kärreman 2011; Timmermans and Tavory 2012). Figure
5 illustrates a process of visualising and combining existing theory. Two different ways of
thinking about hospital spaces are juxtaposed in a schematic plan. The researcher overlaid these
with a more conventional way of thinking about space in architectural practice. The sketched
plan that these theories are brought into, or applied to, is that of a hospital unit familiar to the
researcher. This exercise led to a nuanced understanding of perspectives on and contingencies
regarding hospital corridors. These spaces within (cancer) care facilities are prominent in the
experience of outpatient care while considered secondary or ‘backstage’ from prevailing clinical
and architectural or design perspectives (Authors 2021).

Communication
A concluding step of the analysis involved deciding about what formats dissemination should
take on. Months after having created sketches while listening to the interviews (e.g. Figure 1),
the researcher made compositions of drawings as part of an attempt to synthesise insights
(Figure 6). These formed part of the response to the central research question and convey
aspects of context that support the more explicit themes put forward in the textual descriptions
of the research results. The concrete incentive to do this visually was to disseminate results of
the research to practicing architects. This was among the aims of the research project, and as an
intermediary step there was an opportunity to develop a test within a master of architectural
engineering course.

17

In order to stimulate students to work with the results of the research, drawings were
made to accompany textual descriptions intended to help designers focus on the user.
Conveying the complexity of the results with a clear visual was comparable to formulating
findings in a coherent manner. These drawings were essentially fictional collages, fully rooted in
the empirical material.

Discussion
A sensitive context
In the research process reflected upon, various characteristics related to the sensitive
nature of the research context offered incentives to engage with drawing. Firstly, the interest in
the interaction with hospital buildings required the researcher to be open to information of a
bodily and sensory nature. Drawing formed a ‘way in’ to connect with this information in the

18

empirical material, being more closely related to the senses than words (Edwards 1979 in Aarts
et al. 2013). Drawing may be considered a ‘sensory noticing’: the activity supports the
development of a sensory awareness (Gravestock 2010) and an embodied conversation with
materials (Robson 2020). This aligns with how in architectural design particularly free-hand
sketches have been found effective to support abundant expression of sensory experiences
(Third Author 2014). Also, a drawing (as noun) provides a direct link to the situational
circumstances that it was made in (Hendrickson 2008) where the marks made can highlight and
provoke thoughts about particular aspects of an experience (Hendrickson 2008; Causey 2017).
The second point relates to healthcare contexts as ethically sensitive when incorporating
methods to convey visual (e.g. photographic) or multisensory (e.g. videographic) information.
Comparable to one of the strengths of James’ (2018) sketch and talk method, drawings allow
both personal detail and confidentiality. The different types of note-taking illustrated with our
findings may be put forward as a rich sensory approach that could help respond to ethical
committees’ procedural restrictions or concerns (see e.g. Tutenel et al. 2019) that push for the
pseudonymisation of both people and buildings. A sensory engagement in a sensitive context
may furthermore confront researchers with personal challenges (cfr. Benoot and Bilsen 2015;
Groeneveld et al. 2019). Incorporating drawing techniques while conducting research in
healthcare contexts may be valuable to consider as a personal outlet or coping strategy.

Drawing as own and alien
For the researcher the drawing seems to be both own and alien. Own, firstly, as the researcher’s
subjectivity (Alvesson and Kärreman 2011) takes in place and claims an interpretive space on
the paper. Prestructured understandings, theoretical background and researcher-research subject
dynamics come together. Drawings are an expression of a very personal correspondence (Ingold,

19

2013) with the empirical material. Having the person of the researcher embedded in the analysis
with their drawings perhaps supports an understanding of the researcher’s physical engagement,
their movements and gestures, along with all aspects of their person, colluding in an act of
making a scientific analysis. In the non-verbal engagement of drawing, not all inferences are
known or defined (Koulidou et al. 2020) and yet the drawing facilitates a first step towards
articulating new ideas and making these tangible. Secondly, drawing as method may be
considered as requiring certain skills and abilities (even though Causey (2017) argues it is a
method available to everyone). Therefore, as with other methods, developing and applying
drawing skills is related directly to and dependent on the (individual) researcher.
The drawings in this study are personal and somewhat indistinct. As such, they are a
means to explore the unknown edges of the researcher’s own knowing (Hendrickson 2008;
Gravestock 2010; Causey 2017). If we look again at the top example presented in Figure 2,
recording by drawing is here what Aarts et al. (2013) describe as a way of creating a relationship
with an object. It is a new relationship and a new idea. Cerwonka and Malkki (2007, in
Hendrickson 2008) introduce the notion of ‘tacking’ in their writing about the nature of
theorizing and how this relates to ‘fieldwork’, or what we refer to here as the empirical material:
a moving back and forth between familiar and unfamiliar; theoretical insights and empirical
discoveries. The types of drawings that support theoretical development have a rough and
sketchy quality to them. For the researcher they allowed ‘tacking’ between verbal and visual too.
Schematics – drawn while thinking – are considered not ‘firm’ enough and possibly not worth
the challenge or time investment to replicate in digital environments as figures to support
dissemination of findings. For example, when a schematic is given a third dimension with
certain concepts placed in an influential ‘background position’ or when an initial relationship

20

first drawn in pencil is overlaid with a revised version in ink, the paper retains something of the
development over time. This resonates with Gravestock’s (2010) work on capturing movement
in drawings and what she refers to as ‘re-drawing’ (p.202) with an initial analysis and a secondary
drawing overlaid in one picture. This illustrates how ‘tacking’ exercises may be expanded with a
temporal (and in other cases, spatial) dimension, visually condensing these aspects in drawings
for emphasis or further analysis.
These insights have implications for software commonly used for Computer-Assisted
Qualitative Data Analysis Software (CAQDAS, e.g. Nvivo or Atlas). There may be
opportunities to add depth and layers to coding trees, or support hybrid ways of working with
and enhancing imported drawings within these programs. Specifically for researchers who have
or want to develop drawing skills, this article provides suggestive evidence of drawing as useful
to supplement text-based analysis. In relation to the aforementioned QUAGOL method
(Dierckx de Casterlé et al. 2012), the examples shown in this article can be linked to stages 8 and
9 where a framework of linked concepts is visualised. Also, in relation to creating a theoretical
model (stage 10), it could be interesting to further explore what recommendations can be
formulated for researchers attempting to incorporate drawing into analysis practice.
The ‘alien’ is perhaps what Alvesson and Karreman (2011) refer to as the inconsistencies
or ‘breakdowns’ in the process of analysis that draw a researcher into their empirical material.
What does not align with your expectations? What are the inconsistencies? What stands out as
interesting? Alvesson and Kärreman label “friction as productive force” (2011, p.16) and we
suggest that drawings can be a partner in identifying these ‘breakdowns’ in a critical dialogue
between researcher and empirical material. Drawing as a tool can be employed to break from a
particular way of ordering the world and incorporate alternative vocabularies. Again, it is

21

important to approach the analytical process as a personal one where the best that we can do is
“lace our claims with caution and locate cautions within frames that are as analytically sound as
they are evocatively rich” (Freeman 2015, p.927).
A point for further exploration concerns the potentials and pitfalls of researchers’
collaborations with professional artists and illustrators. This is increasingly common in
anthropology (see online exhibition for examples: Illustrating Anthropology 2020). With the
involvement of another person, identifying personal influence and exploring inconsistencies,
likely becomes increasingly difficult. Furthermore, visual communication about experiences of
the built environment (social and material aspects relating to atmospheres, everyday affects and
materialities) is sensitive to subtle variations. How the dynamics between a researcher and an
artist affect the outcomes of their communication about these phenomena requires further
research.

Drawn towards participants and into data
A final comparison may be relevant to better understand how drawing is employed. It concerns
an example of design research where empathy is explicitly engaged and sketching is used to
scaffold trust and mutual understanding between researcher and participants (Koulidou et al.
2020). It is referred to as a dialogical sketching method as both researcher and participant draw
in an iterative and longitudinal process. Koulidou et al. describe using the drawing method to
gain insight into participants lived experience. However, it is an in-between phase as they
explore design opportunities to create personal and meaningful digital artefacts. The visual
imagery is not an artistic outcome in and of itself. Similarly, the combinations of sketches and
text as shown in Figure 4 demonstrate a synergy between the verbal/textual and visual,
employed as tool to further the analysis. The sketches draw the researcher into the data, while

22

simultaneously drawing ideas together. They partly functioned, for the researcher, as a response

Concluding remarks
Drawing was found useful as a way to connect with multisensory information in the empirical
material that was referred to in this study. Firstly, the personal nature of the drawings embeds
the researcher in the analysis. By looking closely at the role of drawing in the analysis process, it
may be helpful to think of the researcher as making a scientific analysis. Secondly, the drawings
made while visiting research settings and transcribing interviews amplified insights that were not
necessarily verbalised at the time they were made. Thirdly, the drawings played an important
role as points of reference committing sensory details to the researcher’s memory. Particularly in
relation to design/architectural research, we offered examples of bringing spatial information
together with theory in schematic plans also touching on objectives related to dissemination that
provided incentives to draw. We went on to identify a number of ways in which drawing may be
used to supplement text-based analysis techniques, providing examples of implications this may
have for qualitative software analysis and for researchers working in sensitive contexts.
Explicitly incorporating drawing in qualitative analysis has the potential to harness a
multisensory skillset and engage with transcribing in new ways. The embodied act of drawing
may be a way to develop a vocabulary closer to the people central to a study like this one, their
interactions with and movements within the built environment.
