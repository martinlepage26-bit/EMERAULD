Course description:
This course will explore the diversity of Canadian identity markers within novels representing minorities
and marginalized characters. It aims to better understand the relationship between culture and power
within literary representations of identity. The course’s approach will account for the influence of social
norms on the way narratives of the self and community are constructed. It will also allow students to
create bridges between literature and social sciences through the lens of intersectionality, which
accounts for the multiplication of discrimination and oppression in regards to marginalized identities.
The course will begin with an introductory session on the merging of literature and intersectionality. It
will comment on the pertinence of a cross-disciplinary approach to explore themes and narratives around
social power relations in terms of identity, gender, sexuality, class, and ethnicity. Two sessions will be
spent on each novel (exact pages to be determined), providing insight as to how those social categories
are put into narratives and carried out within a particular context. The first session will examine the
sociohistorical context in which each novel was written, and the main themes coming out of them:
authenticity, sense of belonging, community, self-exploration and self-expression, negotiations with and
resistance to social norms, etc. The second will take a look at the way social categories shape literary
representations of Canadian experience. Each work will serve as a locus to observe how identity
(cultural, gender/sexual, race, class, First Nations/Indigenous, etc.) is constru(ct)ed and represented
through multiple influences, from family and community, to ethics and politics, as well as larger social
norms.
Attendance and participation:
It is expected that you attend and be on time for all classes. Failure to attend class will adversely affect
your grade, since participation in the classroom discussions are vital to the course.
Assignments and grading:
Grading will be based on: a take-home mid-term exam, covering the first half of the term (one-page
answers to three (out of five) questions); two ten-question quizzes will serve to monitor your
engagement in the reading and the course content; a cumulative final exam, (two-page answers to three
(out of five) questions); a ten-page final paper, on a topic of your choice (approved by the instructor five
weeks before the deadline). The quality of your writing will account for 20% of each grade. The final

10% of the course grade will reflect the level of your active participation in class (regular attendance,

Take-home mid-term exam: 20%

Intellectual Integrity:
The integrity of your academic work and intellectual thought is valued and respected in our class and at
the University of Vermont as a whole. Plagiarism comes in many forms and will not be tolerated by any
definition. Please learn about the various policies and consequences by reading UVM’s “Code of
Academic Integrity” on the university website. This is an important first step in becoming a responsible
member of UVM’s academic and social community.
The Writing Center:
The Writing Center at UVM provides valuable feedback for all students at every stage of the writing
process. Please familiarize yourself with its locations and policies so that you can utilize this incredibly
valuable resource.
