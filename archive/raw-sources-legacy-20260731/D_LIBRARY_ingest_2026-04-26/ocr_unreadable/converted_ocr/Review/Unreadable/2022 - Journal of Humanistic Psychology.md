# When Threat Affirms: Expanding Self-Affirmation Theory Through the Asian-White

# Multiracial Experience

Abstract

While existing literature on self-affirmation has significantly advanced our understanding of

its protective function, relatively little attention has been given to the nature and role of the

threat itself. To address this gap, the present collaborative autoethnographic study explores

how the second author’s Asian-White multiracial identity—initially experienced as a threat to

her sense of self-integrity—ultimately became a source of meaning and empowerment,

leading to a self-affirming resolution. Over the course of two months, she worked with the

first author (a faculty advisor) to analyze her essays and verbal reflections collected during a

10-week research project previously conducted with six other Asian-White multiracial

college students. The findings suggest that, despite the threat posed by her dual minority

status, she came to embrace her racial identities in a way that freed her from the need to

conform to a singular category. This paper highlights how identity-based threat can play a

central and transformative role in the process of self-affirmation.

Keywords: self-affirmation, threat, Asian-White multiracial identity, collaborative

autoethnography

# Introduction

According to self-affirmation theory, individuals can restore a sense of self-integrity

when it is threatened by affirming values in domains unrelated to the source of the threat

(Steele, 1988). For instance, a person facing a damaged reputation at work may reaffirm their

self-worth by focusing on their role as a devoted parent. Although this aspect of their identity

is unrelated to their professional domain, emphasizing their value as a caregiver can help

maintain a coherent and positive self-concept, thereby mitigating the impact of the threat.

Many scholars across disciplines such as education and health have investigated the

benefits of self-affirmation (Binning et al., 2021; Creswell et al., 2007; Critcher & Dunning,

2015; Crocker et al., 2008; Niles et al., 2016; O'Brien et al., 2021; Sherman et al., 2013;

Łakuta, 2023). Using writing as an intervention, these studies have shown that reflecting on

one’s core values or positive traits can help buffer against threats to specific domains of the

self and preserve overall self-integrity. By focusing on alternative, affirming domains, such

as personal relationships or moral values, individuals can reduce the perceived importance of

the threatened area, thereby sustaining a holistic sense of self-worth.

While this body of literature has significantly advanced our understanding of the

protective function of self-affirmation, relatively little attention has been paid to the nature of

the threat itself. Often overshadowed by the emphasis on affirming alternative domains, the

threat—the very catalyst for the self-affirmation process—has remained under-theorized.

Without a clear understanding of how individuals perceive and experience such threats, our

grasp of how self-affirmation functions remains incomplete.

To address this gap, the present collaborative autoethnographic study examines the

role and characteristics of threat in the self-affirmation process. In this study, an Asian-White

multiracial college student (the second author) reflects on how her multiracial identity was

3

initially experienced as a threat to her sense of self-integrity. Working collaboratively with a

faculty mentor (the first author), she explores how this identity ultimately became a source of

meaning and empowerment, leading her toward a self-affirming resolution.

# Asian Multiracial Identity

Although much of the research on multiracial identity has traditionally focused on

Black-White individuals, scholarship on Asian-White multiracial identity has gained

increasing attention in recent years (Strmic-Pawl et al., 2022). This growing interest reflects

demographic changes in the United States: Asian-White individuals now represent the

second-largest two-race, part-White population, accounting for 22%. Additionally, about

three in ten newlywed Asians marry someone of a different race, with 21% of Asian

American and Pacific Islander (AAPI) men and 32% of AAPI women having White spouses

(Strmic-Pawl et al., 2022).

Much of the existing research on Asian-White multiracial individuals has focused on

the question of which racial group they tend to "move into" (Strmic-Pawl et al., 2022, p. 5).

This body of work highlights the in-between status of Asian-White multiracials and examines

how they choose one racial identity over another, as well as the factors influencing that

choice. Some studies report that Asian-White individuals often identify as White, feeling a

greater sense of commonality with White people and perceiving that others view them as

more White (Harris & Sim, 2002; Lee & Bean, 2007; Saenz et al., 1995; Xie & Goyette,

1997). For example, Lee and Bean (2007), analyzing data from the 2000 U.S. Census, found

that many White–Asian multiracials identified as White. In contrast, other studies show that

Asian-White multiracial individuals are more likely to identify as Asian (Childs et al., 2021;

Chong & Song, 2022; Davenport, 2016; Khanna, 2004; Strmic-Pawl, 2016). For instance,

4

Davenport (2016) found that 36% of respondents reported feeling closer to being Asian,

while only 11% felt closer to being White.

The reasons behind these contrasting identity selections are often linked to

individuals' lived experiences with the respective racial groups. Strmic-Pawl (2016) found

that Asian-White individuals who observed or internalized a sense of White superiority—

often reinforced through social interactions—tended to identify more closely with being

White. In a related way, Merrell et al. (2024) found that experiences of discrimination against

one’s Asian background could lead individuals to more strongly identify as Asian. This

finding complicates Ellemers et al.’s (1993) notion of “membership permeability,” which

suggests that individuals are likely to disassociate from lower-status groups and align

themselves with higher-status ones. Merrell et al. (2024), however, argue that many Asian-

White multiracials do not follow this pattern; even when they perceive the White group as

accessible, experiences of discrimination can lead them to embrace their Asian identity.

While these studies offer valuable insights into racial identification patterns and social

influences, an increasing number of scholars have critiqued the binary framing that dominates

much of the research (Merrell et al., 2024). As Mengel (2001) notes, this binary approach

often implies that multiracial individuals are fragmented beings. By focusing primarily on

external influences and identity selection, such studies tend to reinforce outdated notions of

blood quantum and fractioned identities—ideas that portray multiracial individuals as “less

than a whole person” in a race-conscious society (Mengel, 2001, p. 101). In response, a

growing body of literature calls for greater attention to internal processes and non-binary

frameworks in the study of Asian multiracial identity (Shih et al., 2019). As Chong and Song

(2022) argue, there is a need for more nuanced, fine-grained studies that explore the internal,

psychological mechanisms through which Asian multiracial individuals negotiate their

identities outside of externally imposed social categories.

5

This study responds to that call by exploring the internal processes of identity

negotiation experienced by Julie (the second author), an Asian-White multiracial college

student. Specifically, it investigates how she navigated feelings of disconnection stemming

from her racial background and how these experiences ultimately contributed to affirming her

sense of self. By focusing on how internal factors support the construction of a whole and

integrated identity—and examining the role of racial background as a threat in this process—

this study aims to contribute to the broader discourse on multiracial identity and self-

affirmation.

# Self-Affirmation Theory

Self-Affirmation Theory is a psychological framework that explains how individuals

maintain the integrity of the self when it is threatened (Steele, 1988). The theory posits that

when one domain of the self is challenged, individuals can uphold their overall sense of

integrity by affirming another domain that is unrelated to the threatened area but personally

valuable. For example, an employee doubting their workplace capabilities might restore self-

regard by reflecting on their role as a caring parent or the importance of family. Similarly, a

smoker facing social criticism about smoking may cope by striving harder for a promotion at

work. Underlying these examples is the principle that a threat to one aspect of identity does

not wholly define the self; threats to any single domain do not necessarily damage the overall

perception of the self (Critcher & Dunning, 2015).

Extensive research on self-affirmation has been conducted across fields such as

education and health, often focusing on interventions that involve writing about positive

personal qualities to buffer threats to self-regard (Binning et al., 2021; Creswell et al., 2007;

Critcher & Dunning, 2015; Crocker et al., 2008; Niles et al., 2016; O'Brien et al., 2021;

Sherman et al., 2013; Łakuta, 2023). For instance, Critcher and Dunning (2015) examined the

6

effects of self-affirmation on self-worth. In their study, American undergraduates first

completed a self-esteem measure and were then randomly assigned to either a self-

affirmation condition, where they wrote about their most important value or skill, or a control

condition without this task. Results showed that participants in the self-affirmation group

reported higher self-worth after completing a challenging cognitive task compared to the

control group.

As this literature review indicates, self-affirmation positively influences well-being,

self-control, and related outcomes. However, an important gap remains: the role of the threat

itself has received limited attention. Most research centers on how individuals uphold their

integrity, while the experience and nature of the threat that initiates self-affirmation have

been largely overlooked. To address this gap, the present study investigates how Julie’s

Asian-White multiracial background—often socially judged as a threat—shaped her process

of identity negotiation. It explores how she engaged with diverse aspects of herself and

ultimately defined a coherent sense of self through fluid identity negotiation.

Methods

## Collaborative autoethnography

Autoethnography, as implied by the term itself—auto (self), ethno (culture), and

graphy (analysis)—is a qualitative method in which individuals analyze their personal

experiences within a cultural context and connect these experiences to existing literature and

theory (Ellis, 2004; Ellis et al., 2011). This approach aims to integrate personal narrative,

cultural interpretation, and scholarly analysis in a seamless and reflexive way.

Despite its strengths, autoethnography has faced criticism for the perceived imbalance

between its components. From the “graphy” (i.e., analytical writing) perspective, critics argue

that autoethnography can become overly personal or anecdotal, lacking analytic rigor

7

(Watson, 1995). Conversely, from the “auto” perspective, some argue that the method loses

artistic expression when constrained by academic conventions (Gingrich-Philbrook, 2005).

As a result, autoethnography has often been viewed as methodologically inadequate for

identity research from both artistic and scientific standpoints.

However, recent studies employing collaborative autoethnography—particularly those

involving student-faculty partnerships—have demonstrated the method's potential to address

these concerns (Bloom et al., 2021; Martin & Garza, 2020; Segú Odriozola, 2023; Vang et

al., 2023). In such collaborations, students contribute authenticity and emotional depth

through first-person narratives, while faculty members provide theoretical framing and

methodological structure. As Martin and Garza (2020) emphasize, this approach recognizes

students as knowledge holders capable of expressing their experiences artistically and

positions them as co-researchers, not merely subjects.

In this study, the authors follow Martin and Garza’s (2020) model of collaborative

autoethnography to explore multiracial identity through the shared efforts of an

undergraduate student (second author) and a faculty member (first author). The student offers

lived experience and cultural insight through storytelling, while the faculty member situates

these narratives within relevant theoretical frameworks and scholarly discourse. This

partnership aims not only to bridge the gap between art and science but also to foster mutual

growth and critical reflection through the research process.

## Participants

- Julie

I am the oldest child of a Korean mother and a White Jewish father. Both of my

parents were born and raised in the United States, and both of their families immigrated

within the last century. The combination of my close relationships with my mother’s family

8

and my regular attendance at a local synagogue from a young age allowed me to explore the

distinct traditions and values of the contrasting communities I was part of.

Despite these efforts to find community, the constant rejection I encountered was

deeply frustrating. This sense of exclusion not only motivated my participation in this study

but also reflects the broader arc of my journey through higher education. My inability to fully

understand myself early on sparked a profound interest in understanding others. At a

Northeastern liberal arts college, I study anthropology, psychology, and education for exactly

this reason.

I initially joined Youngji’s study hoping to find a community of other biracial Asian

students I could connect with. While I didn’t find that, I did find something equally valuable:

a sincere sense of self-confidence in my own identity and lived experiences—something I

hadn’t allowed myself to fully explore before. When Youngji invited me to join the project as

a partner, I was extremely grateful. I’ve carried anxiety about being misrepresented and

misunderstood for much of my life, and being asked to collaborate made me feel that my

efforts to articulate my experiences with identity and belonging were genuinely meaningful.

- Youngji

As someone who has personally struggled with anxiety, I have long been interested in

how perceived threats can play a transformative role in personal growth. In a previous

autoethnographic study (first author, 2025), I explored how my experience with anxiety

became a catalyst for self-exploration. Rather than viewing anxiety solely as a limitation, I

began to see it as a motivator—an internal threat that prompted me to discover and cultivate

other, more positive dimensions of my identity.

Initially, my belief in the constructive potential of threat was shaped solely by my

own experience. That changed when I met Julie during a research project on Asian

9

multiracial identities. As an Asian male faculty member at a liberal arts college in the

Northeast U.S., I recognized that Julie and I differed in many ways. Yet, I was struck by the

deep resonance between our experiences. Her journey—navigating her identity as a "dual

minority" (Shih et al., 2019, p. 2) and confronting self-doubt to discover a more integrated

sense of self—mirrored my own path in significant ways. This connection sparked my

interest in learning more about her and exploring her story more deeply.

As our conversations developed, I realized that certain aspects of her lived experience

could only be authentically conveyed in her own voice. Her ability to articulate her inner

world through writing was powerful and insightful—something I felt I could not replicate or

replace. It became clear that her contributions were not only essential but irreplaceable. This

recognition motivated me to pursue a collaborative approach, positioning our work as a

partnership between two researchers rather than a traditional researcher-participant

relationship. The collaboration led me to deeply reexamine how she represents her voice and

gave me the opportunity to grow epistemologically, theoretically, and methodologically as a

researcher.

## Data collection and analysis

The data for this study originated from a 10-week research project conducted prior to

this collaborative autoethnographic study. During that project, seven students at a liberal arts

college each wrote five reflective essays, which they shared and discussed in five group

meetings. Julie’s essays, along with her verbal contributions during these meetings, served as

the foundational data for this co-authored project.

An initial round of inductive analysis was conducted by Youngji, focusing

specifically on Julie’s essays and verbal reflections. This analysis led to the identification of

two central categories: (1) experiencing disconnection and (2) experiencing reflection and

10

growth. These categories were then shared with Julie, who expanded upon them in

subsequent writings and was encouraged to revise or propose new categories that more

accurately represented her journey of identity negotiation.

Over the course of two months, Julie and Youngji met biweekly to review the

evolving analysis. In each session, Youngji presented theoretical frameworks and initial

interpretations, while Julie responded with her insights, revisions, and additional reflections.

These collaborative sessions were integral in shaping the co-authored narrative and ensuring

that both voices—student and faculty—were equitably represented in the interpretation and

presentation of the data.

Inspired by the collaborative autoethnographic work of Martin and Garza (2020),

which explored the experiences of a woman of color navigating her undergraduate education,

this study adapts their methodological approach to examine identity negotiation in the context

of racial and cultural threat. Their work offers a valuable framework for structuring co-

authorship that integrates personal narrative with theoretical analysis, allowing for both

rigorous inquiry and authentic voice.

Findings

## Experiencing disconnection

Being multiracial meant that I [Julie] was socially taught to become a shapeshifter.

The role I played depended on how others saw me, and how I was seen varied depending on

the demographic I was surrounded by. In each new social context that revolved around an

identity I partially resonated with, I learned that I had to know my place.

Ironically, being an outsider to both communities with which I partially shared

identities led me to lean into the opposite identity of the group I was in. When I had no

chance of being seen as an equal based on my identity, trying to be like everybody else was

11

hopeless. Instead, I decided to inflate my differences as a defense mechanism as I feared the

inevitability of my rejection as an imposter. This experience played out across several

contexts, which are described in the following subsections.

- Family experience

My sense of disconnect from my identities extended to my own family dynamics. I

was raised with distinctly Jewish and Korean cultural practices but was highly encouraged to

be American: to work hard, pursue my dreams, embrace individualism and not let my family

define what I chose to do in life. This created a paradox. Although I wasn’t consciously

aware of it until recently, the strength of family values that I saw exhibited in my parent’s

behavior, and the responsibilities they expected of me as the oldest child, led to an extreme

loss of my sense of self.

As a child, part of me believed that my parents held some knowledge about their

ethnic, religious, or cultural communities that they were keeping from me in hopes of raising

children that would fit perfectly into American culture. I always knew that my mother did not

put me or my brother in Korean school because she felt that the religious differences between

that environment and Hebrew school would confuse me. Although it would likely make a

difference, I doubt that knowing the Korean language would have cured my imposter

syndrome when I spent time with my Korean peers. Still, I needed a reason– or a person to

blame– for my failure to belong even though there wasn’t one.

My differences were out of my control, and out of my parents’ control. They did not

share my identity, so they could not fully share in my experiences or feelings. I would not be

accepted among other Jews the way my father was, or among Koreans the way my mother

was. I was raised by them, so I thought that my non-belonging must either stem from their

intentional withholding of cultural knowledge or from something I was doing wrong. I did

12

not understand that I was not the same as them: I could never be seen as their equal within the

communities they moved through because my identity was fundamentally different from

theirs. For a long time, I couldn’t see or accept that what I was experiencing was inevitable.

# - Jewish community experience

I was always alienated from my Jewish community, but I did not realize it until I

distanced myself from it. My Jewishness was often questioned because of my racial identity.

I went to an interfaith reconstructionist synagogue– a notably liberal and accepting branch of

the religion– yet my belonging was pulled into question when White children were accepted

without hesitation. It felt as though my peers were looking for reasons to delegitimize my

Jewish identity without specifically targeting my racial background.

I remember being looked at with disgust and scrutiny for eating a ham and cheese

sandwich in third grade, while my White counterparts could talk openly about celebrating

Easter– a holiday my family chose not to observe to focus on celebrating Jewish holidays–

without having the legitimacy of their Jewishness questioned. I was even laughed at once in

Hebrew school for not knowing when Christmas was. At the time, I did not notice the level of

exclusion I faced, nor could I explain the sense of isolation I felt when my peers failed to

recognize the significance of my identity within the Asian community. To them, being Jewish

meant being only Jewish—nothing else. I couldn’t be fully accepted as Jewish and Korean.

Being Jewish, in their eyes, meant being the same as them.

My identity was inherently intersectional, but in a community that couldn’t recognize

the way I experienced Jewishness, it became illegible. To me, practicing both Jewish and

Korean cultural traditions seemed like a given. To them, my connection to Korean culture

made me less Jewish. I was rarely seen as more than half Jewish. This was not an experience

13

shared by any of the White Jewish kids from interfaith families. I was the odd one out, thus I

could never quite belong.

# - Korean community experience

While I could never be Jewish because of my racialization, I also could never be

Korean because of my Whiteness. When I was around Korean peers, or in any Korean

community beyond my family, my connection to my Korean identity seemed to disappear. I

was afraid that any effort to assert my Korean-ness would come across as trying too hard, as

if I were pretending to be something I wasn't.

My fear was affirmed the few times I conversed with Korean peers about Korean

culture. When I was younger, I used to get excited to talk to other Korean kids—there

weren’t many Korean families in my town, so the chance to share something cultural felt rare

and special. I remember once telling a peer how my family drove an hour to shop at H-Mart,

the Korean-American supermarket chain. When I mentioned the word bulgogi, a Korean

marinated beef dish, I was immediately interrupted by laughter. My attempt to use a Korean

word was so distracting and entertaining that it entirely disrupted the conversation. It was

immediately signaled to me that I should stay out of something that I could not adequately

talk about.

My efforts to belong in a Korean community as Korean only made me look less

Korean. Trying to fit in only made my lack of belonging more apparent. Thus, I learned not

to acknowledge that I was Korean. It was easier for me to be accepted by Koreans if I did not

defend my Korean identity: it was preferable that I knew my place and kept my distance. I

would only find acceptance if I rejected the very identity in which I sought acceptance.

# - K-12 regular school experience

14

Due to the deep disconnect I felt from my identity that came not out of my own will,

but out of the images imposed on me by others, I tended to assume the identity that I was

associated with depending on where I was and who I was with. As both Korean and

Ashkenazi Jewish in a school with a large Asian population and a small Jewish one, I was

looked to for insight in history class when we discussed the Holocaust, but not the Korean

War, despite my family’s profound experience with both traumatic events. I felt much more

White and Jewish when I was going to public school, where I was surrounded by a larger and

more diverse population, than the Hebrew school I simultaneously attended. I leaned into my

Jewishness more in school. There were not many Jews at my school, so whenever Jewish

holidays were celebrated, I was looked to for an explanation. I was the spokesperson for

Jewish students, not the spokesperson for racial diversity that I was in synagogue programs.

Interestingly, in a class of five hundred students, there were only six Koreans. Still,

when topics relating to Korea were discussed, nobody would ask me for input, and I wouldn’t

eagerly give it. I desperately wanted to: I knew much about events we learned in world

history class from family anecdotes and knew that my input might have value. Yet I felt like

it wasn’t my place, because nobody saw me as Asian. A white-looking person with a Jewish

last name talking about being Asian in a class full of Asian students was guaranteed to make

me look like an imposter. Thus I withdrew myself from being Korean in school spaces.

- College experience

When I entered college, the dramatic shift in social environment—where racial and

cultural dynamics were vastly different from what I had known—forced me to reevaluate my

relationship with my racial identity. At first, the culture shock revealed itself in my

subconscious decision to avoid the kinds of spaces where I had once felt unwelcome back at

15

home. I withdrew and instead devoted myself to academics and personal interests—

something that gave me, for the first time, space to spend time with myself.

In high school, I had been constantly on the move, sometimes gone for up to sixteen

hours a day between school, studying, and fencing. There was no room to pause or reflect on

whether I felt fulfilled; any free moment had to be productive. But in college, uprooted from

everything familiar, I found myself facing a depth of anxiety and depression I had never

known. That year was painful, but it also forced me to recognize something I had never fully

embraced: my own agency. When multiple relationships became emotionally harmful, I

realized that I had the power—and the responsibility—to choose myself, to protect my well-

being, and to begin defining who I was on my own terms.

## Experiencing reflection and growth

Experiencing such dramatic shifts in my social environments over the past few years

left me with no choice but to confront my relationship with myself. I had to find stability

within when I could not find it in the world around me—and to do that, I had to understand

why I struggled to validate my own identity.

For as long as I could remember, I had felt that I wasn’t “enough” to fit into any one

category. I relied on others to tell me who I was, constantly measuring myself against them.

Prioritizing how others thought and felt gave me a way to avoid facing my own insecurity. If

I stopped to reflect, I would spiral into a familiar refrain: “you’re not enough.” I was not

Korean enough, not White enough, not Jewish enough to claim any part of myself with

confidence. I lived to please others because I could never quite accept myself.

The feeling of not being “enough” was amplified by the disconnect between my lived

experience and the generalized assumptions tied to the identity groups I belonged to. I often

felt like an imposter—like I hadn’t struggled hard enough to earn my identities. When I tried

16

to take ownership of who I was, I became exhausted from constantly having to justify my

point of view and defend the legitimacy of my identity. When I tried to suppress its

significance, I became just as burnt out—pretending I wasn’t affected when I felt deeply

misunderstood. I couldn’t control how I was perceived based on the stigmatized aspects of

my identity, but I desperately wanted to. That lack of control led me to internalize blame and

diminish my sense of self. The more alienated I felt, the more my self-hatred grew.

What I eventually realized was that it wasn’t any single aspect of my identity that

needed validation—it was my point of view. Lately, I’ve begun to feel a deeper sense of

affirmation, not because I’ve changed who I am, but because I’ve learned to accept that my

way of moving through the world is shaped by the very differences that once made me feel

isolated. For so long, I silenced and delegitimized myself, waiting for permission or

instruction to exist authentically. I now see that I had been seeking affirmation from people

whose experiences didn’t reflect my own. I let others define me and dictate what I should

believe because I had no framework for advocating for my differences. I internalized every

assumption made about me instead of recognizing the simple truth: it’s okay if others never

fully understand me.

I came to realize that I had the privilege of choosing how to spend my time in

college—and I wanted to use that privilege with intention. That realization led me to study

anthropology. I was drawn to the field because examining the world through cultural and

social dynamics helped me begin to understand myself. Anthropology gave context to my

feelings of alienation and allowed me to see my identity within a vast, interconnected

sociopolitical and historical landscape. Without fully realizing it at the time, I turned to my

studies for a form of personal validation. But through that process, my academic work, paired

with my lived experience, gave me a critical lens through which I now view the world. It

allowed me to take ownership of my identity and sparked a desire to help others do the same.

17

In beginning to understand myself—though the process is ongoing—I developed a deeper

appreciation for the necessity and power of learning.

The version of myself I now recognize as authentic is the one that doesn’t fit in—and

I’ve come to see that as a strength, not a flaw. My struggle for acceptance and success now

fuels the work I do as an educator. Living at the intersection of complex identities has taught

me to see the world—and society—as equally complex. The critical perspective I’ve

developed through social rejection has become one of my greatest affirmations. I no longer

seek belonging to soothe myself; instead, I use my experiences to validate and uplift others. I

don’t want anyone to feel the way I have felt—or at the very least, to know they’re not alone

in feeling different.

Discussion

As shown in the Findings section, Julie experienced a profound sense of

disconnection, feeling she did not fully belong to either the Asian or White racial groups. In a

racially binary environment that pressured her to choose one, she attempted to fit into each

group but faced rejection from both. Despite her “dual minority” status threatening her sense

of integrity (Shih et al., 2019, p. 2), she ultimately discovered her true self by embracing her

racial identities in a way that freed her from the need to fit into a single category.

Julie’s journey of self-discovery highlights two important contributions to the self-

affirmation literature, with threat playing a central role in both. First, this study suggests that

self-affirmation can arise not only from recalling pre-existing positive values but also from

developing new, meaningful aspects of the self in response to challenges. Previous research

has largely emphasized self-affirmation as a process of reminding individuals of strengths

they already possess (Escobar-Soler et al., 2024). For example, Yang et al. (2023) found that

a brief self-affirmation intervention—where students reflected on personally important

18

values—significantly improved the English test performance of migrant students. This effect

was explained by a reduction in stereotype threat, suggesting that self-affirmation with pre-

recognized positive values can help restore self-integrity under identity-based pressure.

In contrast, Julie’s experience suggests that self-affirmation can be a dynamic process

of constructing new positive identities. As described in the Findings, her sense of affirmation

did not stem from recalling established self-worth but emerged through an ongoing effort to

understand and redefine her present self. Through navigating and resisting rigid racial

categories, she ultimately forged an alternative identity that brought a deeper sense of

authenticity and affirmation. Thus, self-affirmation in this case was not a static product but a

transformative journey of cultivating latent aspects of the self and discovering a truer identity.

A key factor in this transformative process was the persistent role of threat. Julie’s

dual minority status represented a continuous challenge to her self-integrity from childhood

onward. When rejected by the White group, she turned to the Asian group for belonging—

only to be rejected again. This experience of “double rejection” has been well documented in

multiracial identity research (Chong & Song, 2022; Mengel, 2001; Strmic-Pawl et al., 2022).

For example, one participant in Mengel’s (2001) study reflected, “Then there are my friends

who hang out with me when I am by myself, but when they get around [other] Asian people,

they don’t talk to me… they stop talking and look at me awkwardly” (p. 109). Similarly, a

participant in Strmic-Pawl et al. (2022) shared, “Like during Asian American heritage month,

it’s like, I feel like I’m part of it but I’m not sure if I can say that” (p. 11). Like the

participants in those studies, Julie’s Asian heritage marked her as not fully White, while her

White heritage distanced her from being fully Asian—leaving her excluded from both

communities.

Yet rather than halting her development, these dual threats acted as a catalyst for

Julie’s growth. The rejections from both Jewish and Korean communities, though deeply

19

unsettling, propelled Julie toward self-exploration and identity reconstruction. The adversity

sharpened her desire to form a coherent sense of self that transcended narrow racial

definitions. This finding suggests that identity threats, while painful, can also accumulate in

ways that foster resilience and personal growth (Shih et al., 2019). Precisely because of the

dual threats she faced in a rigidly essentialist racial world, Julie was pushed to adopt a de-

essentialist view of identity—one that sees race as fluid and socially constructed rather than

fixed (Shih et al., 2019). Without experiencing these rejections, she may not have discovered

this alternative, more liberating identity space.

Second, Julie’s experience of discovering her true self illustrates that self-affirmation,

including the phase of being threatened, is inherently relational and deeply shaped by social

contexts. At first glance, self-affirmation theory may appear to focus solely on the individual,

given its emphasis on the “self.” However, Cohen and Sherman (2014) emphasize that social

relationships are crucial in the self-affirmation process. They introduce a recursive feedback

model called the cycle of adaptive potential, which shows how a single act of self-affirmation

can initiate a cascade of positive changes—not only within individuals but also in their

interactions with the social environment. In this model, the self-system and the social system

work together to sustain and amplify self-affirmation over time. For instance, when someone

builds a strong athletic identity as a source of affirmation, that identity does not develop in

isolation. It emerges through ongoing relationships with coaches, teammates, and fans. In this

way, self-affirmation is not merely individual but is more accurately described as “social”

affirmation (Cohen & Sherman, 2014, p. 361). As Asch (1954, as cited in Cohen & Sherman,

2014) noted, the ego is not solely focused on self-enhancement—it also seeks connection,

cooperation, and mutual understanding.

Importantly, threat, like affirmation, is also socially constructed and relational. In

Julie’s case, the threat to her racial identity was not something she chose but something

20

imposed upon her by others from an early age. Despite being born with both Asian and White

heritage, her identity became a site of judgment—shaped not by her own understanding, but

by the racial expectations and assumptions of others. This socially imposed judgment framed

her racial background as a threat to her self-integrity.

Ironically, the same social relationships that contributed to Julie’s sense of threat also

enabled that threat to become a catalyst for her growth. Research indicates that individuals

who successfully integrate multiple aspects of their identity often experience greater mental

health and resilience (Brook et al., 2008; Sanchez et al., 2009; Shih et al., 2019; Thoits,

1983). Conversely, unresolved identity conflict can result in significant psychological

distress. Crucially, the extent to which these factors are experienced as supportive or

conflicting depends largely on how they are interpreted through social relationships. Brook et

al. (2008) offer a compelling example: a working mother may initially feel that her job

detracts from her parenting. However, if she redefines her work as a means of financially

supporting her children, it takes on a relational significance that adds meaningful value. In

this way, the roles of “worker” and “mother” become mutually reinforcing, enhancing both

her well-being and her sense of self-affirmation.

Similarly, Julie began to reinterpret her multiracial identity not simply as a source of

personal tension, but as a powerful resource for others. Rather than seeking belonging solely

for herself, she found affirmation in using her lived experience to validate, educate, and

support those who might feel similarly isolated or different. As she put it, “My struggle for

acceptance and success now fuels the work I do as an educator…. I don’t want anyone to feel

the way I have felt—or at the very least, to know they’re not alone in feeling different.” In

helping others feel less alone, she herself gained a renewed sense of purpose—transforming

what was once a source of threat into a meaningful contribution.

21

As this discussion illustrates, Julie’s journey highlights two core dynamics of self-

affirmation, both deeply shaped and propelled by the presence of threat. Although threat is

sometimes overlooked in self-affirmation research, this study underscores its essential role. If

navigated constructively and socially, threat can act as a powerful catalyst for growth. Steele

(1988), who introduced self-affirmation theory, also emphasized this point: “I view these

self-affirmation processes as being activated by information that threatens the perceived

adequacy or integrity of the self” (p. 262). Without threat, individuals may lack the

motivation to explore and affirm underdeveloped or marginalized aspects of the self. Thus,

rather than something to avoid, threat can be reimagined as a respected force—one that

challenges individuals to access latent capacities, reconstruct identity, and become more

authentic versions of themselves.

Implication

This study suggests that educational and health interventions using self-affirmation

should not hesitate to engage with the role of threat. Rather than avoiding it, we should

recognize that experiences of threat—when approached constructively—can serve as a

powerful catalyst for growth. As Julie’s story illustrates, threats to identity can lead

individuals to question, reflect, and ultimately deepen their understanding of themselves.

These moments of discomfort are not merely obstacles to be overcome but can be integral to

identity development and psychological resilience. This aligns with broader findings that

people often gain strength through the process of navigating adversity (Shih et al., 2019).

Julie’s mother, understandably, sought to protect her daughter from the confusion and

emotional complexity that might come from engaging with her Korean heritage. She feared

that exposure to the Korean community might intensify Julie’s feelings of not fully

belonging—what we might recognize as a threat to racial identity. However, this study shows

22

that shielding individuals from identity-based threat can also limit opportunities for self-

discovery and affirmation. By avoiding complexity, we may inadvertently suppress chances

for growth. Had Julie been supported in exploring both sides of her racial identity more

freely—even if that meant facing discomfort—it could have opened up space for earlier

integration and self-understanding.

The implication is not that we should force individuals into identity conflict, but that

we should create environments that allow for uncertainty and exploration without rushing to

resolution. When people are confined to a single category or pressured to conform to rigid

identity labels, opportunities for self-affirmation can shrink. On the other hand, uncertain or

“in-between” spaces—while potentially anxiety-inducing—can offer fertile ground for

reflection, growth, and the development of authentic identity. In this light, threat is not

something to be avoided at all costs. It can be embraced, even appreciated, as a meaningful

part of the self-affirmation process.

To conclude, we should create more spaces in educational and health contexts where

people are invited to share the experiences that have challenged them—not with shame, but

with the understanding that these experiences can become sources of strength, insight, and

connection. If we are willing to respect the role of threat in the self-affirmation process, we

may uncover more empowering, more human, and more transformative ways of supporting

identity development.
