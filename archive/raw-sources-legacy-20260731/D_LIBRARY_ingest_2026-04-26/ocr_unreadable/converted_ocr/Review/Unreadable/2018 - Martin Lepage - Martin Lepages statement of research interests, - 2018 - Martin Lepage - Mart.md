Statement of Research Interests
Martin Lepage, Ph.D.
martinlepage26@me.com
In our digital era, material culture keeps mutating faster and faster. Everyday items can
now hold great value to certain people, whereas other items that used to be sacred or
most-prized are often represented in new and less typical ways. In fact, material culture,
understood as a series of social constructs, points directly to power relationships between
those who define the objects and those who use them (Miller, 1998; Woodward, 2007;
Hicks and Beaudry, 2010). But what does it show about how we engage with material
things such as books, tattoos, clothing, nature, cooking tools, ritual tools, beauty and
grooming products, etc.? What does it show in regard to social norms or what technology
and social media allow us to do? With such questions, my aim is to investigate the
relationship between material culture and the transmission of religious and embodied
identities. My goal is to explore in depth the ways in which collective and individual
practices share a common set of ritual tools and performances that help negotiate with
normative tradition, modify it or carry it on. These tools, as physical and aesthetic
artifacts, permeate every aspect of culture and come into play with how identities are
created and negotiated (Gilchrist, 1994; Kimmel, 1997; Appadurai, 1998; Hicks and
Beaudry, 2010). This research will allow me to focus on how material culture circulates
or is taken into account in the construction of people’s identity, namely the Radical
Faeries, and their community. I am particularly interested in looking at the
commodification of objects, and at how they help individuals take part in or challenge
tradition, whether religious, sexual or cultural.
More specifically, it is my wish to delve deeper into an intersectional exploration of
alternative masculinities and socio-political movements surrounding hegemonic
masculinity. As such, a broader research endeavor would allow further the conversation
between religious studies, gender studies and men’s studies, and create tighter
comparisons between a greater variety of identities occupying different (cisgender,
transgender, queer, gay, bisexual, heterosexual men, racialized men, ageing men,
disabled men, etc.) social positionings. It would expand my previous research on
contemporary Pagan and LGBTQ communities, which pass on their material heritage in
ways that are still very much unknown. Comparing this phenomenon with the
appropriation of First Nations traditions, for example, which often meet with Pagan or
LGBTQ communities and values in the context of socio-political activism, would
certainly prove to be fruitful. These identities are certainly based on different ideologies
and practices, but, at the same time, profoundly influenced by Western thought and way
of life. As such, they all have developed different branches in the same umbrella of
sociocultural traditions, which people pass on in many alternative and innovative
intersecting ways that remain to be unveiled.
My doctoral dissertation was a study of identity negotiations in the Montreal Pagan
community, regarding gender and sexual orientation. Much of the research on the topic
suggests that contemporary Paganism offers a very inclusive milieu where LGBTQ
Pagans can express the specificity of their identities through collective and individual

practices and performances whose gendered meanings and relations can be modified at
will. But I demonstrate, contrary to popular belief, that these religiosities still reproduce
normativity, and that identity is better expressed and assumed when the individual is
detached, at least for a time, from the community. To complete this research, I have
traveled to Toronto, Ottawa, and Quebec City, interviewed experts on the topics of
Paganism, magic and gender from Ontario and Quebec universities, and created a
diversified and comprehensive original dataset from ethnographies and LGBTQ
informants’ interviews. I did an extensive exploration of the diverse relationships
between religion and sexuality, as they can be normative, as well as loci of agency.
Because certain issues around identity and power arose in my research, I also worked on
discourses around sexuality, the history of the LGBTQ movement, and different
approaches to gender and sex in society. For the most part, my approach to research is
influenced by queer feminist studies, religious studies, cultural studies,
socioanthropology of religion and sexuality. I have explored and successfully applied the
use of the life narrative methodology with participant observation, in order to contrast
different power dynamics surrounding gender. Moreover, I have articulated a broadly
useful queer perspective allowing the analysis of power relations, within individual and
collective experiences, between identity, authenticity, belonging, and social recognition.
In that regard, objects are used to assert identity: they allow people to gain power over
their lives, to go against several norms, whether related to gender, religion, or social
status (Oldenziel, 1996; Retallack, 1999; Latour, 2007; Woodward, 2005; Fowler, 2010;
Jones and Boivin, 2010). But they also are tied to a wider sociocultural, political and
economic background which prescribes the extent to which people's power can be
claimed. Objects can also express multiple meanings and hold different values to
different people in the same community or in society in general. By paying attention to
the ways certain objects or, more generally, the accumulation of certain types of objects,
partake in communities’ codes and practices, this study will explore the interplay
between the meaning given to material culture by individuals, the way they use them and
how they help them be part of their community. It focuses on masculinity among the
Radical Faeries, a narrower aspect of my previous research on contemporary Pagan and
LGBTQ communities, which pass on their material heritage in ways that are still very
much unknown. It is also rooted in the understanding that religion and religious
experience are part of everyday life, whether aligned with or alternative to prescribed
traditional practices and worldviews. The goal is, in the first part of this project, to
review the present literature around men's social practices and to determine the degree to
which the presence and importance of certain objects participate in the diverse ways men
identify as such in different contexts. It will take a look at specific objects that hold
multifaceted meanings, like jewelry, items of clothing, as well as the use of certain
cosmetic or grooming implements, household and/or ritual tools. The second part of the
study will seek to delve deeper into gay and queer men's religious experience by
questioning how they actually adhere to the use of these objects as vectors of alternative
masculinities.
Although I have been focused on my graduate research for several years, I have been
actively involved in conversations with students and scholars from many universities
since 2011; through organization and participation in our annual Young researchers

I have served as a research assistant for projects on sexuality, ageing and masculinities,
on gendered school rites, on youth and sexuality in Quebec, on marginalized populations
in France, and on religious diversity in Montreal. I have also served as a teaching
assistant in courses about religion and sexuality, homosexuality and gendered identities,
rituals, culture and Western society, as well as Quebec culture, more specifically. I also
have spoken in many courses related to those topics. In addition, I have a previous
background in literary studies, and a basic training on the Ethics and Religious Culture
curriculum.
In the next few years, I am interested in developing solid methods on the aforementioned
interdisciplinary approach to research, particularly combining inductive and deductive
methodologies with thematic qualitative data analysis. As such, I want to explore the
various practices through which men who belong to the Radical Faeries identify as
masculine. Since the 1970's, the Radical Faeries, a political, environmental, and spiritual
movement of gay and queer men, have challenged hegemonic masculinity. As such, my
research will focus on the ways in which religious practices converge with material
culture and masculinity within a specific cultural context. This research interrogates how
Radical Faeries, whose identification to masculine norms can be problematized in
different ways, negotiate their identities in regard to binary gender norms as they are
performed in religious context. It compares how diverse masculinities come into play,
within identification processes, with conceptions of virility (Connell, 2005; Jeffrey and
D’Allondans, 2008).
To do so, I will consider socialization processes to men's material culture in a dual
perspective related to theories in religious studies (Ménard, 2006). The first angle is
associated with virility rites (Jeffrey and Lachance, 2012) that, through repetition,
determine particular forms of masculine experience of body appropriation (Wulf, 2003;
Schechner, 2006). The second complementary angle is based on systems of sacralized
symbols, allowing a comprehension of a network of meanings that put into coherence the
experiences of specific groups (Lévi-Strauss, 1958; McGuire, 2008). In addition to what
religious studies can bring, this research is supported by an intersectional theoretical
framework (Crenshaw, 2005; McCall, 2005; Brotman and Lévy, 2008; Dorlin, 2009; Mc
Andrew, 2010). This theoretical approach recognizes a diversity of experiences within
observed groups, due to the multiple social positionings of individuals (religion, age,

This research will focus on diverse socialization, identification, integration and
recognition processes of virility behaviors via material culture within masculine
experience. The data-gathering axis of this research uses qualitative methodologies
adapted to the analyses of social processes and phenomena, as well as the meaning given
by individuals to their actions (Paillé and Mucchielli, 2012). It aims to recruit gay,
bisexual, transgender and queer men of different age groups and diversified cultural
origins in order to gain a better understanding of the complex power relations coming
into play in the transmission and reproduction of masculine identities in society
(Connell, 2005). It will first interrogate the contemporary transmission processes and the
ways in which various norms are reproduced throughout the course of one’s life
(sacredness of virility, virility rites, prohibitions around virility, beliefs and myths about
virility). It will then focus on the integration of these norms within masculine identity
and their (emotional, social and sexual) experience vis-à-vis those norms (practices and
rites).
Data analysis will specifically turn to variations in behaviors and attitudes towards
material culture throughout the course of their life around virility norms (discrepancies
and continuities). It will borrow Rémy and Ruquoy’s (1990) four-step method, bringing
to light recurring themes, comparing significations, forming categories and obtaining
more objective meanings (Paillé, 2006). It is also my intention to combine the benefits of
« traditional » socioanthropological methods of analysis with data analysis software like
Atlas.ti or NVivo.
