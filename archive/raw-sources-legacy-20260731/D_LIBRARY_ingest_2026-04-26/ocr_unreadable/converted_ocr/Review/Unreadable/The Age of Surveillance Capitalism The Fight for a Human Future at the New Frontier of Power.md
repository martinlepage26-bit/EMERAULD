BOOK REVIEW

The Age of Surveillance Capitalism: The Fight for a Human Future at the New
Frontier of Power, by Shoshana Zuboff, New York, Public Affairs, 2019, 704 pp.,
e20.00, ISBN 978-1610395694
This book by Shoshana Zuboff, professor emeritus at Harvard Business School, can be
counted among the studies that denounce the existence of social oppression of the individual. It is not difficult to suppose that the author seeks to free us from the institutional control exercised, in this case, by the Internet industry and social networks. The old books of
political philosophy that proposed to free the individual from social institutions, as JeanJacques Rousseau and his disciples did in their day, also had a warning tone. Zuboff takes
up this urgency a few centuries later to remind us that the use of digital tools is neither
neutral nor innocuous, but responds to the economic design of those who own these tools.
‘The fact that what is called the technological development of modern times has been so
largely oriented economically to profit-making is one of the fundamental facts of the history of technology’ (22).
The social institutions of the digital world use and control us to obtain economic results,
hence the title ‘surveillance capitalism’. ‘I consider surveillance capitalism’s operations as a
challenge to the elementary right to the future tense, which accounts for the individual’s
ability to imagine, intend, promise, and construct a future’ (25).

1. Social networks and the internet must be oriented towards the
common good
Zuboff suggests that there is a common confusion between the benefits of technological
development and the abuse that the owners of this development exercise in our day. While
the growth of this industry is very positive for society, the lack of clarity and transparency
in the way our private information is used for economic gain is very harmful. This author
wishes to emphasize that technological development should not inevitably imply a
‘utilitarian’ use of technology, where users have to accept that technology carries some risks,
which they accept in order to use it. It does not seem that the author wishes to criticize the
goodness of technological development, but rather to aspire to a better use of that development, in view of the common good. ‘The evidence of our psychic numbing is that only a
few decades ago US society denounced mass behavior-modification techniques as unacceptable threats to individual autonomy and the democratic order. Today the same practices
meet little resistance or even discussion as they are routinely and pervasively deployed in
the march toward surveillance revenues’ (25).
Zuboff raises a legitimate concern in her book. She poses the question of whether human
activity in our day, which is so marked in Western society by the use of digital technology,
is on the whole geared towards the common good, or not. She wonders about the intentions of those who have the task of designing these instruments, since they are complex systems that not only offer the individual the possibility of implementing his desires without
considering his real needs, but also constantly incite the user to depend on the use of technology. Zuboff finds that it is a fallacy to think that technology has a neutral orientation,
since precisely those who develop technology do so with a purpose. Often the purpose is to
obtain economic benefits, other times it is to accumulate information and therefore power.

If, as Zuboff seems to fear, the addiction to technology that arises from the intrinsic design
of digital communication and information tools leads to actions that are contrary to the
dignity of the person, then we are faced with a whole system that is harmful to
human beings.

2. The digital industry dominates the social sphere
In her book, Zuboff follows the reflection of sociologist Emilie Durkheim who suggested
that the market and economic development follow the ‘existential conditions’ of individuals. It is as if a culture and a society define for itself what individuals will be able to consume, and therefore will consume. The aim of social institutions is to place people within a
set of predetermined options, where, without the possibility of real choice, they can at least
console themselves with the products available. For Zuboff this is especially the case with
digital technology, because the internet and digital platforms become broad existential conditions, where we can move, navigate and discover what we want without mediation
or waiting.
Our author suggests that the digital industry became a commercial miracle that gave its
creators the strength of a religion. For example, in terms of online music sales alone, within
a few months Steve Jobs claimed that Apple was the largest music distribution company in
the world. ‘Within a month there were five million downloads, then ten million three
months later, then twenty-five million three months after that. Four and a half years later,
in January 2007, that number rose to two billion, and six years later, in 2013, it was 25 billion. In 2008 Apple surpassed Walmart as the world’s largest music retailer’ (33).
Zuboff observes that government is incapable of exercising efficient control and coordination because its processes are too slow, so that large digital technology companies become
the repository of modern democracy. She quotes Jerry Page, founder of Google, arguing
that it would be better to trust Google than democratic institutions. ‘Page defended
Google’s unprecedented information power with an extraordinary statement suggesting
that people should trust Google more than democratic institutions: “In general, having the
data present in companies like Google is better than having it in the government with no
due process to get that data, because we obviously care about our reputation. I’m not sure
the government cares about that as much”’ (63).1
The key to the digital conquest of the economic and political world lies in the fact that
the instruments are designed to respond to its expectations of search and autonomy.
However, the fallacy is precisely that these tools do not give us true autonomy and freedom
of choice. ‘This is the existential contradiction of the second modernity that defines our
conditions of existence: we want to exercise control over our own lives, but everywhere that
control is thwarted. Individualization has sent each one of us on the prowl for the resources
we need to ensure effective life, but at each turn we are forced to do battle with an economics and politics from whose vantage point we are but ciphers’ (49).
To demonstrate what she calls deception, the author points to the history of the Google
search engine as the pillar of the surveillance capitalism we live in today. In the beginning,
Google’s founders Jerry Page and Sergey Brin did not use the secondary material of each
search, until they realized that this information was invaluable for understanding each customer. The company has achieved an incredible development in collecting enough information from each user to be able to exercise social control. When their first press conference
took place in 1999, one year after the founding on September 4, 1998, Google had only 7
million users. ‘At that early stage of Google’s development, the feedback loops involved in

improving its Search functions produced a balance of power: Search needed people to learn
from, and people needed Search to learn from’ (70). Now, Google has 4.3 billion users.
Since Google’s founders did not want to include advertising in their search engine, it
was necessary to find a way to make money without it. The solution was to create a bridging system between what the user is looking for and what companies wanted to offer.
Google’s role was key in the process, allowing it to aggregate relevant information within
its own firm. ‘If there was to be advertising, then it had to be “relevant” to users. Ads would
no longer be linked to keywords in a search query, but rather a particular ad would be
“targeted” to a particular individual’ (75).
With this move, initially aimed at avoiding advertising on the search engine screen,
Google managed to collect, organize and refine a large amount of collateral information
that would allow it over time to define the behavior of billions of people. The point of discovery came when, on the TV show called ‘Who wants to be a millionaire?’, one of the
questions to be answered was Carol Brady’s date of birth. Thousands of people turned to
Google to look up the answer, because it offered such accurate information. This clearly
marked the beginning of a barometer of social behavior. ‘The result is that both the world
and our lives are pervasively rendered as information’ (177).
Not only can Google charge companies for placing their advertising, but it also knows
how many of the people searching for food at a certain time receive information about a
certain food, etc. Likewise, it is not enough to just show a product, it must correspond with
the buying style of the person who is searching for it. Google has so much information concerning people that it can filter the companies to propose to the user the one that is ‘more
convenient’ to satisfy his need. ‘In other words, Google would no longer mine behavioral
data strictly to improve service for users but rather to read users’ minds for the purposes of
matching ads to their interests, as those interests are deduced from the collateral traces of
online behavior’ (79).
Zuboff is concerned because she observes that a company like Google is able to suggest
a large number of products, with the suggestions targeted in reality for the economic gain
of big companies, while at the same time letting each individual believe that he or she really
decides everything about his or her own life. For her it is possible to speak of a great social
control of the masses where we are at the mercy of the internet, of a search engine, of an
algorithm. ‘Only then could Google accurately assess the content of a page and how users
interact with that content. This “content-targeted advertising” based on Google’s patented
methods was eventually named AdSense. By 2004, AdSense had achieved a run rate of a
million dollars per day, and by 2010, it produced annual revenues of more than $10 billion’ (84).

3. Final considerations
Zuboff’s book is interesting for the great amount of information it presents and for the
awareness it brings that the means with which we organize society must allow us to achieve
what we need and not something else. However, it is worth remembering that human
beings are not subject to moral determinism, even when the means at their disposal are
very limited. Beyond the choice of instruments, there is the human capacity to choose to
act for an end that we consider good. In any case, Zuboff reminds us that our way of acting,
both personally and socially, ought never to be guided simply by motives of economic utility or productive efficiency. Unfortunately, some governments try to manage their citizens
in this way, as Zuboff confirms with concrete data: ‘In 2016 Chinese search engine Baidu,
often referred to as the Google of China, announced that its ‘Big Data Lab’ uses location

data from its 600 million users to track and predict the dynamics of the Chinese economy.
The company built an ‘employment index’ for the national economy as well as a
‘consumption index’’ (234).
People who truly wish to serve others in society will seek to make use of these tools to
care for those most in need, to include those who have fewer opportunities, to live in a
more educated and humane way with others in society. For this, it is necessary to rethink
the anthropological foundations of digital tools such as social platforms and the Internet. In
the end, it could be precisely the transcendent and spiritual dimension of man that has
been lost. Social conditions can improve, but never to the point of fully satisfying man.
Improving society depends on many factors, but the ultimate solution does not lie in social
organization, but in recovering human dignity in all fields of human life, including in modern ‘surveillance capitalism’.
