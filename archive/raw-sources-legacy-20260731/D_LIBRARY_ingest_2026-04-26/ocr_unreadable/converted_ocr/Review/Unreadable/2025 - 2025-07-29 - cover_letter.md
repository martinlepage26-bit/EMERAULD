**MT-2025-07-29**

**Decision: REVISE AND RESUBMIT**

Dear Martin Lepage,

Many thanks for submitting your revised article ‘"Reboot": Performance, Gender, and the Computation of Identity in Postmodern Society’ to *Media Theory*.

Following feedback from referees (whose comments are pasted below, in addition to a pdf with some extra comments from Ref B), we would like to encourage you to revise and resubmit the article for consideration. As you can see, the referees are both very positive about the potential of the article, but suggest similar ways in which the article needs to be revised before it can be published.

The revised version will go back to the same referees for their views on whether the article is ready to be accepted or not, and you should take all their comments on board when revising. Please highlight the main changes made in your text; you may also want to provide an anonymous cover letter to explain to the referees what you have and haven’t done.

I’d be grateful if could send me back the revised version when you can, ideally within the next 2 months; please also take the opportunity to ensure that it’s in house style, if it isn’t already.

Thanks again for thinking of the journal, and I look forward to receiving the revised version.

Regards,

Simon Dawes

Co-Editor, *Media Theory*

**REF A**

Thanks for the opportunity to review this piece. I found the article to be lucidly written in refreshingly accessible prose. I also appreciate the quirkiness of its subject matter, which (if not exactly timely) does indeed feel prescient in some respects.

In sum, I enjoyed the essay. However, I think that it needs some revision and expansion to pass the bar of publication, which I take to entail making a contribution to a field of scholarship and/or a set of scholarly understandings that aggregate around a topic. To that end, I offering the following considerations:

- My central suggestion is that the author could work significantly further in elaborating what their reading of ReBoot teaches us that we don’t already know. Put differently, what do we learn from viewing contemporary culture through this ReBoot genealogy? This is particularly pertinent because much of what is said about ReBoot is readily legible elsewhere, in much more widely acknowledged and influential sci-fi sources. To explain further: As I view it, the goal of the paper is to historicize a set of concepts (performativity, gender, and computation) by reading them through ReBoot. Implicit in this is the idea that there is a change (over time) in the concepts, practices, and materialities that make up (for example) gender, and the method of tracking these changes in a media object helps to reveal these particularities. Laudably, I think this is present in the essay, but demands much more explication: as it stands, any but the most generous reader might reasonably understand the essay to be pointing out *correspondences*(between, for example, theoretical literature on gender fluidity and the representations in the show) more than co-implications…it could be read at times a little like a ‘paint by numbers’ exercise (even if, again, there are many subtle points subtly made!).

One way to address this would be to add a comparative element to the essay. The author gestures in this direction through references to Mortal Kombat, but what would be helpful would be a more engaged and detailed comparison about the alignments and divergences ReBoot has from a cognate media object (Bladerunner immediately comes to mind as a possibility, but there are many) so that we can really get at the stakes of the argument. Likewise, since the argument is as much about queerness and gender as it is about the show, it would be helpful to contextualize the cited scholarship a little: the author could, for example, show why the show aligns with Munoz’s vision of queerness better than others’, and how this alignment brings out something about Munoz’s thought that might not be obvious without it—again, this would help to mobilize the particularities of the show and the theory alike so that they can each teach us something about the other.

On a different front, there are a few places where attending a bit more closely to the contingencies of representation would be helpful. As a tactic, I suggest at least provisionally adding “representations of” prior to most uses of the word “identity” and references to categorical claims so that the contingencies of representation aren’t forgotten. (These can be removed later, but I think having them there will lead to subsequent changes.) Quite often in the piece there seems to be an implicit assumption of a one-to-one mapping of the representation of something and the thing (I.e. the abstraction)—this is particularly problematic for an erstwhile queer reading! This can also lead to an at times simplistic modelling of the problem, such that (for example) in contrast to the claim to troubling normative boundaries around line 188 I might offer that it may be worth considering whether the 90s didn’t in fact reveal how these boundaries were themselves untethered from “simple heterosexual models” such that representations of their fluidity were less troubling than reinforcing. (Indeed, insofar as a Foucauldian reading of heteronormativity is being invoked, this would be part and parcel of that perspective.) I also found it curious that there is very little reference to the aesthetic of the show after the opening pages, and I wonder if there might be something to consider here as well.

Finally, a few nit-picks:

- with reference to the claim that “If ReBoot is a meditation on the logic of computation, it is also an allegory for the ethics of encountering alterity,” Consider whether the logic of computation—rooted as it is for many theorists in a principle of universal exchangeability—is anathema to encountering alterity. If this is the case, there is an opportunity to read the show against itself a bit more—which would also go some way towards engaging the contingencies of its representations.

- with reference to the claim that certain characters (and the show more broadly) are “queer per se:” if the show is indeed queer (rather than being open to a queer reading), how might this help us to historicize queerness? Alternately, might it be worth acknowledging that queerness is always in the mix, so that the reading demonstrates how this happens in ways that can be surprising? The same applies, I think, to the discussion of how femininity is variously represented—here, I think Chomsky’s famous observation is apt, namely that “The smart way to keep people passive and obedient is to strictly limit the spectrum of acceptable opinion, but allow very lively debate within that spectrum.”

- in reference to the claim that “ReBoot is not simply a show about games, but a show that is a game, one where each character must perform the right self at the right time with the right tools or risk deletion:” I really don’t understand this, but it seems to again miss something of representation…Isn’t the situation that the characters are put in exactly what the show is ‘about’, and therefore it is a show about games? how is the show itself a game any more than any other show?

- the essay would benefit from pushing back a bit against the assumption (promoted by the show) that it is engaging computation. Certainly, there are numerous invocations of computation and one might even claim that a hegemonic understanding of computation is central to its aesthetic (and, indeed, that is produced computationally, as the author notes), but there is fertile ground in the difference between this and computation, in the same way that representations of sexuality or families or governments or law enforcement etc are always constructing as much as describing that which they depict. With this in mind, I’d love the author to explore how the repeated computation motifs might in fact be doing something other than simply reflecting computation, and what we can learn from this at it is rolled out a decade or so after Apple’s famous “think different” moment.

If all that seems negative, I want to reiterate that there is much to recommend in the essay, and I hope that the author will pursue it for publication. Expanding the essay to include a comparative analysis and deeper engagement with its concepts (and the scholarship that surrounds them) will help to bring the already insightful engagements of the essay better to bear on important questions in contemporary media studies.

**REF B – also see attached pdf.**

The hinge of the essay is that *ReBoot* is prescient in its understanding of both pressures placed on gendered identity in networked life, while sketching a model for anti-capitalist and gender queering work. That is the essay’s aesethetic claim. What the essay promises, pedagogically, is to show how to better understand the protocols of gendered identity in networked life with the 1994 television show as our guide.

What ends up happening in the essay, however, feels more like an attempt to align powerful, familiar conceptual tools with the television show. Butler’s concept of performance, Galloway’s concept of protocol, and Foucault’s concept of epistemes and disciplinary power are central here. But with the focus on finding concepts that fit the television show as an appropriate explanatory apparatus, an apparatus adequate to the program’s prescience, we lose the opportunity to learn more about identity in networked life. This would require that the emphasis of the essay’s own interpretive work shift, slightly, so that concepts like performativity aren’t simply shown to have been correct all along (this begs the question of what historical forces have colluded to keep those concepts intact and relevant despite the transformations and pressures of networked life). I think the essay would live up to its own promise better if it used the television show and its suite of concepts to teach us about instances of identity work in the networked, computational present tense (3 decades after the show aired). In other words, how does performativity work, specifically, in networked life? Does it have to change or be adapted? Are there ways in which its faith in repetition is undermined? Etc. I’d ask the same thing of every concept, and, if in fact a concept really needs no modification, I think the essay could be curious about why. Why, in other words, across so many decades of change, change that the essay in fact takes as its central concern, does disciplinarity continue to have explanatory power? What does this teach us, in particular, about identity as it is lived and contested in networked life? In my margin notes, I’ve noted all the places where I think there is an opportunity to do this kind of conceptual work—which is probably more a shift in emphasis than it is a re-conceptualization.

There’s also a feeling that the essay’s concepts don’t build on each other, but simply get added to the account serially. In my margin notes in the PDF, I’ve pointed to several places in the essay where I have felt this as a lost opportunity.

Because I think the essay could still do so much more work to teach its readers how to think about gendered identity in contemporary networked life, I’m suggesting a revise and re-submit. But I think it’s very clear that this essay will make an important contribution to contemporary Media Studies.
