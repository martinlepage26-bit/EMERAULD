Introduction
What do people think when they hear the words social justice? What comes to mind
when people think of social justice related to the Bible or Christianity? What do people think
when they hear of racism related to white people in America? Chances are, depending on your
worldview, you might have answered “White people are responsible for the systemic racism
seen in our society today.” and you might have said, “The Bible supports social justice, and
Christians should support social justice and equality.” In our society, these sentiments are held
by most individuals who identify as progressive. And surprisingly, these sentiments are
growing among churches and Christians in this country. While Christians are called to uphold
justice, there is a vital difference in how the world and the Bible define justice. How one
defines justice depends on the worldview they ascribe to.
As Christians, the Lord has called us to uphold values such as justice and equality as
these are both part of loving your neighbor. Believers are given this charge in Proverbs which
says “Speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves; ensure justice for those being
crushed. Yes, speak up for the poor and helpless, and see that they get justice” (Proverbs 31:89).1 This shows that Christians should fight for the marginalized and oppressed and see that
they receive justice. However, this justice must reflect the character and love Christ
exemplified and should guide how such justice is seen to fruition. We are called to spread the
Gospel while advocating for the less fortunate. Moreover, Christians often focus on one of
these over the other. Conservatives often focus on evangelism but do not focus as much on

True faith should also be reflected in the pursuit of righteous things, such as desiring and

We see the importance of those in conjunction in Micah chapter 6 which states, “No,

that can be found in Christ Jesus.
As explored in this thesis, Critical Race Theory (CRT) is a social theory and ideology
that teaches that racial inequities are embedded in American society. Critical theorists believe
that racial inequities are embedded in all aspects of society such as policies, laws, and other
institutions. Based on this prognosis, CRT proponents believe that the current system of

3
systemic racism must be challenged and replaced with systems that lead to more equity for
oppressed groups and power be taken from groups in power and given to those oppressed
groups. However, as will be explored later as well, the foundations of CRT and the methods
critical theorists and supporters employ to reach the goal of social equity are questionable,
unbiblical, and at times immoral, making the movement's beliefs and tactics oftentimes no
better than the evil it seeks to eradicate. The tenets of CRT will be explored in depth in the
following sections of this thesis.
Social justice is another term that is often used in conjunction with CRT and other
social theories of the like and describes the methods by which critical theorists further their
own understanding of justice. Social justice, while quite a nebulous concept, holds several
definitions depending on the group employing the term. However, this does not mean that all
definitions are correct. Within the biblical context, the phrase “social justice” would be
alluding to the command in Scripture to help the marginalized and oppressed. However,
within the secular context, the term often refers to the processes by which power and
influence are redistributed to oppressed groups to create a more equitable society. Oftentimes,
these tactics are immoral and unbiblical but can be effective when replaced with more biblical
principles of justice.
On the contrary, biblical social justice emphasizes restorative justice and places love
and mercy at the forefront to achieve justice more effectively. While secular social justice
often places groups of people against one another, creating tension and promoting division by
failing to separate the sin from the sinner, biblical social justice focuses on reforming the
behavior while maintaining a level of love, respect, and grace afforded to the individual being

cases, biblical social justice emphasizes forgiveness, love, and mercy.
Because of this misunderstanding, critical theorists and other secular social justice
supporters often allude to the biblical call to justice as support for their cause, and to draw
Christians in. Therefore, since Christians are called to justice and to love their neighbor and
thus, should support every movement that claims to support or seek the best interest of any
marginalized group. However, progressives, and sadly Christians as well, are
misunderstanding what biblical social justice looks like compared to secular ideas and
definitions of social justice.
As followers of Christ, we should all be seeking justice and equality for any oppressed
group in our society. We are called to judge righteously but also to love everyone the same
way Christ loves. There is a need for biblical social justice in our society regarding racial
relations. However, there is a proper way to accomplish this goal. While CRT attempts to do
this, it falls short and, in some cases, it accomplishes the opposite and supports rhetoric of
furthering racism and inequality in our society in the name of secular social justice instead of
biblical social justice. When these biblical principles are used to replace the secular principles
currently found within secular social justice movements, biblical social justice will be more
effective in addressing injustice than CRT. From this understanding, this thesis will argue that
CRT is a secular ideology that further spreads the injustices it claims to advocate against and
must be addressed and replaced with biblical principles of justice.2

The phrase “secular social justice” will be used throughout the rest of the thesis to refer to the current

of justice for marginalized groups and individuals.

1.1 Definitions of Key Terms
This section will cover a few key terms important for the overall understanding of CRT
and what it teaches. Also, note some of these terms have been modified from their original
definitions within the context of CRT.
Intersectionality: “Intersectionality” was originally coined by Columbia Law Professor
Kimberle Crenshaw. Crenshaw defines the term as “A metaphor for understanding the
multiple ways that multiple forms of inequality or disadvantage sometimes compound
themselves and create obstacles that often are not understood among conventional ways of
thinking.”3 Rod Dreher is another scholar and CRT opponent who has defined
Intersectionality, as “The concept that all those oppressed by the privileged class–the
patriarchy, whiteness, and so forth–are connected by virtue of their oppression and should
challenge power as a united front.”4 Critical theorists use this concept as a means to determine
one's level of oppression and their relationship to other oppressed groups. This helps
determine one's status (Oppressed or Oppressor) within society based on the group they are
assigned to.

whiteness as “The overt and subliminal socialization processes and practices, power

1.2 Brief History of Systemic Racism in the United States
CRT was developed after a long racial history and relations in the United States and is
heavily supported by those who advocate for secular social justice. Racial relations throughout
the history of the United States eventually led to the culmination of historical racism, leading
to the development of CRT. Racism is a very real and evil reality for this country and has
been throughout its history. Before the formation of the United States, slavery found its way
to the Americas which saw the forced labor of Africans who were kidnapped and brought over
on ships from the African Continent. Slavery found its way to the United States in 1619 when
the first ship arrived in Virginia, transporting the first slaves.11 This was the first wave of
many which lasted from 1619 until 1808 when the slave trade to the United States was
abolished by the Slave Trade Acts of 1807.12 While this only affected the transportation of
slaves across the Atlantic, the domestic trade of slaves was still prevalent in southern states.
Slavery continued in the United States for another 58 years after the abolition of the Slave
Trade. Marking a total of 246 years of slavery in the United States. This culminated in the
