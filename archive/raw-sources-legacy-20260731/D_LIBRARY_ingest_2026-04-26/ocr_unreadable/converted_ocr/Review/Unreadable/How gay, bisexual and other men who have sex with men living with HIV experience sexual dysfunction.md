Background. Sexual dysfunction (SD) is prevalent, yet underaddressed, among gay, bisexual and
other men who have sex with men (GBM) living with HIV. We aimed at exploring the perceived
causes and effects of SD from the perspective of Montreal-based GBM with HIV. Methods. During
2024, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 31 Montreal-based GBM with HIV who
experienced SD (reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, premature/delayed ejaculation or pain
during sex) in the past 5 years, asking about perceived causes and impacts. Data were analyzed using
reﬂexive thematic analysis. Results. Participants’ median age was 46 years (range 27–74 years). The
most common SDs were reduced libido (n = 26; 84%) and erectile dysfunction (n = 25; 81%).
Participants reported a median of three concurrent SDs. Themes regarding the perceived causes
of SD were: (1) biopsychosocial challenges across the lifespan, including aging, physical health
(e.g. hormonal/cardiovascular problems), adverse life events and mental health problems; (2) living
with HIV (in terms of HIV diagnosis and stigma) and concerns about antiretroviral therapy sideeffects; and (3) gay sexual culture, which encompassed challenges in navigating expectations,
relational/sexual ambivalence, and combining sex and drugs. Themes related to the impacts of
SD included: (1) negative emotions (e.g. frustration, low self-esteem); (2) relational strain; and (3)
adaptation (e.g. prioritizing intimacy over sex alone). Conclusions. Findings highlight the multifaceted
experience of SD among GBM with HIV. Participants perceived that biopsychosocial challenges across
the lifespan, living with HIV and gay sexual culture contributed to SD. Although SD led to emotional
and relational distress, participants indicated strategies to manage it.
Keywords: AIDS, LGBT, qualitative research, queer, sexual dysfunction, sexual functioning, sexual
problems, sexuality.

Introduction
Male sexual dysfunction (SD) refers to diﬃculties that can aﬀect the sexual response, such
as reduced libido, erectile dysfunction (ED), premature/delayed ejaculation and painful
intercourse.1 Globally, SD is prevalent among men, with rates increasing with age.2 In
Canada, studies have reported that 30% of men aged 40–59 years experience reduced
libido.3 In Quebec, SD is common, with an estimated prevalence among men and women
of nearly 20% in a community sample, and 33% in a sample from a local sexology clinic.4
Literature consistently shows that SD is more prevalent among gay, bisexual and other men
who have sex with men (GBM) compared with heterosexual men,5,6 and even more among
those with HIV.7 Data from a community-recruited survey in 2017–2018 indicate that loss
of libido was the most common SD, with approximately 56% of Montreal-based GBM
experiencing some level of reduced libido in the past 6 months.8 Although the prevalence
data on SD have led researchers to argue that it should be considered comorbid to HIV
infection,9 it is often unreported10 and underaddressed in practice.11 If left untreated, SD
can aﬀect the quality of life of people with HIV by contributing to anxiety and depression,
HIV medication non-adherence,12 and strain on intimate relationships.7

Recruitment and screening
Recruitment took place from July to December 2024 through
advertisements in HIV/sexual health clinics, community
groups, social media and snowball sampling. A bilingual
ﬂyer (French and English) with a QR code and web link led to
an online screening tool, where eligibility was assessed. To
screen for SD, participants were asked: ‘In the past 5 years,
has there ever been a period of 6 months or more when
you had any of the following sexual problems or concerns
that caused you signiﬁcant distress?’. This modiﬁed version
of Flynn et al.’s19 single-item screener, which was validated
in US samples,19 allowed for rapid assessment. Adaptations
for use in this project included addressing distress (per
DSM-5 criteria);1,4 using plain language, to avoid medical
jargon;23 and extending the recall period from 1 to 5 years for
a less restrictive timeframe of relatively recent SD experiences.

Data collection

Methods
Study design
We used a qualitative exploratory research design to examine
participant perspectives18 using semi-structured interviews
with Montreal-based GBM with HIV. This study received
ethics approval from the McGill University Faculty of Medicine
and Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (#24-05-008).

Eligibility criteria
To be included in this study, participants had to: (1) be at aged
≥18 years; (2) identify as a man; (3) identify as gay/bisexual;
or, if not gay/bisexual-identiﬁed, have had sex with another
man in the past 5 years; (4) be based in the Montreal area; (5)
have received a positive HIV diagnosis; and (6) self-report to
have experienced any of ﬁve manifestations of SD (reduced
libido, ED, premature ejaculation, delayed ejaculation and/or
pain during anal sex) in the past 5 years. The SD needed to last
≥6 months19 and be associated with a signiﬁcant level of
distress. This deﬁnition of SD aligns with DSM-5 diagnostic
criteria.1

Quota sampling was used to obtain a diverse sample of the

After providing consent, eligible participants completed a
sociodemographic survey on LimeSurvey24 covering age;
education, annual income and employment (as proxies for
socioeconomic status);25 migration status; current partnership
status; and years living with HIV. These variables were selected
based on previous literature linking them to SD in people with
HIV.13,22 Participants were then contacted via email to
schedule a semi-structured interview by video or phone call.
Consistent with the established quotas, we aimed for at least
30 interviews to ensure a breadth of experiences across age
groups and SDs. The interview guide (Box 1) began with a
clariﬁcation of ‘sexual dysfunction’ to ensure a shared understanding for the interviewees, followed by questions about its
causes and impacts. Participants chose their own pseudonyms
for use throughout the study and were compensated 35 CAD
for participation.

Statistical analysis
Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed through
Microsoft’s transcription tool (an institution-approved
software), then revised for accuracy. We used an intelligent
verbatim approach, which omits non-essential elements (e.g.
pauses, silences, repetitions, stutters), unless contextually
relevant (e.g. laughter, ironic tone).26
Data were analyzed with Atlas.ti 25.0.127 using Braun and

Existing research on SD among people with HIV has
predominantly investigated its predictors. Identiﬁed factors
include biological causes, determined or exacerbated by HIV
(e.g. HIV-related inﬂammation, low testosterone, neuropathy);7
psychological issues (e.g. depression, anxiety); and relational
stressors (e.g. HIV stigma).13 Iatrogenic eﬀects, such as the
impact of antiretroviral therapy (ART), remain controversial,
with mixed ﬁndings on their role in SD.14,15 The unique sexual
culture of GBM is also worth considering. For instance, the
emphasis on physical attractiveness can contribute to body
image dissatisfaction and performance anxiety related to
practices such as anal sex, casual sex or group sex.7 Moreover,
illicit drugs commonly consumed by GBM for the purpose of
enhancing sex can also impair sexual functioning.7
Despite their potential to improve understanding of the
processes underlying SD and HIV, qualitative methods are
underutilized in this ﬁeld, and mainly focus on changes in
sexual behavior after HIV diagnosis, HIV disclosure and
sexual risk.16 Patient perspectives are crucial in healthcare
research, as they provide insights into the personal and
relational dimensions of health conditions, which are often
not captured by clinical assessments.17 Understanding their
perspectives is essential to develop eﬀective, patient-centered
SD interventions. Hence, the aim of this study was to use
qualitative methods to explore the perceived causes and
impacts of SD among GBM with HIV.

Results
Sample
Ninety-four people responded to the screening survey. Fifteen
were excluded for either not completing the consent checklist
or failing to meet the inclusion criteria. Seventy-nine were
deemed eligible to participate; however, 28 did not reply
after being contacted to schedule an interview. Twenty
respondents were excluded, as they were suspected of being
imposter participants.31
The ﬁnal sample comprised 31 participants with a median
age of 46 years (range 27–74 years). Just over half were
immigrants (n = 16; 51.6%), and 13 (41.9%) were single.
Two-thirds (n = 20; 64.5%) held a university degree.

Although most were employed (n = 19; 61.3%), nearly 40%
(n = 12) reported an annual income <20,000 CAD.
Participants had been living with HIV for a median of
15 years (range 2–36 years).
Reduced libido (n = 26; 83.9%) and ED (n = 25; 80.6%)
were the most common SDs. Over half of the participants
(n = 18; 58.0%) experienced three concurrent SDs
(median 3; range 1–4), most frequently reduced libido, ED and
delayed ejaculation (n = 11, 35.5%). Only four participants had
just one SD. Table 1 provides full demographic and SD
prevalence details.

provide a detailed presentation of each theme. Fig. 1 presents

Physical health. This theme captures participants’ view

Hormones. Several participants linked hormone levels,

STIs. STIs were perceived as a cause of SD, as they often

Living with HIV
Living with HIV was in itself considered a cause of SD, in
terms of HIV diagnosis and stigma, and the potential sideeﬀects of ART.
HIV diagnosis and stigma. Participants discussed various
aspects of HIV stigma and struggles with the initial HIV
diagnosis period, HIV disclosure, and fears of rejection and
criminalization for non-disclosure. Sam (aged 33 years),
Mathieu (aged 45 years) and Juan (aged 45 years) described
a period of low mood and withdrawal from sex after receiving
their HIV diagnosis. Adam (aged 32 years) opened up about
his experiences of rejection due to his serostatus, and Benoit
(aged 50 years) recalled a moment where anxiety about
disclosing his HIV status led to sexual performance issues
and subsequent feelings of inadequacy. Having lived with
HIV for several decades, Louis (aged 60 years), Richard
(aged 74 years) and Réal (aged 71 years) reﬂected on their
experiences following their diagnoses during a time when
facing death seemed inevitable, emphasizing the profound
shift in priorities and the existential challenges they
encountered, including their approach to sex.
Potential ART side-eﬀects. Several participants voiced
concerns about the potential sexual side-eﬀects of ART.
Laurent (aged 48 years) and Bernard (aged 40 years) noticed
a decline in their sex drive after switching to another ART
regimen, although they remained uncertain whether their
SD was due to the medication itself or psychological factors.
Louis (aged 31 years) expressed frustration and confusion
about his HIV medication, noticing decreased libido after
starting ART and an increase when missing doses. He also
struggled with trust in his medication, fearing it might not be
fully eﬀective in making him undetectable, which heightened
his anxiety about HIV transmission. Richard (aged 74 years)
reﬂected on ﬁrst-generation ART and its cardiovascular sideeﬀects as possible contributors to his current ED.

Discussion

Aging, health and life stressors
Previous research has observed that SD is common in older
men, with an earlier onset among those with HIV (middle age)
compared with the general population (aged >70 years).7
Aging was perceived not only as a source of challenges, in
terms of SD, health problems and relational issues, but as
leading to emotional growth, and opportunities for wisdom
and self-awareness. Qualitative studies regarding aging and
sexuality indicate that although aging introduces physiological
barriers to sex, it also fosters emotional maturity, and a
redeﬁnition of intimacy and desire.35
Aging and HIV are also notably related to physical health
problems brought up by our participants, including conditions,
such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease and cancer, all known
predictors of SD.7 Participants also hypothesized that low
testosterone was linked to reduced libido or ED; however,
testosterone replacement therapy, for them, did not result in
improvement. This is consistent with ﬁndings about low serum
testosterone among men with HIV likely not being entirely
responsible for SD.7
STIs were also described as a cause of SD. STIs causing
genital/anal ulceration, inﬂammation or scarring can

contribute to SD by aﬀecting genital sensitivity, causing
discomfort and inducing anxiety about sex.36 These physical
and psychological eﬀects may lead people to avoid sex,
experience distress related to intimacy or struggle with
arousal and performance, further exacerbating SD.36
Participants noted how adverse life events can have lasting
eﬀects; histories of sex work and sexual abuse were
particularly impactful, aligning with studies showing how
these experiences can disrupt sexual functioning.37 Participants
who attributed their SD to mental health issues noted the
impact of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (often used
to treat depression) on libido, ejaculation and erection. This
is consistent with research on selective serotonin reuptake
inhibitors and their common sexual side-eﬀects aﬀecting up
to 80% of users.38,39
These ﬁndings suggest that a comprehensive approach to
treating SD among GBM with HIV may be appropriate, one
that addresses the intersection of aging, physical/mental
health conditions and life stressors, not just as risk factors,
but also as experiences shaping SD and sexuality. In line with
participants’ insights, such eﬀorts could stress that aging is not
solely a process of decay and loss of youth, but an opportunity
to redeﬁne intimacy and build resilience, while taking care of
its related challenges.

HIV and SD
Living with HIV was linked to anxiety about disclosing
serostatus, experiences of rejection and stigma, and fears of
criminalization under Canada’s HIV disclosure laws, which,
to participants, contributed to SD. These challenges were
perceived to disrupt intimacy, echoing previous studies on the
negative eﬀects of HIV stigma on sex and relationships.40
Notably, such experiences persist in the age of Undetectable =
Untransmittable, which conﬁrms that people with an undetectable viral load cannot transmit the virus.41 This highlights
the ongoing need to educate the public on Undetectable =
Untransmittable, and advocate for reforming laws that
criminalize HIV non-disclosure.
Concerns about ART’s side-eﬀects on sexual function were
common. Literature remains inconclusive about links between
speciﬁc medication regimens and SD,7 and participants’
uncertainty on the topic echoes this ambiguity. It is also
possible that initiating or switching ART may be associated
with perceived SD, as patients often attribute new symptoms
or health conditions to recent changes in their treatment
regimen.14 Given that most studies in this area are over a
decade old, there is a clear need for updated research on the
eﬀects of newer ART regimens on SD to better inform
patients and health providers about their potential sexual
side-eﬀects.

To our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst qualitative study to explore
the perceived causes and impacts of SD among GBM with HIV.
This research adds novel insights into the multifaceted and
interrelated biopsychosocial factors inﬂuencing SD in this
population, building on patterns identiﬁed by quantitative

three, with the most common being inhibitory in nature:
reduced libido, ED and delayed ejaculation. Premature
ejaculation was the least commonly reported SD, consistent
with literature on its low prevalence among gay men.33
This pattern relates to the dual control model of sexual
functioning, which emphasizes the eﬀect of excitatory and
inhibitory inﬂuences on sexual response.34 This suggests
that SD in our sample may be primarily driven by heightened
inhibitory mechanisms. We discuss our identiﬁed themes
further highlighting the multifaceted nature of SD: biopsychosocial challenges across the lifespan, living with HIV
and gay sexual culture.

Struggling and adapting to SD
The negative emotions (e.g. frustration, inadequacy)
experienced by participants mirror previous research linking
SD to poor mental health outcomes.46 Struggling to meet
partners’ expectations can lead to relational strain. Indeed,
mismatched libido is a common problem among couples
seeking sex therapy.47 Despite these struggles, participants
showed various adaptation strategies. Some viewed reduced
libido as an opportunity to refocus on non-sexual aspects of
life or deepen emotional intimacy. Although the perceived
causes of SD were biopsychosocial, its impacts were mainly
psychological and relational. Healthcare professionals should
consider the broader emotional and interpersonal consequences
of SD by addressing both emotional/relational distress and
adaptation strategies. Integrating sexual function into the
primary care of people with HIV ensures it remains a key
component of quality of life assessments and patient-reported
outcomes. Future research should explore long-term coping
trajectories, investigating how GBM with HIV adapt to
changes in sexual functioning over time and across diﬀerent
relationship contexts.

Limitations
This study has several limitations. Self-selection bias may
have inﬂuenced participation, skewing the sample toward
extroverted men open and interested in discussing the
topic, given the high representation of men with experience

of multiple types of SD. The sensitive nature of the topic
may have led to incomplete narratives, as some participants
might have withheld certain details. However, many shared
deeply personal stories, including sexual abuse, suggesting
the interviews fostered candid discussions, mitigating some
concerns about underreporting.
Data relied on self-reported SD by participants, rather than
diagnostic records or clinical interviews, thus being subject to
recall biases. However, it is important to note that most SD
care is based on self-reported symptoms and discomfort by
patients.48
Although qualitative research is context-speciﬁc, our
ﬁndings may also be relevant to heterosexual men with HIV
and to HIV-negative men in the general population living in
other large urban centers in high-income countries comparable
to Montreal.

Conclusion
Participants identiﬁed biopsychosocial challenges across the
lifespan, living with HIV and gay sexual culture as causes of
SD. Although SD led to emotional and relational distress,
some participants were able to cope and adapt to it. Clinicians
could integrate psychological and relational support into SD
care, addressing patient-perceived causes and promoting
resilience. A structured clinical checklist based on these insights
could help guide discussions, ensuring that interventions align
with patient needs.
