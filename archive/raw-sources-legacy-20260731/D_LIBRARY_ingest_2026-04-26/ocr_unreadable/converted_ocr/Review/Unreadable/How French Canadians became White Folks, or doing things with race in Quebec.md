ABSTRACT

As far as nineteenth century Anglosaxons were concerned, French Canadians
were not quite white. This article considers the racial dimension of FrenchCanadian subjugation, long elided and ignored by historians. However, the
descendants of French Canadians are no longer construed, and no longer
construe themselves, as a racialized group, but rather as a modern, white,
western nation. These changes in racial assignment still inﬂuence how French
and English Canadians make sense of Canadian politics and identities today.
Although Quebec’s dual majority/minority status in Canada has been well
studied, this article also taps into critical race and whiteness studies to
examine how notions of evolution, civilization and progress continue to
inﬂuence how to do things with race in Quebec.

Brodkin and other more recent works, like Wendy Hui Kyong Chun’s ‘Race

to contribute to these debates by expanding the ﬁeld of Critical Whiteness
Studies to consider the complicated racial identities of Canada’s Frenchspeaking province of Québec. Although the word ‘race’ is rarely employed
today, as Alana Lentin and others have pointed out, it silently underscores
and animates Canadian and Québécois identities, often eliding uneven
power relations that exist among or between social groups (see Bilge 2013,
158; Lentin 2012, 12). On close inspection, the particular ways in which
French-Canadian and Québécois identities are racialized are noteworthy, in
part because we tend to forget that race is (and has always been) so pivotal
to these identities, but also, as we will see, because of race’s remarkable
instability and relationality. Following Wendy Hui Kyong Chun’s lead, I will
ask and attempt to answer the question ‘what does race do’ in Québec?
Chun’s reframing of simplistic biological and cultural deﬁnitions of race
allows me to examine the ways that race is cited or recycled, rejected or
claimed and think through the conceptual consequences of these relational
processes in the context of Quebec.

Critical race and whiteness studies
From biologist Stephen Jay Gould (1996) to feminist science studies scholars
Evelynn Hammonds and Rebecca M. Herzig (2009), decades of research in the
sciences and the social sciences have revealed race to be a biological illusion.
Critical race theorists remain interested in the ways that we ignore these
scientiﬁc facts, yet it is also clear that even as we pry race away from
biology, ‘racism is as healthy today as it was during the Enlightenment’ (Morrison 1993, 63). Since the late 1980s, critical race theorists have also turned
their attention to hegemonic constructions of whiteness. In Western societies,
whiteness is normalized to such an extent that, as Richard Dyer and others
point out, white people are not often racially seen and named. White
people often assume themselves to be ‘just’ people, generic human beings,
not belonging to a racial grouping. For this reason, according to Dyer, and
other scholars in the ﬁeld, ‘the point of looking at whiteness is to dislodge
it from its centrality and authority, not to reinstate it’ (Dyer 1997, 10). But
the social deﬁnition of ‘white’ has changed over the years and researchers
such as Karen Brodkin, Noel Ignatiev and many others (see also Dyer 1997;
Frankenberg 1993; McClintock 1995; Morrison 1993; Roediger 1999, etc.)
study the ways that groups of people that we now consider to be white,
like Irish and Jewish communities, have at times been portrayed as racially
different or Other. For example, Ignatiev examines the ways that the Irish
‘became white’ and explores ‘how the Catholic Irish, an oppressed race in
Ireland, became part of an oppressing race in America’ (Ignatiev 1995, 2).

Brodkin analyses what she calls the ‘unwhitening’ and the ‘whitening’ of Jewish
people throughout history, always contingent on the social and economic
factors at play. It is clear from Ignatiev and Brodkin’s analysis that race is
many things at once: a socially constructed category used to naturalize inequality; an individual and collective identity for both oppressed and oppressive
groups of people; a discursive practice; a political tool that can be exploited
by what Stuart Hall calls diametrically opposed doctrines (Hall 1997). But as
Richard Dyer explains:
Given the overwhelming advantage of being white, in terms of power, privilege
and material well-being, who counts as white and who doesn’t is worth ﬁghting
over – ﬁghting to keep people out, to let strategic groups in, ﬁghting to get in.
(Dyer 1997, 52)

New research has begun to add interesting dimensions to how racial discourses create corporeal realities, not the other way around. For Nadine
Ehlers, race is performative because, through repetition, race brings into
being what it names. Ehlers’ Racial Imperatives; Discipline, Performativity, and
Struggles Against Subjection offers us a valuable performative and disciplinary
model of race:
… rather than being corporeal ‘truths’, blackness and whiteness are a) normative and regulatory ideals, b) coercive demands, and c) forms of power, as
they are enmeshed with certain forms of knowledge that invest bodies [ … ].
These knowledges that invest the body create race as a corporeal reality, the
truth of which is supposedly located in (and constructed through the semantics
of) colour and blood. (2012, 5)

Ehlers’ account of race as performative allows me to ask, what are the normative and regulatory imperatives of black and white racial ‘truths’ that create
French Québécois as black and/or as white? Likewise, Wendy Hui Kyong
Chun provocatively suggests that instead of asking ourselves what race is,
we might ask what does race do? In a special issue of the journal Camera
Obscura, Chun and other contributors advocate for a technological deﬁnition
of race: ‘Race as technology … displaces ontological questions of race –
debates over what race really is and is not, focused on discerning the difference between ideology and truth – with ethical ones: what relations does
race set up?’ (2009, 28). Given this reconceptualization of race as a performative technology rather than a somatic or ideological truth, let us now examine
evidence that points to the ways that French Canadians were once racialized2
in order to pose a series of ethical instead of ontological questions.

In Québec, heroic myths and colonial adventures about voyageurs, coureurs
des bois, draveurs and trappeurs also often hide the reality of a violent conquest. Scholars like Martin Allor argue that Canadian myths of origin are
based on the reconstruction of British and French colonialism as the acts of
two founding ‘races’ and that Canadian constitutional recognition of cultural
duality created a theory of empty land, of terra nullius, which erased Canada’s
First Nations (1994, 35–36). Notwithstanding the fact that ‘the pre-colonial
history of the French in Quebec was an imperialistic one’ (Hutcheon 1995,
132), interestingly, English colonizers also once thought of and described
French colonizers in speciﬁcally racialized terms, despite what now seems
like the striking similarity of their white skin. For example, the expression
‘speak white’3 testiﬁes to a historic rhetorical link between discrimination
against people of colour and that of French Canadians and the French
language in Canada. This expression was once a common insult used by
English Canadians to slight French Canadians. In 1897, for example, speaking
in French, Henri Bourassa opposed the ideas of his English-speaking colleagues in Parliament and was told to ‘speak white’ (Lacoursière, Provencher,
and Vaugeois [1969] 2001, tome IV, 89). This insult is nothing less than a racial
slur used to dismiss and demean the French language interests Bourassa was
defending in Parliament. To ignore the racial dimension of the insult ‘speak
white’ is to ignore the inextricable role race plays in the relationship
between French and English settlers. Although evidence of the racialization
of French Canadians presented in this article is somewhat limited due to
space restraints, it is nonetheless emblematic of the dominant nineteenth
century frame through which French Canadians were construed and depicted
by English-Canadian elites at the time.
After the Patriot Rebellions (which took place in Lower and Upper Canada
in 1837 and 1838), Lord Durham ([1839] 1969) was sent by England to Canada
to investigate colonial grievances. His Report on the Affairs of British North
America (or The Lord Durham Report) was published in 1839 and represents
a major document in Canadian history that ultimately led to the Act of
Union, to Confederation and ultimately to the founding of the Canadian
nation-state. Interestingly, the word race appears 32 times in the document

He famously states, for example, ‘I expected to ﬁnd a contest between a government and a people. Instead … I found a struggle, not of principles, but of

Over and over again, French Canadians are described as inferior in the
Durham report. Some scholars have argued that the use of the word ‘race’
is simply representative of the way the word was used at the time, which,
of course, is true (see, for example, Ajzenstat 1988; or Burroughs 1972). But
as we will see, the concept of race, not just the word, is essential to the arguments Durham made about French-Canadian assimilation.
The stories of early explorers and their ‘discoveries’ are anchored in narratives about the white man’s adventurous spirit and tenacity, which is central
to the construction of white supremacy and colonialism. According to Dyer:
Enterprise is an aspect of both spirit itself – energy, will, ambition, the ability to
think and see things through – and of its effect – discovery, science, business,
wealth creation, the building of nations, the organization of labour (carried
out by racially lesser humans) [ … ] white people lead humanity forward
because of their temperamental qualities of leadership: will power, far-sightedness, energy. These were the very qualities [ … ] posited as characteristic of the
white race. (1997, 31)

Thus, what supposedly makes the white man superior is his ostensible enterprising nature. Whiteness and masculinity are inseparable in such colonial
imaginings as women did most certainly not tend to be valued for their
energy, will, ambition, intelligence and were largely excluded from the
business of discovery, science or wealth creation. These were, and sometimes
still are, characteristics and abilities socially valued in men. When Durham
suggests that Anglo-Canadians would predominate because of ‘their superior
knowledge, energy, enterprise, and wealth’ ([1839] 1969, 44), he is calling on
an already established discourse of white supremacy and patriarchy to
support his argument.
Consequently, when Durham describes French Canadians as ‘not so civilized, so energetic, or so money-making a race as that by which they are surrounded’ ([1839] 1969, 145), he is employing the racist rhetoric that Anne
McClintock calls ‘racial idleness’ or ‘black sloth’: ‘Of all the stigmata of degeneration invented by the settlers to mark themselves from the Africans, the most
tirelessly invoked was idleness [ … ]’ (McClintock 1995, 252). In colonial

In addition to the Durham Report, which coupled French Canadians with
racial discourses, several political cartoons from nineteenth century Canadian
newspapers and magazines depict French Canadians as ‘not quite white’. The
brief analysis of two political cartoons that follows is in no way meant to be
exhaustive, but rather, when considered in relation to other evidence presented above, is emblematic of the dominant nineteenth century frame
through which French Canadians were racialized by English elites at the
time. As a form of ‘satirical journalism and a type of visual opinion news discourse’ (Greenberg 2002, 182), political cartoons blend ‘normative prescriptions and factual beliefs’ and ‘offer newsreaders condensed claims or mininarratives about putative “problem” conditions and draw upon, and reinforce,
taken-for-granted meanings of the world’ (Greenberg 2002, 182). The cartoons analysed below were chosen because they illustrate the racialization
of French Canadians at this particular point in Canadian history. Speciﬁcally,
in the two political cartoons I analyse in this article, French Canadians are portrayed as primates.
Apes and monkeys have often been a prominent feature of scientiﬁc
racism. In the eighteenth century, scholars were obsessed with the hunt for
the ‘missing link’ in human evolution from ape to man, and anthropologists
often likened Hottentots, and later Australian Aboriginals, to monkeys and
chimpanzees (see Dubow 1995, 22). In the nineteenth century, prominent
anthropologists like Elliot Smith compared what he called ‘primitive’ races
to ‘man-like apes’ in their supposedly ‘limited innate ability to invent civilization’ (Barkan 1992, 41). For McClintock, monkeys were ‘fetishes straddling
nature and culture’ and ‘were seen as allied with the dangerous classes: the
“apelike” wandering poor, the hungry Irish, Jews, prostitutes, impoverished
black people [ … ] who were collectively seen to inhibit the threshold of

And the production of French Canadians as not quite white subjects relies on

But a cautionary note is needed at the end of this analysis. It would be
incorrect to conclude that Anglosaxons simply misinterpreted the otherwise
objective whiteness of French Canadians. I will argue, in the ﬁnal section of
this article, that French Canadians were in fact as unwhite in the nineteenth
century as French Québécois are white today. The question we must return
to is not whether or not French Canadians and Québécois are really white;
rather, what did and does race do and what relations did and does it set up?

The whitening of Québec
Many once-distinct immigrants of ‘not quite white’ European origins, like Irish,
Italians and Jews, have been redeﬁned as a singular Euro-American ‘white’
population within which, as Massey and Sanchez point out, ‘ethnic identiﬁcation was an “option” that was voluntary and contingent’ (Massey and
Sanchez 2010, 12). In Quebec, while French Quebecois are today linguistically,
culturally and politically distinct, they are no longer considered, and no longer
consider themselves, to be racially distinct. So how then did ‘not quite white’
French Canadians become white Québécois? Although an in-depth analysis of
this process is beyond the scope of this article, a few key moments and issues
are worth developing here.
In the 1960s, during Quebec’s Quiet Revolution, Pierre Vallières’ provocative manifesto Nègres blancs d’Amérique (White Niggers of North America), challenged Québécois class injustice and exploitation and seemed, at least by its
title, to reappropriate the ‘négritude’ once attributed to French-speakers by
likening this struggle to that of the African-American civil rights movement
in the USA. The social, cultural and economic changes that occurred during
1960s Quebec were profound and have been well studied, yet we have
only begun to examine the ways that Quebec intellectuals and activists
often availed themselves of cross-racial metaphors like ‘nègre blanc’ (see
also Brière 2003; Clarke 1996; Scott 2014). Like many American Jews, French
Québécois tend to think of themselves as ‘invested in social justice and in
