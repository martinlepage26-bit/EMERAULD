A Note on Anomalous Experiences of Children and Young People
Children commonly have experiences viewed as ‘anomalous’, ‘peak’ or ‘exceptional’ (see
Lovelock & Adams, 2017; Rodriguez, 2017). Coles (1990) identified a range of children’s

experiences involving nature; near death or health-crisis episodes; peak moments during

Methodology
The pilot study used a participatory approach with children and young people that
experimented with interdisciplinary research methods (art, play, movement and a self-enquiry
activity - ‘Take a Selfie). Participatory research aims to empower children and young people
in research practice by ‘challenging the donor recipient model of top down, academically
informed practice or policy recommendations’ (see Author B & Larkins, 2019). Children and

Fo

young people shaped the methodology in research moments. Elwick & Green (2020) refer to

rP

these as ‘moments of wonder’ when there are ‘interruptions in the flow of things when
something catches the attention and makes us think again, and anew’ (338). The pilot study

ee

was designed to work with a small group of children and young people. This was intended to

rR

afford opportunities to spend time with individual participants and learn from the pilot. In this
way, the study was partly pre-designed and partly shaped by children throughout the process
(see Author B & Larkins, 2019).

eastern philosophical traditions (see Barua, 2015). It is a process of inner exploration that
searches for the ‘I’ of experience and can promote wellbeing. We (the children, young people
and researcher) developed a method called ‘Take a Selfie’ (see Author A, 2021). This involved
blending the Vedantic ideas of self-enquiry, with the cultural phenomena of ‘the Selfie’. Choi
& Bhem-Morowitz (2018) note how ‘selfies’ have been observed in relation to narcissism and

Discussion
Returning to the study of anomalous experiences in childhood, exposes problems concerned
with a dominant worldview, research methods and our understanding of what it means to be
human. Interdisciplinary research approaches are long overdue in studies concerned with
anomalous experience- as is the engagement of children and young people in this type of
research. As Wilde & Murray (2010) note ‘the study of anomalous experience would be further
enhanced if research used qualitative [and] idiographic approaches’ (59).
