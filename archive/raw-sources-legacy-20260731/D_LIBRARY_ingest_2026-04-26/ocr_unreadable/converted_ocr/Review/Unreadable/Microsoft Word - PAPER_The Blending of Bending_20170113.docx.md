The Blending of Bending:
World-building in Avatar:
The Last Airbender and Legend of Korra
Thomas Van Hoey Z2

Linguistics in Comics and Animation
>SR"
Autumn 2016
Abstract
Nickelodeon’s animated television series Avatar: The Last Airbender (ATLA) and Avatar: Legend
of Korra (LOK) have made a considerable impact since they have been released, more than a
decade ago. One of the most fascinating elements is the world in which the story is set—it
integrates both low-level elements from various Eastern as well as Western cultures. In this
intertextual tapestry a huge correlative network is identified, which rests on the conceptual
blending of PEOPLE and NATURE, similar to correlative networks in Chinese five-elemental
theory and Western humourism. Similar cognitive blending processes are then identified for
the hybrid animals in the series. A third example is the blending of modern elements into the
previously established world of ATLA during LOK. These underlying blends and low-level
intertextual blends are greatly responsible for the success of both series.

Keywords:
Blending theory, Avatar, ATLA, LOK, four elements

1. Introduction
The opening sequence above explains in very few words the premise of Avatar: The Last
Airbender. This was an animated cartoon that ran on Nickelodeon from February 2005 until
July 2008, and which was created by Michael Dante DiMartino and Bryan Konietzko
(DiMartino & Konietzko 2010). It is about a world in which some people are able to bend the
four elements: water, earth, fire and air. The Avatar is the only person who can bend all four
elements. However, a hundred years before the story of this series he vanished from the
world and is now found by two Southern Water Tribe teenagers, the waterbender Katara and
her slightly older non-bender brother Sokka. Together they help the Avatar, called Aang, in
his quest against the imperialistic Fire Nation, in order to bring balance to the world. After
the ending of the series, a steady amount of comic books have been published that detail the
adventures of Aang and his friends after the first series.
Later, it also sparked an animated sequel, Avatar: The Legend of Korra. This ran from
2012 until 2014. This story is set 70 years after the first series and deals with the new Avatar,
Korra, and her quest to bring balance to the world. This in turn will be followed by a comics
series (to be published in the summer of 2017).
Let us first investigate the impact of Avatar in a more detailed manner.

A second measure to gauge the impact of a series is by looking at the awards it has received.
The first series, Avatar: The Last Airbender (abbreviated as ATLA) has won many awards,
including 5 Annies (a prize for excellence in animation); 1 Genesis award (for raising
awareness on animal-related issues), and 1 Emmy award. Meanwhile, Avatar: The Legend of
Korra (hereafter LOK) has won 2 Annie awards, 7 IGN awards (a prize for entertainment), as
well as numerous voice acting awards.

A third measure is the expansion into other media and products, which can for
instance also be seen in Japanese serials like Dragonball, Naruto, One Piece, and of course
Pokémon. The first three have in common that they started out as a comic (manga >C) but
later received an animated version (anime ), which was followed by separate animated
movies, more merchandise and even museum exhibitions and dedicated stores. Pokémon
started out as a game by Nintendo and then got its anime, movies and merchandise.
Conversely, the Avatar series started out as an animated cartoon of American origin. Only
then did it get a comic book sequel. It did, however, also get a live-action movie in 2010, called
The Last Airbender, directed by M. Night Shyamalan. IMDb ratings, as well as Rotten Tomato

heavy protests against the casting of three white actors for roles that were clearly supposed
to be people of colour. It even sparked a website called ‘racebending.com’, a pun on the
bending of the elements that is central to Avatar (Lopez 2012). The racebending movement is
a grassroots organization of media consumers who advocate for underrepresented groups in
entertainment media, and are opposed to phenomena like ‘whitewashing’ and ‘yellowface’ in
e.g. movies and television.
Merchandise for ATLA includes Lego sets, toys with Burger King, a theme rollercoaster ride,
three video games (Avatar: The Last Airbender; The Burning Earth; Into the Inferno), and a
Massively Multiplayer Online Role-Playing Game (MMORPG): Avatar: Legends of the Arena.
The expansion would not have been possible without a large fan base and fan fiction culture,
a phenomenon that is not limited to Avatar but also occurs frequently with Japanese
franchises (see e.g. Leong 2010) or Western literary figures (see e.g. Noppe 2010).
Furthermore, fan fiction as a phenomenon has been the subject of many studies, as argued in
Hellekson & Busse (2006). Two important sources in this regard are the fan-edited Avatar wiki
(Avatar Wikia) and a blog on the platform tumblr, run by a certain Jin, called Avatar:The Last
Airbender Annotations (A:TLA Annotations). The Avatar Wiki holds more than 12,000 articles
and the tumblr page was updated mostly from 2011 to 2015.
That the fan base was large enough and diversified enough can be inferred by the intertextual
references that have been made to the Avatar series in other media. Some examples of
television series include: The Simpsons, Cold Case, and Community. In the domain of film we
have: Gulliver’s Travels (2010) and The Happening (2008), which was also directed by Shyamalan.
Additionally, A Very Potter Musical and its sequel A Very Potter Sequel reference to the series
multiple times (Avatar Wikia: "List of Avatar: The Last Airbender references in popular culture").
There is no doubt then that Avatar has made a large impact on Western popular culture,
therefore adaptions were made in French, German, Dutch, Hebrew, Hungarian, Italian,
Hungarian, Norwegian, Polish, Portuguese, Romanian, Russian and Spanish. Both ATLA and
LOK got adaptations in Mandarin Chinese and Korean; curiously, only the first season of ATLA

1.2.

Researching the Avatarverse

As can be seen above, Avatar left quite a mark on popular culture. What sparked my own
interest in this television show was the world in which the characters travelled, the bending
they used, and of course the story. Being a bit older than the target audience, I first watched
the Dutch version Avatar: De Legende van Aang [“Avatar: The Legend of Aang”, the Dutch
version of ATLA] when I was sixteen. The first element that stands out is the lifelike
animation, especially concerning the bending of the elements — ‘benders’ are able to
manipulate the four elements using martial arts-like moves. As a teenager, these kind of
movements of course held my interest. Next, the characters do not travel through an empty
world, but through a world that is well-built, with plenty of backstories. It feels real enough,
just like the magical world of Harry Potter is convincing, or Middle-Earth in The Lord of the
Rings, or Westeros in Game of Thrones. The story that takes place in this world also grabs your
attention. You just want to know how Aang will fare against the evil Fire Lord and whether
he will be able to save the world.
Watching reruns on the Dutch Nickelodeon channel, combined with my studies in Sinology
only deepened the interest I had in the show’s universe. This is because the writing system
that is adopted in the world of Avatar is Chinese characters and the language used resembles
premodern Chinese in most cases — in fact creators DiMartino and Kunietzko hired
calligrapher S.L. Yang to translate written messages (DiMartino & Konietzko 2010:126–129). I
suspected that characters were seen as a neutral script, because of their historical ubiquitous
usage in China and other East-Asian cultures like Japan, Korea and Vietnam; complete mutual
understanding, however, is a myth (DeFrancis 1984).
The three elements mentioned above – world-building, bending and story – all deserve their
own exploration. Fortunately, some scholarly research has been carried out before 1 . All

This blend between East and West is of course what makes the series so attractive for wider
audiences. And it is also what leads us to our research question: can we identify some crucial
cognitive blends (in the Cognitive Linguistic sense as developed in the work of Mark Turner
and Gilles Fauconnier) that underlie the Avatarverse? As mentioned by Gene Yang above, the
setting draws mostly on Asian elements while the narrative structure and the issues that are
addressed are mostly fundamentally Western (American). As such, we will not go fully into
the story itself, but we do recognize its importance as a source for Western elements. We will
provide the main lines of Aang’s quest and later Korra’s challenges in Appendix 1. Even
though we provide a summary of the almost 40 hours of anime, it is still a medium-long read,
in order to let the reader experience the depth of the story and the world in which it is set.

in this paper, viz. we will introduce the main works of the “Avatarverse” (cf. infra) and
introduce the theory of conceptual blending. This is followed by an introduction to
intertextual elements of the Avatarverse (section 2) and three case studies of blending in this
world (sections 3-5). This is followed by the discussion and conclusion (section 6).
As mentioned before, first we have to discuss the material on which this paper is based
and the methodology we will use to answer our research question.

1.3.

Material

As can be seen in the previous sections, the Avatarverse – the world in which the Avatar series
is set – has been a huge source of inspiration for numerous studies and fan-generated content.
But because we are concerned with the worldbuilding of the main series, we will limit
ourselves to what is to be considered as the canon. This includes the animated series of Avatar:

1.4.

Methodology

The theoretical framework this paper will use to explore the question of worldbuilding in the
Avatarverse rests mostly on the seminal work of Lakoff & Johnson (1980) on conceptual
metaphor theory, as well as its extension in conceptual blending theory. This was first
developed around the year 2000 by Gilles Fauconnier and Mark Turner (Fauconnier 1994;
1997; Fauconnier & Turner 2003; 2006) and saw some further development in the work of e.g.
Dancygier & Sweetser (2005), Coulson (2006), Oakley (2007), and Oakley & Hougaard (2008).
Two important works that support a big part of the argument in this paper are Hiraga’s (2005)
discussion of iconicity in literary works that uses conceptual blending theory, as well as
Kövecses’ (2007) work on metaphor and culture.
The basic principles of conceptual metaphor theory can best be exemplified by two
examples, based on the short introductions in Geeraerts (2010:203–222) and (Kövecses
2007:5–8). In (1) LOVE IS A JOURNEY and (2) MORE IS UP, LESS IS DOWN; we see a collection
of sentences that each have a figurative meaning. As such, they use certain words in their
non-literal way. In other words, they map the usage from its literal meaning unto a more
figurative meaning. Lakoff & Johnson’s big insight was that these sentences don’t exist
individually, separate from each other — instead they are mapped on a more fundamental
level, viz. mappings between concepts. They grouped sentences as those in (1) and (2) under
two conceptual metaphors, using the notation of TARGET IS SOURCE. The abstract idea of
8

‘love’ can thus be better understood in more concrete terms, such as in this case a ‘journey’;
hence, LOVE IS A JOURNEY. The same goes for the directional metaphor in MORE IS UP, LESS
IS DOWN: we can understand the abstract concepts of ‘more’ and its antonym ‘less’ in terms
of the directions ‘up’ and ‘down’.
(1) LOVE IS A JOURNEY
Look how far we’ve come. We are at a crossroads. We’ll just have to go our separate ways. We
cannot turn back now. We are stuck. This relationship is a dead-end street. I don’t think this
relationship is going anywhere. It’s been a long, bumpy road. We have gotten off the track.
(2) MORE IS UP, LESS IS DOWN
The number of books printed each year keeps going up. My income rose last year. The number
of errors he made is incredibly low. His income fell last year. He is under age. If you are too hot,
turn the heat down.

Two crucial aspects of conceptual metaphor theory are the directionality in the mapping
between the source and the target, and the deeply entrenched nature of the metaphors — it
is usually not an online conceptualisation, but an already deeply ingrained mapping.
However, sometimes this directionality is not clear, or the mapping is not well entrenched
and happens online. This happens especially in cases where the end result is somehow
different from either source or target. One illustrative case is the phrase flood of asylum seekers
or stop the flow of immigration (Hart 2008), in which asylum seekers (target) are thought of in
liquid terms, like water or in this case a flood (source). This is because they are typically
thought of as moving in large numbers. Furthermore, the idea of ‘flood’ of course has a
negative connotation, all contributing to the negative evaluation of these people who are
usually just looking for a safe place to stay, as seen in the ongoing (since 2015) refugee crisis
in Europe. Looking at those two exemplary phrases from a linguistic point-of-view, we see
that the metaphorical mapping between the two conceptual domains happens online, as part
of a political agenda to convince us that immigrants are a bad thing for a country and should
be kept out. How this online cognitive process between the two domains works can be
explained using the blending theory framework.

As can be seen in Figure 3Figure 3, we are dealing with two input spaces. Elements of these
input spaces can be linked to one another by generalizing elements in the generic space. The
interaction between these different mental spaces or cognitive domains then results in the
blended space, where the mapping between elements of the input spaces is fully formed. That
is why water can be linked to people, because they are both basic-level concepts; and flood
to migration because they both develop in time: flood as an event and migration as a process.
In other words, this generic space is what enables elements from the two input spaces to be
blended together.
With this explanation of the basic theoretical model we are ready to explore the
Avatarverse. The story can be found in appendix 1, as mentioned before.

was set in, similar to the Bayeux Tapestry in France that depicts the Norman conquest of
England in 1066. Looking from afar, we see a nice woven spectacle of the story. If we take
some steps closer — zoom in as it were — and study the make-up of the tapestry, it would
immediately be clear that we are dealing with threads of different origins in a myriad of
colours, e.g. Chinese, Japanese, Hinduist, Buddhist, Tibetan culture but also a major Western
cultural part (DiMartino & Konietzko 2010; Johnsson 2014; Tan 2014; Avatar Wikia; A:TLA
Annotations). Let us give two examples: names of the main characters and some of Western
