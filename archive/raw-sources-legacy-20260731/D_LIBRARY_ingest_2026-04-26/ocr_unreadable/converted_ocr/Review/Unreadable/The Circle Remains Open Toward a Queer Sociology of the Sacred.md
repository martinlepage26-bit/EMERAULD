**The Circle Remains Open: Toward a Queer Sociology of the Sacred**

**Introduction**

This article examines contemporary witchcraft and queer Pagan practice in Montreal[[1]](#footnote-0). It is an essay about witches, but not the kind that hide in fairy tales or flicker briefly across Halloween decor. These witches are real, complex, laughing, wounded, fierce. They are queer and trans, urban and solitary, lineage-bound and iconoclastic. They invoke spirits, call down the moon, chant against pipelines and patriarchy. They gather in basements, in forests, on Zoom. They know the world is broken, and they cast spells not to escape it, but to touch it differently[[2]](#footnote-1).

Contemporary Paganism does not speak in dogma but in gesture, affect, and symbol. It is less a single religion than a spiritual cartography, a terrain of overlapping mythic maps, revived pantheons, seasonal rites, and sensual devotions[[3]](#footnote-2). In Montreal, this terrain takes on a particular contour: one shaped by linguistic hybridity, queer presence, and post-secular yearning[[4]](#footnote-3). Here, neo-Paganism is not merely inherited; it is conjured, claimed, stitched together from fragments of folklore, feminist politics, ecological urgency, trauma legacies, and digital subculture[[5]](#footnote-4). It is both ancient and future-facing, fiercely embodied and relentlessly fluid.

Yet, within these expansive, enchanted spaces, tension persists. Binaries endure: Goddess and God, male and female, the chalice and the blade. Sacred texts are often unwritten, but their scripts are deeply gendered[[6]](#footnote-5). Ritual form, even when improvised, can reproduce the heteronormative logics of lineage, polarity, and mythic balance. These moments, of misalignment, of discomfort, of reconfiguration, are not anomalies. They are sites of knowledge. They are the queer sacred made visible[[7]](#footnote-6).

How, then, do queer and trans practitioners move within such frames? How do they craft livable, magical lives at the margins of tradition? How do they refuse not the sacred, but the terms under which it is usually understood? This essay explores those movements, the ritual negotiations, symbolic reworkings, and embodied subversions through which LGBTQ neo-Pagans in Montreal queer the sacred, and are themselves reconfigured by its force. But more than that, it explores the epistemological stakes of taking such practices seriously. It asks what happens to the category of “religion” when those who have been cast as magical, heretical, or excessive begin to narrate their own theologies[[8]](#footnote-7).

Between October 2015 and December 2020, I entered this world not only as a researcher, but as a participant-witch. My fieldwork unfolded across full moon circles in basement temples, public rituals in community art spaces, and sweat-laced Beltane dances beneath open sky. I was present at initiation ceremonies, seasonal festivals, political spell-castings, and moments of conflict and reparation. I interviewed twenty practitioners in depth, but learned from many more, in whispered conversations around fire pits, through zines passed from cloak to cloak, in workshops on consent magic, ancestor reverence, and trans deity invocation[[9]](#footnote-8). The bodies and stories in this essay are drawn from these encounters: textured, fragmentary, and radically alive.

Some were solitary practitioners crafting rituals in bedrooms strewn with tarot cards and hormone vials. Others were high priestesses of longstanding covens, carrying scars and pride from decades of activist witchcraft. Many moved between lineages, traditions, and roles. They were animists and agnostics, kinksters and caretakers. Some came to Paganism through feminist reading groups; others through Tumblr aesthetics, queer trauma, ayahuasca ceremonies, or inherited Celtic spirituality[[10]](#footnote-9).

They were not always welcome. Some were misgendered during circle, asked to perform roles that denied their bodies, or pushed out of lineages that clung too tightly to binary gods[[11]](#footnote-10). But they stayed, or they returned, or they built new circles. They invoked trickster spirits, rewrote the Wheel of the Year, dressed as gods no one had yet named. They brought the sacred back from the edge, and refused to let it exclude them[[12]](#footnote-11).

My research situates itself within and against the growing literature on contemporary Paganism. Seminal works by Hutton (1999), Adler (2006 [1979]), Magliocco (2004), and Clifton (2006) have traced the historical and aesthetic genealogies of modern Witchcraft. Others, like Pike (2001) and Berger (1998), have begun to ask how Paganism interacts with gender, sexuality, and power. But few have placed queer and trans embodiment at the heart of their inquiry. This essay does not simply add LGBTQ voices to an existing frame; it recenters the frame around the ritual lives of those whose bodies, desires, and cosmologies do not fit[[13]](#footnote-12).

I draw from queer theory not aBs a topical lens but as a method of attunement. I am indebted to Butler’s performativity (1990, 1993), Sedgwick’s affective epistemologies (1990), Halberstam’s notion of queer failure (2011), and Muñoz’s call for queer futurity (2009). I also engage the anthropology of lived religion (Ammerman 2007; McGuire 2008), recognizing that sacred life happens not in creeds but in breath, touch, and rhythm. Feminist ethnography (Behar 1996; Abu-Lughod 1993) anchors my commitment to partiality, intimacy, and the politics of representation.

I write from within. My own queer, Pagan body is not outside the text, but one of its instruments. I cast circles, called quarters, invoked deities I did not always believe in. I shared meals, heartbreaks, and ecstatic dances with my interlocutors. Sometimes I was scholar; other times, initiate, ritualist, confidant. This positionality is not a methodological flaw but a condition of knowing[[14]](#footnote-13). It shaped not only what I was allowed to witness, but also how I was transformed by it.

This doubleness allowed me to trace the micro-politics of sacred space: the moments when someone’s voice cracked during invocation, not from error but from transition; when gendered metaphors collided with lived ambiguity; when a participant sat quietly outside the circle, not from disrespect, but from dissociation. These moments are not disruptions. They are the material of this essay[[15]](#footnote-14).

This essay asks:

* How do queer and trans neo-Pagans in Montreal express gender, embodiment, and cosmology through ritual?
* In what ways do they reproduce, resist, or reinvent sacred scripts?
* How does queering the ritual also queer the very category of religion?
* What does it mean to treat magic not as superstition, but as method, as knowledge, as resistance?

Rather than impose coherence, I map contradiction. I attend to both the healing and the harm, the spells that liberate and the liturgies that constrain. I write with love, with ache, and with a deep sense of debt to those who trusted me with their stories[[16]](#footnote-15).

This first section of this essay situates Montreal’s Pagan landscape and the traditions most central to this study: initiatory Wicca, Reclaiming Witchcraft, and their local adaptations. Section Two outlines the theoretical scaffolding, with attention to queer embodiment, performativity, lived religion, and the politics of normativity. Section Three details the methodological approach, grounded in reflexivity, affective accountability, and ritual participation.

Sections Four and Five offer the core ethnographic material. section Four examines how queer identity is performed, affirmed, and negotiated within ritual. section Five explores how power operates in these spaces, who speaks, who leads, who is seen, and who is rendered peripheral.

Section Six widens the circle. It queers the very category of “magic,” positioning it as an embodied, decolonial, and gender-dissident epistemology. It argues that queer neo-Paganism is not only a ritual genre but a spiritual method, a cosmology of care.

The conclusion does not seal the spell. It breaks it open. It reflects on what it means to think, to write, and to listen from within queer enchantment. It insists that to study magic is to be touched by it, and to allow that touch to change the terms of knowledge itself. This is not a conclusion, but an invocation. Let the altar remain unset. Let the story remain unfinished. Let the queer sacred speak.

**Montreal’s Pagan Landscape**

Montreal’s ritual landscape is neither cohesive nor static. It pulses with the rhythms of the moon, flares with sabbats, and reconstitutes itself in borrowed halls, whispered blessings, queer flirtations, and flickering candlelight. To walk into a Montreal Pagan ritual is to step into a layered space: a reclaimed church basement adorned with rainbow prayer flags; a park in the Plateau where a genderqueer priestess leads a Beltane dance; a queer-inclusive shop on Saint-Laurent where workshops on ancestor reverence and trauma magic spill into conversations about hormones and heartbreak. The city’s Pagan landscape is not defined by temples, but by networks, of people, herbs, zines, playlists, gestures. It is improvised, relational, enchanted.

This terrain unfolds within broader currents of contemporary Paganism, particularly initiatory Wicca and Reclaiming Witchcraft, yet it exceeds both. What follows is not a fixed map but a shifting constellation, of practices, memories, and political commitments. This map is queer not only in content, but in form: it resists linearity, embraces contradiction, and centers those at the margins of both religious and academic legitimacy.

Contemporary Paganism in Montreal presents itself less as a codified religion than as a plural, layered ecology[[17]](#footnote-16). It gathers witches, druids, animists, brujas, faeries, those who believe in many gods, no gods, or gods they invent themselves. Most practitioners I met were less concerned with doctrine than with texture: how the sacred felt, how it smelled, what it allowed them to do or become[[18]](#footnote-17). Rather than belief, what unites this field is practice. A transmasculine witch might host full-moon baths for trauma release, mixing spellwork with therapy. A femme astrologer might open ritual with Beyoncé lyrics and offer honey to Venus. A two-spirit circle might blend Cree cosmologies with Wiccan form.

The term “Paganism” itself has long been a site of projection, once a Christian category of exclusion, later reclaimed by countercultural movements[[19]](#footnote-18). In Montreal, it becomes something else again: a queer archive, a speculative cosmology, a living bricolage[[20]](#footnote-19). For some, Paganism is return; for others, invention. Most hold both truths at once, honoring Celtic ancestors while quoting queer theory, invoking Lilith while practicing Reiki, wearing sigils inked on skin while debating pronouns. They know the charge of the Goddess and the politics of the pronoun[[21]](#footnote-20). Paganism here is not a category but a field of play, a place to ritualize grief, to make the invisible tangible[[22]](#footnote-21).

If one logic defines this community, it is bricolage. Rituals are pieced together from memory, dreams, and digital threads[[23]](#footnote-22). A Samhain rite might combine Wiccan casting, Reclaiming chants, a moment for missing and murdered Indigenous women, and a dance to Florence and the Machine. The sacred emerges not from fidelity to form, but from the charge of presence[[24]](#footnote-23). “My altar is a mess, Kuan Yin next to Aphrodite next to glitter pinecones I found in the Plateau,” Zara, a nonbinary Reclaiming witch, told me. “It’s all sacred. It’s all me.”

This eclecticism is not random; it is politically alive. Rituals answer to the moment: grief after a hate crime, celebration after a name change, grounding before surgery, remembrance of queer ancestors lost to AIDS or exile[[25]](#footnote-24). Sacred practice is not separate from social life, it is one of its modalities[[26]](#footnote-25).

While many groups embrace innovation, others retain inherited forms. Wiccan covens often keep symbolic polarities of Goddess and God, chalice and blade[[27]](#footnote-26). Even among queer practitioners, these can echo heteronormative logics[[28]](#footnote-27). Lou (she/they), a trans femme practitioner, reflected: “I love the structure. It helps me feel held. But sometimes, I wish the Goddess had a penis, too.”

Such tensions between form and fluidity invite theoretical attention. Some rewrite scripts; others step outside them altogether. A few replace the Great Rite with rituals of transition, calling in directions not as genders but as actions, “to grieve, to protect, to open”. Montreal itself participates in these transformations. Its hybridity, its Catholic ghosts, its bilingual fractures, all shape ritual life). Sacred spaces are makeshift: artist studios, yoga rooms, anarchist bookstores. The circle is always provisional, the altar mobile.

To understand this fluid landscape, we need tools, conceptual as much as sensory. Theory, here, is not an afterthought but a compass. It orients us toward the politics embedded in every chant and candle. The thinkers that guide this essay, Butler, Sedgwick, Bell, Fraser, Muñoz, TallBear, help articulate how identity is enacted, how recognition operates, and how magic itself can be epistemological.

Nancy Ammerman and Meredith McGuire call for attention to lived religion: what people do, not what they say they believe. This insight animates the Montreal field, where theology is less spoken than enacted, through gesture, rhythm, and repetition. Sacred life here is made through the mundane: boiling herbs, whispering prayers in the bath, rearranging an altar. These are not peripheral acts but central ones, the daily texture of queer enchantment.

Judith Butler’s performativity reveals how identity, and ritual, take shape through repetition. To perform “the High Priestess” or call down the Goddess is never neutral; it draws on histories, desires, exclusions. When queer or trans practitioners modify these gestures, they are not breaking ritual but reanimating it. The sacred cracks, and queerness floods in.

Nancy Fraser’s (1997) and Axel Honneth’s (1995) theories of recognition deepen this reading. Within ritual, recognition is more than social, it is spiritual. Who may call the God? Who counts as a “real” witch? Queer participants describe both misrecognition and revelation, being erased by gendered roles, yet later asked to bless the circle, to be witnessed as whole. Recognition, here, is a sacred act, the difference between being tolerated and being believed.

Catherine Bell reminds us that ritual enacts power. Even in communities claiming horizontality, hierarchies persist, elders and novices, initiated and uninitiated, those with lineage and those without. For queer practitioners, ritual can both liberate and constrain. Reading through Bell, we see not purity but ambivalence: enchantment as both resistance and reproduction.

Following José Esteban Muñoz and Kim TallBear, queerness itself becomes method, a refusal of closure, an embrace of the not-yet. To queer ritual is to open it, to uncertainty, laughter, ghosts. To queer scholarship is to write from the body, to let emotion be data. This essay does not seek to define queer Paganism, but to move with it, to trace its spirals, to dwell in its ruptures. Theory is not opposed to magic; it is another form of it. The thinkers drawn upon here provide a vocabulary for understanding ritual as epistemological practice, as a way of knowing and unknowing, of conjuring worlds otherwise. Montreal’s neo-Pagan landscape thus emerges as both field and theory, both practice and proposition, a ritual commons where queerness is method, enchantment is critique, and the sacred remains gloriously under construction.

**Embodied Ethnography: Fieldwork as Ritual, Reflexivity as Spell**

This section details the methodological underpinnings of this study, but it also tells a story of intimacy, vulnerability, and enchantment. It is the story of how I entered this field not just as a researcher, but as a queer, practicing witch. The lines between observer and participant were never stable. I was invoked as often as I was invisible. This is not a flaw. It is the ground of my knowledge.

What follows is not simply a list of methods. It is an account of methodological entanglement. It is about what it means to do research in a space where every circle called is also a mirror, every chant a form of affective labor, every ritual a potential undoing.

Fieldwork for this study took place between October 2010 and December 2014 in Montreal and its surrounding regions. My entry into the field was not secured through formal letters or institutional endorsements. It happened through presence: showing up, staying quiet, asking questions, being seen. Access came through enchantment.

I attended open rituals in community spaces, private ceremonies in cramped apartments, festivals in wooded landscapes. I helped carry crates of candles, offered rides, and learned how to smudge without appropriating. I was invited not because I had credentials, but because I carried myself with care. Trust was the true method

In these spaces, I was rarely just an ethnographer. I was asked to call quarters, to cleanse the space, to help rewrite ritual texts. When someone cried, I held their hand. When someone asked about my practice, I answered honestly. This proximity was not a compromise of rigor, it was the condition of insight.

My methodology combined semi-structured interviews, participant observation, and sensory fieldnotes. But those words feel too clinical for what unfolded. I prefer to call it ritual ethnography. Interviews were not conducted. They happened, often after ritual, while sharing food, walking to the metro, or smoking under the stars.

Observation was not detached. It was embodied. I smelled the incense. I trembled in trance. I forgot where I was and came back crying. Fieldnotes were written on napkins, in notebooks, and in dreams. They recorded what was said, but also what was felt, unsaid, or refused.

I also collected ritual scripts, altar photos, chants, digital ephemera, and stories passed between bodies. These artifacts were not treated as data, but as fragments of cosmology.

I write this essay as a queer, white, settler researcher, fluent in both English and French, shaped by feminist thought, transfeminist kinship, and post-secular longings. My queerness shaped how I was seen and what I could see. I was not an outsider, but I was not neutral either. In some circles, I was welcomed as kin. In others, I had to earn trust slowly. My body carried its own ambiguities: gender-nonconforming, shifting, both marked and assumed. These ambiguities opened doors, but also made some rituals difficult to inhabit. I never stopped negotiating.

Following Donna Haraway (1991), I embrace situated knowledge. Following Kim TallBear (0213), I refuse the fantasy of detached expertise. My voice is in this essay not to claim authority, but to trace the conditions of my seeing.

Ethics were not administered through forms. They were practiced, ongoing, situated, imperfect. All interviews were conducted with consent, and names were changed unless participants requested otherwise. In a few cases, I destroyed notes upon request. But the deeper ethics were affective. I did not include everything I saw. I withheld stories that felt too raw, too sacred, or too unresolved. I respected the boundary between what was offered and what was shared. I also practiced what I now call “ritual confidentiality”: a refusal to expose the most fragile, potent parts of ritual life to academic scrutiny. Some spells should not be written. Some circles should remain unbroken.

The twenty formal participants ranged in age from 22 to 58. They identified across a spectrum of genders, sexualities, and spiritual lineages. Some were initiated in Wiccan traditions; others identified with Reclaiming, Druidry, eclectic paths, or no lineage at all. A few had left lineages after conflict or exclusion. They included trans women, genderqueer faeries, cis lesbians, agender witches, polyamorous priestesses, and nonbinary brujos. They came from diverse class backgrounds and were mostly white, though some identified as Indigenous, Métis, or mixed-race. Many were bilingual, and their rituals reflected Montreal’s linguistic and cultural hybridity. They did not speak with one voice. They argued, improvised, contradicted each other. Their unity was not in doctrine, but in devotion, to living lives that made room for enchantment.

This section has offered a portrait of a fieldwork process rooted in presence, responsiveness, and queer attunement. But it is also a spell. To do this kind of research is to be changed by it. I began with questions about gender and ritual. I left with an altar of my own, a dozen friendships, a deep sense of accountability, and a grief I cannot fully name. Some of what I learned can be cited. Some can only be carried. This essay is not just an account of queer neo-Paganism in Montreal. It is a testimony to what it means to witness, to be seen, and to believe that magic, real, aching, complicated magic, can shape not just the ritual, but the research.

## **Ritual Performance, Power, and the Queering of Magic**

This section traces how queer neo-Pagan practitioners in Montreal embody and negotiate their identities within ritual space, and how those performances of self become entangled with questions of authority, hierarchy, and knowledge. Through life narratives, participant observation, and sensory fieldnotes, we enter a world where gender and spirituality are not merely identities, they are verbs. They are acts of crafting, of world-building, of sacred experimentation. They are lived, rehearsed, and at times ruptured within the potent temporal space of the circle.

Ritual is not a neutral ground. It is a container shaped by cosmology, power, and affect. For queer practitioners, it is also a site of becoming, where embodiment is affirmed, questioned, and made otherwise. Within these circles, LGBTQ practitioners navigate sacred roles, symbols, and gestures. They move between celebration and discomfort, affirmation and friction. Rituals are never just rituals, they are mirrors, magnifiers, and portals. When queer and trans people step into a circle, they bring with them not only intention but history: the weight of having been misread, misnamed, misrecognized. Within ritual, the hope is not simply to be included, but to become visible in ways the outside world rarely permits.

Mira, a nonbinary femme witch, once said after calling the West: “It’s not that I believe the water spirits are real. It’s that they believe I am.” For many, ritual is not about metaphysical belief, it’s about relational recognition. It is the one place where the voice that cracks, the body in flux, the pronoun that changes, are not mistakes but magic. Ritual becomes autobiography with symbols. It is where the sacred and the social converge.

Traditional Pagan rites, especially in Wicca and Reclaiming, are built around repeated gestures: the casting of the circle, the calling of quarters, the drawing down of deity. These structures offer grounding and containment, but they also carry gendered expectations. The calling of elements is often gender-coded (Air as masculine intellect, Water as feminine emotion). The Great Rite symbolizes heterosexual union through the penetration of blade into chalice. Deities are invoked in pairs: Goddess and God. Roles are split by gender: High Priest and High Priestess.

For queer practitioners, this choreography can be both stabilizing and stifling. Julien (they/he), a genderqueer practitioner, told me: “I like the rhythm of the rites. But I always end up rewriting the lines in my head while I say them.” Others take a more radical approach: rewriting scripts, queering roles, disrupting polarity. One group replaced the four classical elements with states of being: Grief, Desire, Pleasure, and Rage. Another rewrote the Charge of the Goddess to include trans ancestors and femme dyke elders. These interventions are not just about language, they are about cosmology. They rewire the ritual’s symbolic system and conjure new maps.

Queer identity in ritual is not stable, it shapeshifts. Participants often described feeling “more real” during ritual. But what that meant varied: more embodied, more fluid, more powerful, more mythical. Eli (he/they), a transfeminine butch witch, described it this way: “In ritual I’m not Eli the trans guy. I’m the Keeper of the West. I’m the Vessel. I’m the snake. That’s not escapism, it’s expansion.” For others, ritual became a laboratory to test pronouns, energies, gendered archetypes. They would try on “priestess” one moon, “godform” the next. These were not contradictions, they were initiations. Gender in these spaces functions like a spell: a repeated, affective, performative act that generates its own truth through embodiment and belief.

Yet not all experiences were transformative. Many participants described moments of pain, exclusion, or exhaustion. One nonbinary person was asked to “stand with the women” during a ritual. Another was corrected for using “they” in a group invocation. Others were told their gender made them ineligible to lead). These were often the product of unconscious norms, scripts inherited without examination, but they left wounds. One fieldnote reads: “He sat outside the circle the whole time. No one asked why.”

Some responded with withdrawal; others with ritual refusal, stepping out of roles, changing words mid-spell, bringing in alternative symbols. Many began to form their own circles, where gender was not assigned but conjured. Improvisation became central, not as lack of preparation, but as a form of openness: to the body’s needs, to the spirits that arrive, to the unexpected grief that rises. Someone forgets the words and sings instead. Someone bursts into tears and the circle shifts to hold them. These are not failures of form, they are expressions of the sacred. “We’re always making it up,” Zara said. “That’s not chaos, it’s magic.” The queer sacred is not found in perfection, but in the glitch, the slip, the gasp.

### *Power in the Circle: Authority, Tension, and the Limits of Inclusion*

If queer and trans practitioners perform identity in sacred space, they must also contend with structure. Rituals are never only aesthetic, they are political. They are sites where legitimacy is negotiated, authority asserted, exclusions enacted. Power moves through Montreal’s queer Pagan spaces in subtle and overt ways: who leads, who is heard, who is believed, and who is silenced.

Alternative spiritualities are not free from hierarchy. The refusal of institutional structure can mask dynamics rooted in charisma, tradition, or implicit normativity. Queer practitioners are often praised for creativity but denied authority. They are asked to speak their truth, then redirected when it disrupts the flow. This is not hypocrisy, it is contradiction. It is the tension of naming power where others see only light.

The sacred circle is a choreography of roles: caller of quarters, invoker of deity, holder of space. Titles like “High Priestess” or “Elder” carry symbolic weight, even where hierarchy is disavowed. Yet who gets to lead? Many queer and trans participants described being invited to participate but rarely to guide. They were praised as visionaries but denied the microphone. “They say they want change,” said Simone, a nonbinary Reclaiming priest. “But when I bring it, they freeze. I’m allowed to be mystical, but not authoritative.”

Authority is not only spiritual, it is gendered, racialized, and embodied. Being seen as “serious” or “initiated” often aligns with cisnormative and classed codes. Inclusion becomes labor: translating one’s experience into palatable terms, educating others mid-ritual, absorbing microaggressions for the sake of harmony. Jay, a genderfluid witch, captured this exhaustion: “They said they wanted to learn. But every time we met, I had to teach. I wasn’t there to teach. I was there to call Fire.”

Some left. Some stayed and grew silent. Others reimagined leadership, rotating roles, rejecting fixed titles, invoking deities who shift and blur. As Zara said: “A place where no one is above, but everyone is radiant.” Power, in these spaces, becomes less about who calls the quarters and more about who feels held. Who arrives fully. Who is allowed to transform the ritual, and be transformed.

Even liberated circles can reproduce the social. Rituals that aim to heal can still wound. Yet to name these tensions is to honor the work. Queer practitioners are not naïve, they know inclusion is never guaranteed. But still they gather, call, invoke, remember. Their practices do not erase power, they queer it. They offer new ways to lead, to listen, to cast and be cast. The circle, like the world, remains imperfect. But it also remains possible.

### *Queering Magic: Epistemologies of Enchantment and the Politics of the Irrational*

From ritual to cosmology, from the structure of the circle to the ground it is drawn upon: the queer sacred must be taken seriously, not as inclusion within religion, but as insurgent knowledge that destabilizes distinctions between religion and magic, sense and nonsense, reality and the real). To queer magic is not to decorate altars with rainbows. It is to contest the colonial and theological hierarchies that rendered certain spiritualities illegible. Magic is not primitive nor naïve, it is a technology of queer survival, a way of knowing.

Magic, for many practitioners, is not metaphor but method. It is real because it transforms. It alters perception. It makes the impossible momentarily tangible. “Magic is how I survived trauma,” said Maria, a nonbinary medium. “It told me I wasn’t broken, I was porous.” Magic becomes epistemology: grounded not in proof, but in presence. It welcomes intuition, affect, synchronicity, the messy, the uncanny. To embrace magic is to embrace the irrational as resistance to normativity.

The boundary between religion and magic has long been policed by gendered and colonial ideologies[[29]](#footnote-28). Religion was said to be rational and ethical; magic, manipulative and primitive. Queer witches inherit this exclusion. When they invoke deities that shift gender, or build altars in drag, they are often dismissed as unserious. Yet these very gestures generate new modes of relation, to spirit, to land, to self. To queer magic is to reclaim it as a suppressed epistemology that privileges aesthetics over logic, becoming over being.

Magic is not only belief, it is praxis, a methodology for navigating the unknowable. In this sense, queer ritualists are theorists, experimenting with the alchemy of metaphor, the architecture of desire. Helena, a transfemme witch, called her altar “a lab for reality hacking.” For her, a sigil was not symbol but force, a charged glyph making a different future briefly accessible.

To study such magic is to risk enchantment. It demands writing that bends genre, ruptures linearity, invites readers into the circle. Queer magic is spectacular, glitter and smoke, wigs and feathers, runes drawn in eyeliner. Camp becomes consecration; glamour becomes ontology. In a Beltane rite, a drag priestess invoked Venus in sequins while a butch drummer called down Mars. Laughter turned to tears. The erotic and divine became indistinguishable. This was not parody, it was liturgy.

In a disenchanted world, magic becomes refusal: a protest against reduction. Queer enchantment is insurgency. By casting spells, invoking fluid deities, and crafting ecstatic aesthetics, queer practitioners assert a different relationality, one not bound by productivity or coherence. Ritual becomes protest. Glamour becomes strategy. Magic becomes not what is unreal, but what remains unruled.

Magic, then, is central to queer world-making, not a marginal ornament to religion but a challenge to its foundations. It queers knowledge itself, insisting that understanding can emerge from ritual, drag, and desire. If ritual is where queers navigate sacred space, magic is where queerness remakes the sacred altogether. The circle does not close here. It expands. The altar multiplies. The spell continues. This is not a conclusion, it is a glamour. A shimmer of the sacred yet to come.

**Conclusion**

This essay began as an ethnography of ritual and identity and, in its unfolding, became something larger: a sustained meditation on the sacredness of queer world-making and the methodological implications of taking enchantment seriously. Through sustained fieldwork in Montreal’s neo-Pagan communities, deep engagement with feminist, decolonial, and queer theories, and a commitment to ritual not just as object but as epistemology, we have followed how LGBTQ+ practitioners do more than adapt spiritual frameworks, they reshape the very grammar of the sacred.

What emerged is not a story of easy inclusion. Nor is it a tale of radical rupture. It is a cartography of friction. Neo-Pagan spaces, particularly those structured by Wicca and Reclaiming Witchcraft, offer potent tools for gender affirmation, symbolic play, and cosmological healing. But they also reproduce the very binaries and hierarchies they seek to overcome. In that contradiction lies the ritual labor of queering: not purification, but improvisation. Not transcendence, but transformation.

This essay thus contributes to three scholarly conversations: Pagan studies, the sociology of religion, and queer studies. In each case, it does so not by merely adding queer subjects to existing paradigms, but by rethinking what counts as knowledge, what constitutes ritual, and how power animates spiritual life.

Within Pagan studies, this essay insists on the centrality of queer voices, not as anecdotal or exceptional, but as constitutive. Much of the existing literature on Paganism has focused on its revivalist, feminist, ecological, or esoteric dimensions. While crucial, these analyses have often sidelined questions of gender fluidity, sexual politics, and racialization. This work expands the field by demonstrating how queer practitioners are not simply participants in neo-Paganism, but its theorists, critics, and remakers.

By attending to moments of misrecognition, ritual refusal, and symbolic reimagination, this ethnography complicates overly celebratory narratives of inclusivity in Pagan traditions. It asks difficult questions about sacred legitimacy, theological authority, and the inheritance of binary metaphors. It foregrounds practitioners who don’t just cast spells, they queer them, revise them, and sometimes reject the very cosmologies they were trained in.

The essay also offers a methodological intervention. It argues for a Pagan studies that is not only descriptive but transformative, one that treats ritual as theory, and enchantment as knowledge. To sociology of religion, this work offers both data and critique. It documents a contemporary spiritual movement that exists outside institutional frames, doctrinal orthodoxy, and census categories.. It traces how queer practitioners assemble meaningful spiritual lives through improvisation, aesthetic labor, and embodied experimentation.

But more fundamentally, it challenges the epistemological boundaries of the discipline. Classical sociology, from Durkheim to Weber, has long drawn a line between religion and magic, belief and performance, legitimacy and deviance. These distinctions were never neutral. They were structured by colonialism, patriarchy, and rationalist bias.

By centering magic as a queer epistemology, this work destabilizes the very categories through which religion has been sociologically known. It demands that we take seriously the knowledge produced in ritual, not only as affect, but as analytics.. It insists that religion is not just institutional, it is intimate, fragmented, affective, and sometimes flamboyant. It invites sociologists to listen differently: not only to beliefs, but to gestures, glitches, and glitter.

In the context of queer studies, this essay insists that the spiritual is not an afterthought. Too often, queer theory has foregrounded critique of normativity, embodiment, and futurity while leaving religion and spirituality to the sidelines, or treating them with suspicion. This essay offers an alternative: a queer studies that is not afraid of the altar, the chant, the trance. It treats ritual not as superstition, but as performance theory in motion. It explores how queer and trans practitioners use magic not as metaphor but as method: to rework kinship, to heal trauma, to mark transition, to grieve the ungrievable. It contributes to queer thought by expanding its terrain, from the bedroom and the archive to the sacred circle. It does not abandon critique, but it pairs it with curiosity. What if queer world-making is not just about disruption, but also about invocation? What if the future is not only imagined, but summoned?

Throughout this essay, we have followed rituals in their most intimate and most theatrical moments. We have witnessed sacred scripts rewritten, roles reimagined, and symbols queered. We have seen how drag can be a liturgy, how misgendering can fracture a rite, and how sacred space can be a site of both healing and harm. The work of queer Paganism, as this essay shows, is never just expressive. It is cosmological. It is ontological. It is political.

For scholars, it is also methodological. We must now ask: How do we research what resists language? How do we write rituals that weren’t made to be transcribed? How do we stay with the trouble, not to resolve it, but to learn from it?

Queer Pagan ritual is not a performance of resistance. It is resistance as performance. It does not aim to be coherent, it aims to be real. In the cracked voice of an invocation, in the trembling hand holding a candle, in the laughter after the glitch, something sacred happens. Something that deserves to be studied, not as anomaly, but as revelation.

This is not a conclusion. It is an opening. The circle, as many rituals proclaim, is cast beyond time and space. It does not close, it transforms. The altar is not cleared, it is multiplied. The spell is not broken, it is shared. So let us return, again and again, to the sites of queer enchantment. Let us study them not only with theory, but with breath. Let us let the ritual mark us, unsettle us, inspire us. The queer sacred is not an object. It is a relation. Let the relation remain. Let the circle remain open. Let the queer sacred speak.
