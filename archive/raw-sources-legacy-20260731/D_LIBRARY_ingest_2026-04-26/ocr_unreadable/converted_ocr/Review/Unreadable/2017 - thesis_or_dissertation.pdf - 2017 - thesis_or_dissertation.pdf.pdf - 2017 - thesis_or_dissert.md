Proposed Project Title:

Gender, Identity and Material Culture: Negotiations around "stuff"
7.

Proposed Project Abstract (briefly summarize in one paragraph, 250 words maximum):

In our digital era, material culture keeps mutating faster and faster. Everyday "stuff" can now hold great value to
certain people, whereas other items that used to be sacred or most-prized are often represented in new and less
typical ways. In fact, material culture, understood as a series of social constructs, points directly to power
relationships between those who define the objects and those who use them. But what does it show about how we
engage with material things such as books, tattoos, clothing, nature, cooking tools, ritual tools, building tools, etc.?
What does it show in regards to social norms or what technology and social media allow us to do? This project
aims to investigate the relationship between material culture and the transmission of religious and embodied
identities. It will explore in depth the ways in which collective and individual practices share a common set of
ritual tools and performances that help negotiate with normative tradition, modify it or carry it on. These tools, as
physical and aesthetic artefacts, permeate every aspect of culture and come into play with how identities are
created and negotiated. This project will focus on how "stuff" circulates or is taken into account in the
construction of people’s identity, namely the Radical Faeries, and their community. It is particularly interested in
looking at the commodification of objects, and at how they help individuals take part in or challenge tradition,
whether religious, sexual or cultural.
8.

Briefly, how does the proposed project relate to your doctoral dissertation?

My dissertation is a study of identity negotiations in the Montreal Pagan community, regarding gender and sexual
orientation. Much of the research on the topic suggests that contemporary Paganism offers a very inclusive milieu
where LGBTQ Pagans can express the specificity of their identities through collective and individual practices
and performances whose gendered meanings and relations can be modified at will. But I demonstrate, contrary to
popular belief, that these religiosities still reproduce normativity, and that identity is better expressed and assumed
when the individual is detached, at least for a time, from the community. More specifically, this project focuses
on masculinity among the Radical Faeries, a narrower aspect of my previous research on contemporary Pagan and
LGBTQ communities, which pass on their material heritage in ways that are still very much unknown. It is also
rooted in the understanding that religion and religious experience are part of everyday life, whether aligned with
or alternative to prescribed traditionnal practices and worldviews.

9.

Describe your proposed study in detail (no more than 1000 words):

Objects are used to assert identity: they allow people to gain power over their lives, to go against several norms,
whether related to gender, religion, or social status (Gilchrist, 1994; Miller, 1998; Woodward, 2007; Hicks and
Beaudry, 2010). But they also are tied to a wider socio-cultural, political and economic background which
prescribes the extent to which people's power can be claimed. Objects can also express multiple meanings, and
hold different values to different people in the same community or in society in general. By paying attention to
the ways certain objects or, more generally, the accumulation of certain types of objects, partake in communities'
codes and practices, this study will explore the interplay between the meaning given to "stuff" by individuals, the
way they use them and how they help them be part of their community. The goal is, in the first part of this project,
to review the present literature around men's social practices and to determine the degree to which the presence
and importance of certain objects participate in the diverse ways men identify as such in different contexts. It will
take a look at specific objects that hold multifaceted meanings, like jewelery, items of clothings, as well as the use
of certain cosmetic or grooming implements, household and/or ritual tools.The second part of the study will seek
to delve deeper into gay and queer men's religious experience by questioning how they actually adhere to the use
of these objects as vectors of alternative masculinities.
As such, it will explore the various practices through which men who belong to the Radical Faeries identify as
masculine. Since the 1970's, the Radical Faeries, a political, environmental, and spiritual movement of gay and
queer men, have challenged hegemonic masculinity. As such, this project will focus on the ways in which
religious practices converge with material culture and masculinity within a specific cultural context. This research

interrogates how Radical Faeries, whose identification to masculine norms can be problematized in different ways,
negotiate their identities in regards to binary gender norms as they are performed in religious context. It compares
how diverse masculinities come into play, within identification processes, with conceptions of virility (Connell,
2005; Jeffrey and D’Allondans, 2008). This project is based on the qualitative analysis of life narratives, showing
how gender norms influence life course. It expands the lens of queer theory (Butler, 2006; De Lauretis, 2007;
Dorlin, 2008) towards an intersectional approach comprising gender, religion, race, class, sexual orientation, age,
physical capacity, etc. Moreover, it looks for ways in which lived religion and religious education, understood as
loci of oppression and/or emancipation from gender norms (McGuire, 2008; Fedele and Knibbe, 2013; Lepage,
2013, 2015), partake in the transmission or the negotiation of virility as fundamental to the definition of
masculinity (Clare, 2004; Jeffrey and Lachance, 2012).
This research will consider socialization processes to men's material culture in a dual perspective related to
theories in religious studies (Ménard, 2006). The first angle is associated with virility rites (Jeffrey and Lachance,
2012) that, through repetition, determine particular forms of masculine experience of body appropriation (Wulf,
2003; Schechner, 2006). The second complementary angle is based on systems of sacralized symbols, allowing a
comprehension of a network of meanings that put into coherence the experiences of specific groups (Lévi-Strauss,
1958; McGuire, 2008). In addition to what religious studies can bring, this research is supported by an
intersectional theoretical framework (Crenshaw, 2005). Questioning essentialist social categories like sex, sexual
orientation, race or age, intersectionality is interested in the crossing of social positionings and in the interaction
of forms of discrimination and subordination engendered by these intersections (Brotman and Lévy, 2008; Dorlin,
2009; McCall, 2005; Mc Andrew, 2010). This theoretical approach is most pertinent to highlight the specific
experience of LGBTQ men. It allows for a better understanding of virility norms and their impact on the
construction and performance of men’s gender identity. It also recognizes nonetheless a diversity of experiences
within observed groups, due to the multiple social positionings of individuals (religion, age group, social class,
etc.) as much as individual trajectories themselves (McCall, 2005).
This research project is two-fold: 1/ To clarify the meaning of such concepts in regards to the Radical Faeries, 2/
To gather data through interviews. The first axis will, first, deepen the many definitions of virility and show why
it is associated with sacredness, with prohibitions and mythic knowledge. Secondly, it will explore the multiple
behaviors of virility as documented in scholarly literature. Thirdly, it will focus on diverse socialization,
identification, integration and recognition processes of virility behaviors via material culture within masculine
experience. The second data-gathering axis of this research uses qualitative methodologies adapted to the analyses
of social processes and phenomena, as well as the meaning given by individuals to their actions (Paillé and
Mucchielli, 2012). It aims to recruit 20 gay, bisexual, transgender and queer men of different age groups. It will
also see to diversify cultural origins in order to gain a better understanding of the complex power relations coming
into play in the transmission and reproduction of masculine identities in society (Connell, 2005).
Collection of data will be conducted through individual semi-directive interviews. This research will consider the
participants’ life trajectories and the life narrative method (Bertaux, 2010). The interview guide includes three
themes. It will first interrogate the contemporary transmission processes and the ways in which various norms are
reproduced throughout the course of one’s life (sacredness of virility, virility rites, prohibitions around virility,
beliefs and myths about virility). It will then focus on the integration of these norms within masculine identity and
their (emotional, social and sexual) experience in regards to those norms (practices and rites). Data analysis will
then specifically turn to variations in behaviors and attitudes towards material culture throughout the course of
their life in regards to virility norms (discrepancies and continuities). It will borrow Rémy and Ruquoy’s (1990)
four-step method, bringing to light recurring themes, comparing significations, forming categories and obtaining
more objective meanings (Paillé, 2006).

10. Course Description. In no more than two short paragraphs, write a course description for an undergraduate seminar

relevant to our 2018-19 topic, “STUFF.” Depending on departmental curricular needs, your course might run either as a
freshman seminar or as a seminar for majors in the discipline. In either case, your description should appeal to students with
no particular background in the material, so please take care not to propose an unsuitably advanced course. For examples of
freshman seminar descriptions, see The College at Penn website.

The proposed course will look at the relationship between the study of material culture and matters of identity.
With a primary focus on gender, it will examine how regular everyday "stuff" is invested with great normativity,
and at the same time holds the potential for performing profound social and cultural transformation. But why do
we give meaning and value to objects? What kind of heritage can they hold? How does gender come into play
with the way we relate to and use objects? How do they affect us, and help enact social status or the relationship
between identity and community? This course will develop answers to these questions in regards to different parts
of Western culture : women in general, New Age culture and contemporary Paganism, gay culture, clubbing and
dance culture, fashion, social media, television and cinema, comic books, manga and anime, punk and goth
cultures, among others. It will do so by showing how material culture studies can lead to a better understanding of
culture and its study in the humanities; then by going deeper into the power relationships determining the roles
and functions of objects in everyday life and in society in general; finally by showing how objects, as everchanging living things, impact the very foundation of normativity around ethics, politics, and the construction of
knowledge and science today. The course will end on a brief reflection about the study of material culture, and the
epistemologies surrounding it. Bridging the too-often neglected gap between them, and capitalizing on their
diversity, it will advance the understanding of how "stuff" not only speaks about the people who made them, and
the different intent and power relations behind their making, but also about its purpose today, and how it is
transformed and/or preserved by those who value them.
