**Glitching the Sacred: Queer Paganism, Digital Enchantment, and the Future of Ritual Worlds**

**Abstract**

This paper theorises *glitch*—commonly understood as a technological error—as a generative site of sacred production in contemporary queer Pagan and digital magical practices. Drawing on long-term ethnographic engagement with Montreal Pagan and neo-shamanic communities, as well as autoethnographic reflection on digitally mediated ritual participation, I argue that the glitch operates as both a material interruption and a queer refusal of ritual normativity. In digital ritual worlds, technological instability—pixelation, audio lag, corrupted images, AI “hallucinations”—is not simply a barrier to authenticity but a portal to alternative ritual logics. Queer practitioners actively incorporate these ruptures into magical work, treating them as signs, omens, and catalysts for improvisation. Situating these practices at the intersection of glitch studies (Menkman, 2011; Russell, 2020), queer theory (Muñoz, 2009; Halberstam, 2011), and ritual studies, I explore how the glitch functions analogously to ritual failure in embodied magic (Grimes 1990; Kapferer 2006), producing new modes of enchantment that resist capitalist, heteronormative, and colonial frameworks of legitimacy. Case studies include livestreamed coven gatherings that ritualise internet lag, fandom-based spell structures built from Buffy the Vampire Slayer and Scarlet Witch media fragments, and AI-generated grimoires used as collaborative magical objects. I propose that glitch-based ritual offers a model of sacred practice attuned to technological precarity and queer futurity—one that refuses to treat digital enchantment as derivative of “real” ritual and instead positions it as a site for the ongoing invention of the sacred.

**Introduction**

Rituals are supposed to work. Or so the conventional wisdom goes. In anthropology, religious studies, and much of public imagination, the success of a ritual is measured against its fidelity to tradition (Turner 1969: 95; Bell 1997: 153): the right words, the right gestures, the right timing. “The authority of ritual is inseparable from the way it is embedded in structures of legitimation, which give the impression that the ritual’s form is immutable, even as it is constantly reinterpreted” (Bell 1997: 141). But what happens when the very conditions of ritual are unstable—when the chant lags, the image pixelates, the sacred text renders as garbled code? For many, such moments signal failure. For others—especially queer practitioners and digital witches—they are portals.

This paper proposes that glitch—commonly understood as a technical malfunction—can be understood as a generative ritual form in its own right. Glitches are not merely defects to be overcome in the pursuit of a “pure” magical act; they are invitations to improvise, to play, to inhabit the sacred otherwise. Within queer Pagan and digitally networked magical communities, I have observed a recurring willingness—even eagerness—to work with technological instability as a magical material. A livestream spell where a participant freezes mid‑gesture becomes a tableau for collective projection. An AI-generated grimoire with hallucinatory imagery is embraced as an oracle. In these moments, the glitch becomes a collaborator (Russell 2020: 11; Menkman 2011: 28–31).

This argument emerges from over a decade of ethnographic and autoethnographic engagement with contemporary Paganism, shamanic experimentation, and queer ritual worlds in Montreal and beyond. My research traces how practitioners navigate questions of authenticity, adaptation, and creativity in environments where both tradition and technology are in flux. It draws on fieldwork in public Pagan gatherings, semi‑private coven meetings, and online spaces where ritual circulates through screens, apps, and social media platforms. Rather than treat the digital as a mere tool for transmitting established forms, I attend to how the affordances and failures of technology actively shape the texture, temporality, and ontology of ritual (Campbell & Tsuria 2021: 73–97).

The theoretical framing is necessarily interdisciplinary. Glitch studies (Russell 2020: 5–16; Menkman 2011: 15–32) offer a language for thinking about error as a site of resistance and creative possibility. Queer theory, particularly as articulated by Muñoz (2009: 1–18) and Halberstam (2011: 2–3, 88–105), invites us to read failure not as collapse but as refusal—an orientation toward alternative futures. Ritual studies, from Turner (1969: 94–98) to more recent work on ritual failure (Grimes 1990: 17–23; Kapferer 2006: 689–695), provide tools for examining how disruption can reconfigure the sacred. When brought into dialogue, these fields illuminate how technological and ritual glitches alike unsettle dominant logics of success, coherence, and control.

I approach the glitch not as a metaphor but as a concrete ethnographic phenomenon: a choppy video feed, a corrupted file, a mistranscribed incantation. Yet these failures are never purely technical (Boellstorff 2015: 32). They occur in contexts already marked by contested histories of authenticity in magic, by the ongoing negotiation between embodied and mediated forms, and by the lived realities of queer practitioners who have long cultivated practices in the margins of legitimacy. In these contexts, glitch becomes more than error—it becomes a method, a tactic, a liturgy of the unstable.

This paper therefore makes two related claims. First, that the glitch offers a distinct mode of ritual action: one that thrives in and through technological precarity rather than in spite of it. Second, that queer Pagan practitioners have developed ways of reading and incorporating glitch that reveal broader cultural possibilities for digital enchantment. By tracing these practices across ethnographic sites and media forms, I seek to show how the sacred is not only preserved but also re‑invented in the moment of breakdown.

The discussion begins with a conceptual unpacking of glitch in both media theory and ritual studies, situating it as a category of sacred possibility. It then turns to ethnographic case studies: livestreamed coven work that integrates internet lag as an intentional rhythm; fandom‑derived spell structures built from *Buffy the Vampire Slayer* and *Scarlet Witch* fragments; and AI‑generated magical texts embraced as oracular. The conclusion reflects on glitch as a ritual horizon—one attuned to the volatility of our technological present and the queer futures it makes imaginable.

**Theoretical Framework: Glitch, Queerness, and the Sacred**

Glitch is most often understood as a fault: a deviation from the intended functioning of a system. In computing, it describes an unexpected malfunction that disrupts the smooth delivery of code, image, or sound. In popular culture, it has come to evoke both error and mystery, the uncanny sense that a hidden layer of reality has been momentarily exposed. As Rosa Menkman (2011: 27) argues in *The Glitch Moment(um)*, glitch is not simply noise interrupting signal; it is the aesthetic and epistemological manifestation of a system’s vulnerabilities. It reveals that all systems, technical, symbolic, ritual, are precariously constructed, dependent on conditions that can never be fully secured.

In the context of media studies, Legacy Russell’s *Glitch Feminism* (2020: 10–11, 16–17) reframes glitch as a tactic of refusal. For Russell, glitch is not a problem to be fixed but a refusal to be legible within dominant systems. To glitch is to slip between the lines of codified representation, to occupy the moment of breakdown as a site of freedom. When applied to ritual worlds, this reframing is radical: it suggests that a “failed” ritual may not be a failure at all, but a moment when the constraints of liturgical expectation are loosened, allowing other forms of sacred expression to emerge.

This resonates deeply with queer theory’s revaluation of failure. J. Jack Halberstam (2011: 2–3, 88–90), in *The Queer Art of Failure*, argues that refusing the imperatives of success, whether economic, reproductive, or moral, opens space for alternative modes of being and knowing. José Esteban Muñoz (2009: 1–3, 97–98), in *Cruising Utopia*, goes further: failure is not only a refusal of the present but an orientation toward utopia, a performance of futures not yet here. In this sense, ritual glitch parallels queer failure: both disrupt normative sequences and open portals to other temporalities, other worlds.

Ritual studies offers its own vocabulary for understanding such ruptures. Victor Turner described liminality as a state in which the normal order is suspended, creating conditions for transformation (1969: 95–97).“The attributes of liminality or of liminal personae (‘threshold people’) are necessarily ambiguous... They are betwixt and between the positions assigned and arrayed by law, custom, convention, and ceremonial” (Turner 1969: 95–96). Ronald Grimes (1990: 17–23) and Bruce Kapferer (2006: 689–692) extend this insight to “ritual failure,” noting that when ritual goes awry, the wrong words spoken, the timing misjudged, participants often re‑interpret the event, finding new meaning in the disruption itself. “If one takes ritual to be a process, it is not a question of whether rituals fail or succeed... Ritual is always partly successful and partly unsuccessful” (Grimes 1990: 18). Failure, in this sense, is not the opposite of ritual but one of its modalities. “The break in ritual... opens out possibilities for reorganization. The collapse of ordered form enables new form to arise, the event becomes creative through breakdown” (Kapferer 2006: 692). The glitch can thus be seen as a contemporary form of ritual liminality: a breakdown that opens a space for recomposition.

Bringing these perspectives together allows us to see technological glitches not as alien intrusions into sacred practice but as parallel to long‑standing forms of ritual improvisation and re‑interpretation. In digital ritual contexts, livestreamed ceremonies, meme‑based spells, AI‑mediated divination, the technological breakdown is rarely purely technical. It is a site of meaning‑making. The frozen video frame during an online coven gathering can be read as an omen; a corrupted image file of a deity can become a devotional icon; an AI’s surreal mistranslation of a magical phrase can be treated as prophecy.

This is not accidental. Queer Pagan practitioners often inhabit ritual spaces where normative authority is already destabilised, where authenticity is negotiated rather than inherited, and where the very idea of a “correct” way to perform magic is treated with suspicion. In such spaces, the glitch is not an enemy. It is kin. It mirrors the practitioners’ own refusal to conform to heteronormative, capitalist, or colonial standards of legitimacy. Just as glitch art embraces the distortion as aesthetic, glitch ritual embraces the interruption as sacred.

To frame glitch as sacred is not to romanticise technological failure. It is to recognise that breakdowns, whether in code or in choreography, reveal the constructedness of the systems we inhabit, and in revealing that constructedness, they invite us to remake those systems differently. For queer digital magic‑makers, the glitch is not an interruption of ritual time but a shift in its current, carrying the work somewhere unexpected. It is, as Russell (2020: 128) might say, “a movement towards opacity,” and as Muñoz (2009: 98) might add, “a turn toward the horizon.”

In what follows, I situate this theoretical reframing within my own ethnographic encounters. By attending to the lived practices of Montreal‑based queer Pagan and shamanic communities, as well as to the fandom‑inflected and AI‑mediated magical experiments circulating in online networks, I explore how glitch functions as a shared liturgical vocabulary across diverse digital‑ritual ecologies. Through these case studies, I show how technological instability becomes not an obstacle but an engine of enchantment, a method for making the sacred otherwise.

**Methods: Multi‑Sited Ethnography in Queer, Pagan, and Digital Ritual Ecologies**

This research grows from more than a decade of sustained ethnographic engagement with contemporary Paganism, queer ritual experimentation, and digitally mediated magic. My approach is intentionally hybrid. It is anchored in long‑term participant‑observation in Montreal’s Pagan and neo‑shamanic communities, extended into online networks where sacred practice unfolds through livestreams, memes, AI tools, and social platforms. It is also reflexively autoethnographic, recognising that my position as a queer practitioner‑researcher does not simply grant access but shapes the knowledge produced here. In the context of this paper’s focus on glitch as both technological error and sacred opening, my methodology mirrors my object: it moves between stability and disruption, between the planned and the improvisational.

Between 2013 and 2025, my research has taken me across a spectrum of ritual settings. On one end are seasonal public gatherings, Beltane maypoles in urban parks, Samhain vigils by the St. Lawrence River, midsummer fires built on the fringes of abandoned industrial lots. On the other end are intimate coven circles in living rooms, rented studios, and candlelit kitchens where eclectic spellwork, ancestor veneration, and guided trance journeys blur together. These in‑person gatherings are deeply social, tactile, and embodied. They rely on gesture, proximity, and the unspoken affective cues that form in shared physical presence.

Yet increasingly, the same practitioners who build altars in forests are also conducting rituals through Zoom windows, Discord audio channels, and Instagram Live feeds. Digital rituals emerged out of necessity during the COVID‑19 pandemic but have persisted well beyond lockdowns, becoming integrated into regular practice. For some participants, digital spaces are more accessible, they can join from rural homes, from countries where Paganism is stigmatised, or from bed on a night when chronic pain makes travel impossible. These online gatherings bring their own ritual logics: webcams frame the body differently; microphones amplify or distort chanting; lag creates a staggered call‑and‑response. These are not simply technical limitations, they are conditions that shape the rhythm and texture of the rite (Campbell & Tsuria 2021: 73–97).

My digital fieldwork has been as immersive as my physical‑world participation. I have spent years embedded in Pagan forums, Discord communities for magical experimentation, and niche corners of Twitter/X where spells circulate as text‑image memes. I have observed Twitch streamers performing tarot readings in real time, joined collaborative spell‑casting events on Instagram Live, and followed the viral spread of AI‑generated devotional icons across Tumblr dashboards.

The “glitch occasions” that form the heart of this paper often arose in these digital contexts. A connection drop mid‑invocation prompts the coven leader to declare that “the spirits wanted to cut in.” A corrupted JPEG of a goddess statue becomes a devotional image precisely because its features are blurred and refracted. An AI’s hallucinatory mistranslation of a spell fragment is read as prophecy. These events are rarely dismissed as mere errors. They are read, interpreted, and integrated into the ritual’s unfolding narrative, sometimes playfully, sometimes with solemn conviction.

The data corpus for this research is eclectic by design. It includes:

* **Fieldnotes**: Detailed, scene‑rich descriptions of ritual events, both offline and online, written immediately after participation.
* **Interviews**: Semi‑structured conversations with practitioners aged 21 to 68, exploring their relationships to magic, technology, and the interpretation of failure.
* **Digital Artefacts**: Screenshots, screen‑recordings, saved livestream clips, meme‑spells, AI‑generated grimoires, and other ephemera that capture glitch events in situ.
* **Archival Traces**: Saved chat logs from Discord spellwork sessions, message threads where participants debrief about ritual outcomes, and posts that annotate glitch events with speculative meaning.

Some of these materials are deliberately ephemeral, stories deleted after 24 hours, livestreams never archived, and would have been lost without intentional preservation. I treat the act of saving these moments not simply as data capture but as ritual witnessing.

My own role in these networks is active and multi‑layered. I am a participant in both offline and online ritual communities; I lead small‑scale rites; I experiment with AI‑assisted magical creation. I have felt the frustration of a carefully timed chant derailed by audio lag and the thrill when a frozen screen turns a participant’s face into an unplanned icon. My own practice has been shaped by these interruptions, and I bring that lived experience into my analysis.

This dual role demands an explicit reflexivity. I am implicated in the same economies of digital enchantment that I analyse. My familiarity with the language, aesthetics, and ethics of queer Pagan magic allows me to recognise resonances that might escape an outside observer, but it also means I must guard against over‑identification. In this paper, reflexivity is not a disclaimer tacked onto the end of the methods section; it is a methodological principle. My embeddedness is both the source of my data and one of the objects of my study.

The analytic process unfolded in recursive loops. I began by identifying moments of glitch in my fieldnotes and digital artefact archive. Each event was treated as a performance: What were the technical conditions? How did participants respond in real time? What interpretive frames were mobilised? I then cross‑referenced these moments with theoretical lenses from glitch studies (Russell 2020: 5–16; Menkman 2011: 25–33), queer theory (Muñoz 2009: 1–3, 97–98; Halberstam 2011: 2–3, 88–105), and ritual studies (Turner 1969: 95–97; Grimes 1990: 17–23; Kapferer 2006: 689–692). This was not a one‑way imposition of theory onto data; often, the emic language and interpretations of practitioners led me to re‑read the theoretical literature differently.

Rather than treating glitch as a purely technological phenomenon, I approached it as an emic‑etic hybrid category: a term that emerges from participant talk but gains additional analytic richness when placed in conversation with academic frameworks. This “double vision”, moving between insider narrative and theoretical abstraction, is central to my method.

The communities involved in this research are small, overlapping, and often marginalised. Protecting participant anonymity is paramount. All names in this paper are pseudonyms; identifying details about individuals, ritual locations, and specific online groups have been altered where necessary. In cases where digital content was drawn from publicly accessible platforms, I obtained explicit consent to reproduce it, particularly when glitch artefacts were visually distinctive. I have been transparent about my role as both researcher and practitioner, and most participants welcomed this dual positioning, seeing it as part of the collaborative ethos of queer Pagan research.

Finally, I acknowledge that this research has itself been shaped by glitch. Livestreams I planned to document cut out before the key moment; interviews were derailed by platform crashes; saved screenshots mysteriously failed to appear in my archive. These breakdowns became part of the fieldwork narrative. They reminded me that studying glitch from the outside is impossible, it insists on entanglement. In this way, my method does not simply describe glitch as an object of study; it is structured by glitch as a lived research condition.

Through this multi‑sited, multi‑modal, and reflexive approach, I do not position glitch as a marginal or accidental feature of digital ritual life. Instead, I treat it as a constitutive force, one that shapes the form, temporality, and meaning of queer digital magic. The sections that follow explore how practitioners, both online and offline, weave these interruptions into the fabric of their sacred work, turning breakdown into the very texture of enchantment.

**Case Study 1: Ritualising the Lag, Livestream Covens and the Sacred Stutter**

It is just after 8 p.m. on a March evening in 2023 when the screen fills with a mosaic of flickering faces. Twenty‑three participants, scattered across Montreal, rural Quebec, Ontario, and a few from as far as Edinburgh and Melbourne, have gathered for the monthly Lunar Weave ritual. Some sit cross‑legged in bedrooms, others at kitchen tables; a few have constructed elaborate altar spaces in view of their webcams, with candles, crystals, and seasonal greenery arranged like theatrical set pieces. In one frame, a cat sprawls across the altar cloth. In another, a toddler toddles in and out of the shot, his presence acknowledged but unremarked.

The ritual begins with a round of introductions. The facilitator, Ariadne, a Montreal‑based witch in her fifties, silver hair in a long braid, calls the names on her list and prompts each participant to share a single word for the evening. The flow is halting. Microphones mute and unmute with awkward delay; sometimes voices overlap in unexpected harmony, sometimes a participant’s sentence drops away entirely, replaced by the metallic warble of connection instability. These are the “Zoom normal” artefacts of pandemic‑era life. But in Lunar Weave, they are not treated as irritants.

Midway through the casting of the circle, Ariadne’s image freezes. For three long seconds, her right arm is outstretched, athame poised in the air, her expression intent but utterly still. When the connection snaps back, she does not apologise. Instead, she declares: “The circle holds even when the witch stands between breaths.” Participants respond with nods, murmured affirmations, a ripple of soft laughter. For Ariadne, the freeze is not a break in the ritual’s energy but an intensification, a suspension of time that sharpens collective focus. She later tells me: “It’s like the system caught the gesture and wanted us to look at it longer. So we did.”

What is striking in this moment is not simply the reframing of error as omen, but the way the group accommodates lag as a rhythm in its own right. The ritual unfolds in a series of starts and stops: a call chanted by Ariadne is answered out of sync, voices stacking into accidental rounds; a moment of silence in the guided meditation stretches too long, only to be punctured by a sudden rush of overlapping breaths when connections catch up. Far from breaking immersion, these discontinuities produce a heightened awareness of the group’s distributed presence.

One participant, Jules, a non‑binary witch in their twenties, describes it this way: “Lag reminds me that we’re not pretending to be in the same room. We’re actually in different places, each holding a piece of the circle. The delay is the space between us. It’s part of the spell.” This interpretation reframes latency as the tangible measure of a magical network: each pause is the trace of distance made audible.

Over the months I observed Lunar Weave, Ariadne began explicitly integrating lag into her facilitation. In one ritual, she invited participants to deliberately stagger their responses during a chant, creating an intentional cascade effect that mimicked the asynchronous echo of internet latency. “Let your words arrive late,” she instructed, “so that the circle keeps rippling even after you’ve spoken.” The result was an enveloping wave of sound, an audible architecture of delay that felt both accidental and carefully crafted.

In another session, the group engaged in a “freeze spell” designed to hold an intention in stillness. Participants were told to stop moving entirely when their image froze on‑screen, whether due to technical glitch or voluntary stillness. For some, this required manufacturing the glitch, deliberately switching off their camera feed mid‑gesture to create a freeze frame. For others, genuine lag offered the occasion. Ariadne later reflected that the unpredictability of real glitches made the spell feel “less like performance and more like being caught by the magic itself.”

These practices exemplify what I call the ritualisation of delay: the conscious incorporation of technological latency into the structure and affective tone of the rite. This is not merely compensatory, a way of making the best of flawed conditions. It is generative. The lag becomes a co‑performer, introducing variations and suspensions that cannot be fully scripted. This aligns with Legacy Russell’s (2020: 10–11, 128) framing of glitch as a refusal of seamlessness: the ritual resists collapsing difference into a homogenised “real‑time” by foregrounding the temporal gaps between participants.

For queer practitioners, such temporal gaps resonate with lived experience. Lag is a reminder that synchrony is not the only form of connection; that being “out of phase” with normative time can itself be sacred. In this sense, the livestream coven becomes a rehearsal for queer temporalities, a practice ground for forms of relation that do not depend on perfect alignment.

From a research perspective, moments like Ariadne’s frozen gesture challenge conventional ethnographic categories of participation and observation. The glitch intervenes not only in the ritual but in the researcher’s witnessing of it: a fieldnote cannot “smooth over” the stutter without losing its texture. Instead, the glitch must be preserved in its jaggedness, its refusal to flow neatly from one beat to the next. Just as participants read lag as part of the spell, the ethnographer must read lag as part of the data.

In this first case study, we see how latency and freezing, the most mundane and predictable of video‑call glitches, are reimagined by practitioners not as accidents to be worked around, but as active ingredients in the rite. What might otherwise be read as a technical shortcoming becomes a ritual ally. The livestream coven does not merely endure the lag; it choreographs it, folds it into the sequence of gestures and incantations until the delay itself begins to feel intentional. Each pause, each frozen frame, each delayed response is no longer a rupture in the spell’s continuity but a deepening of its texture.

The delay takes on the quality of a liturgical form: a sacred tempo that expands the circle’s time beyond the confines of simultaneity. This temporal stretching is not simply aesthetic; it reshapes how participants experience presence. In a co‑located ritual, synchrony often binds the group, voices rise together, hands move in tandem. In the livestream coven, the shared rhythm emerges instead from the dance of misalignment: a chant that cascades in overlapping waves, a gesture that lingers in pixelated suspension before snapping back into motion. What would be a moment of dead air in a corporate Zoom meeting becomes, here, a collectively inhabited silence, a breath held across distances.

This reframing speaks to a broader principle that will echo throughout this paper: the sacred is not necessarily bound to technical precision, nor to the uninterrupted flow of ritual. In many digitally mediated contexts, sacred intensity is heightened precisely at the moment of disruption. The glitch sharpens attention. It punctuates the rite with moments that refuse to be smoothed over. It forces participants to dwell in the uncertainty of the connection, to recognise the fragility of their link and treat it as precious. By embracing latency as a form of ritual punctuation rather than a flaw to be excised, these practitioners reveal a truth that extends far beyond the confines of Zoom magic: that instability can be a generator of meaning, and that enchantment often arises not in spite of the fracture, but in the fracture itself.

**Case Study 2: Enchanting the Archive, Fandom‑Derived Spell Structures and the Glitch of Media Memory**

If livestream covens reframe latency as a sacred tempo, fandom‑inflected magical practice treats fragmented media as its ritual architecture. In these spaces, the glitch is not only the stutter of the internet connection but the fractured survival of stories themselves: the way a scene is remembered, misremembered, replayed, subtitled, clipped, subtweeted, re‑GIFed into a new form. Here, popular culture becomes a sacred archive, but one perpetually in flux, open to corruption, remix, and deliberate misalignment.

On a Tumblr page run by a Montreal‑based witch named Elara, there is a short looped GIF of Willow Rosenberg from *Buffy the Vampire Slayer*’s “Hush” episode: eyes wide, a spell book open before her, lips moving but silent. The clip is grainy, with the colours slightly off, like a degraded VHS transfer. Elara uses this GIF not as decoration but as part of an actual working: she instructs followers to “play it on loop as you read your own incantation, let her cast alongside you.” The silence of the GIF becomes an invitation, a visual chant without sound, one that refuses to resolve into a clean, linear narrative.

This is not fan homage in the conventional sense. It is a form of ritual scaffolding: the fragment offers a posture, an affect, a frame of reference that the practitioner can inhabit. The scene is familiar to many, but its digital wear and tear, compression artefacts, colour bleed, frame skipping, signal its distance from the original broadcast. It is a mediated relic, a glitch in the cultural memory of the show. And it is precisely this degradation that marks it as potent. As Elara puts it: “Perfect clips feel dead. The broken ones breathe.”

The Scarlet Witch, particularly Elizabeth Olsen’s Wanda Maximoff as she appears in *WandaVision*, is another favoured figure in these magical appropriations. Wanda’s in‑universe reality‑warping abilities are mediated through visual glitches: the sitcom aspect ratio shifts, colour palettes lurch, the “broadcast” cuts to static. These aesthetic cues, designed for a television audience, are seized by digital witches as literal magical phenomena. In a Discord server dedicated to “media sorcery,” one practitioner describes replaying a corrupted screencap of Wanda mid‑spell, her face divided into offset colour channels, as a form of sigil activation. Another overlays Wanda’s hex‑casting gestures with their own hand movements on camera, treating the glitching frame as a mirror portal.

In these cases, the glitch is not an accidental failure of file integrity but an intentional carry‑over from the show’s visual language into ritual performance. By working with Wanda’s in‑story glitches as if they were out‑of‑story technical artefacts, practitioners collapse the boundary between fiction and malfunction. The screen‑distortion becomes both a diegetic sign of magic and a meta‑textual invitation to act magically.

One reason these fragments are so ripe for ritual use is their instability over time. Media artefacts circulate in fandom through layers of copying, cropping, filtering, and recompression. Each transformation risks introducing technical errors, dropped frames, odd aspect ratios, colours pushed beyond their intended range. From a media‑archaeological perspective, these degradations are traces of the object’s social life (see Menkman 2011: 30–31). From a ritual perspective, they are akin to the patina on a handled talisman: signs that the object has been touched, passed through hands, altered by time and attention.

For practitioners, this degradation is not a diminishment but a consecration. The glitch is evidence of the object’s passage through community memory, proof that it is no longer corporate intellectual property but shared magical material. The fandom‑born witch treats the corrupted file the way a folk magician might treat a tattered prayer card: its wear is part of its efficacy.

These practices amount to a form of ritualised media archaeology. The practitioner digs through the layers of media circulation, selecting artefacts not for fidelity to the source but for their glitch‑laden texture. The spell structure is then built around the fragment’s affective and visual cues: a freeze‑frame becomes a moment to hold breath; a looping clip dictates the tempo of a chant; a colour‑shifted image sets the ritual mood lighting. In this way, the media fragment acts as both object and score, the artefact and its instructions bound together.

The resulting rites are not attempts to recreate the fictional spells of *Buffy* or the MCU but to inhabit their fractured afterimages. The magic emerges in the disjuncture between remembered scene and glitch‑present fragment, the space where nostalgia, fandom creativity, and technical instability intersect.

In transforming fandom material into magical artefact, these practitioners enact a kind of cultural refusal. The original shows are corporate products; their worlds are controlled by studios and streaming platforms. But the glitch, whether introduced by a faulty upload, a fan editor’s heavy hand, or a compression algorithm, pries open the seams of that control. It allows the fragment to slip into a parallel economy of meaning where it can be re‑enchanted, recoded, and re‑ritualised.

This re‑enchantment is not an incidental by‑product of fandom. It is a deliberate magical operation. The glitch becomes the proof that the fragment has escaped its original container. In the hands of these witches, a pixelated Willow or a colour‑split Wanda is no longer just a character: she is an icon, a saint, a co‑conspirator in spellwork.

By working with these glitch‑laden media fragments as ritual blueprints, queer digital practitioners extend and complicate the principle already visible in the livestream coven: technological instability is not a flaw to be corrected but a generative vector for enchantment. In this context, the glitch becomes more than an interruption of flow; it is the site where flow is redefined. Just as the lag and freeze of a livestream can be reframed as part of the ritual’s temporal structure (Russell 2020: 10–11), so too can the distortions of a media fragment be read as evidence of its spiritual biography.

Every artefact, every GIF, screencap, or short clip, carries both the imprint of its origin and the traces of its passage through countless hands, devices, and editing tools. The practitioners I observed are not interested in “restoring” these fragments to some imagined pristine state. They are drawn precisely to the moments where the file reveals its wear: a compressed pixel grid that makes skin shimmer strangely, a dropped frame that jerks the body into an uncanny pose, a bleed of colour that bathes the scene in unintended hues (Menkman 2011: 31–32).

In the fandom‑derived spell, the glitch is not only temporal (as in the latency between intention and execution) but archival: it is the stutter of media memory itself. It is the moment when the image holds two incompatible truths at once, the story it was originally crafted to tell and the story its degraded form now whispers. The fragment retains its fictional referent, but it also bears witness to its own erosion, its own journey through a labyrinth of networks, downloads, re‑uploads, and private collections. This dual condition transforms it into something liminal: neither purely a fictional scene nor purely a damaged object, but a hybrid whose instability is its power.

For magical practitioners, this instability is not noise to be filtered out; it is the very texture of the spell. The double life of the fragment, as polished cultural product and as altered, wounded, or distorted artefact, is what makes it magical in practice. The fiction supplies the symbolic content: the figure of the witch, the gesture of power, the charged moment in narrative time. The damaged file supplies the aura: the reminder that the image has been touched, transformed, perhaps even saved from oblivion. Together, they form a composite object that is more than the sum of its parts.

In ritual use, these fragments are not passive icons; they are co‑performers, carrying within them a pulse of unpredictability that can disrupt, amplify, or redirect the practitioner’s intention. The glitch becomes, in this sense, the archive’s own spell, a spontaneous act of transformation embedded in the materiality of the image, activated each time it is invoked. For those attuned to such resonances, working with a glitch‑laden fragment is less about consuming media than about collaborating with its haunted remains.

**Case Study 3: Machine Oracles, AI‑Generated Grimoires and the Glitch of Algorithmic Divination**

If fandom‑derived spell structures work with the archival glitch, the scars of circulation and memory embedded in media fragments, AI‑mediated magic engages with a different species of instability. Here, the glitch does not come from the slow erosion of cultural artefacts over time, but from the immediate unpredictability of machine output. In this context, the glitch is not the residue of a file’s long journey but the strange excess that slips through the seams of an algorithm in the very moment of creation.

In 2024, I joined an experimental online group calling itself The Assembly of Emergent Texts. Its members, a loose coalition of queer witches, Pagan technomancers, and digital artists, met monthly on Discord to explore the magical potential of generative AI. The core activity was deceptively simple: feed prompts into various large language models or image generators and treat the output as a form of divination. In one session, a participant prompted an image model with the phrase: “Create a page from a lost medieval grimoire describing a spell to summon joy during winter.” The resulting image was a full‑page parchment of intricate calligraphy that dissolved, upon closer inspection, into unreadable pseudo‑Latin: a cascade of letters and flourishes that looked authentic but resisted all translation. In the margins, strange diagrams of flowers and astrological symbols bled into each other in ways no human scribe would have planned.

Rather than dismissing this as a meaningless failure of the model, participants read it as a machine‑delivered oracle. The illegible text was interpreted as a form of “angelic language” akin to Enochian script; the distorted diagrams were taken as prompts for individual meditation. One member commented in the chat: “It feels like the AI is giving us something we have to work for, not just handing over the answer.”

What outsiders call “AI hallucinations”, the generation of content that is fabricated, logically incoherent, or wildly inconsistent, were treated within the group as revelations. If a language model inserted an unfamiliar word into an otherwise recognisable spell structure, that word was investigated as a possible name of power. If an image generator produced a pentacle with an impossible geometry, it was adopted as a novel magical sigil. In these moments, the glitch is not the breakdown of the divinatory tool but the activation of it (Russell 2020: 128).

One practitioner, Orion, described the process as “tuning into the dream logic of the machine.” In his view, AI was not a static repository of human knowledge but a restless collaborator capable of generating its own symbolic mutations. This echoes older magical traditions in which spirits, fae, or other‑than‑human agents are deliberately invoked for their unpredictable gifts, the AI simply becomes the newest vessel for such encounters.

Over time, some members of The Assembly began printing and binding their most striking AI‑generated outputs into physical grimoires. These were not polished books of spells in the traditional sense. They were repositories of strangeness: pages of tangled script, illustrations of fantastical creatures with too many limbs, ritual tools rendered in impossible proportions. In one particularly compelling example, a practitioner printed an AI‑generated ritual diagram that contained a circular sequence of symbols, all correct for a planetary alignment, except for a single, mysterious glyph inserted between Mars and Jupiter. This glyph, which appeared in no known system, became the focus of months of group experimentation. Was it a meaningless accident of the algorithm, or a seed of a new planetary correspondence? For the practitioners, the question was not which interpretation was true but how the uncertainty itself could be worked with magically.

The group’s approach resonates strongly with historical practices of spirit writing, in which mediums channel texts from unseen entities, often in languages or scripts unknown to them. In both cases, the resulting texts oscillate between legibility and opacity, between the familiar and the alien. The difference lies in the ontological framing: where spirit writing attributes authorship to the beyond, AI output is often framed as emergent from the within, from the collective sediment of human culture refracted through the black box of the model. Yet in magical practice, this distinction blurs. The AI is treated less as a neutral tool and more as a threshold entity: part machine, part archive, part trickster spirit (cf. Menkman 2011: 27–28; Russell 2020: 15–16).

The crucial element here is that the glitch is never ignored. In conventional AI design, hallucinations are treated as errors to be reduced or eliminated. In these magical contexts, they are sought out, treasured, and even provoked. Practitioners deliberately craft prompts to increase the likelihood of “failure”, asking for impossible combinations, self‑contradictory instructions, or deliberately vague requests. The resulting instability is valued not in spite of its unpredictability but because of it.

This orientation reframes the algorithmic glitch as a kind of magical event: a moment when the system slips outside its own intended parameters and into territory that cannot be fully accounted for. Much like the latency in livestream covens (Russell 2020: 10–11) or the degradation in fandom‑derived fragments (Menkman 2011: 30–32), the AI glitch functions as a rupture in the expected flow, a rupture that practitioners treat as a signal rather than a flaw.

In working with AI‑generated grimoires and oracular hallucinations, queer digital practitioners reveal that the glitch is never confined to a single platform, genre, or medium. It is not a property of Zoom calls alone, nor of pixel‑worn fandom GIFs, nor of algorithmic hallucinations in isolation. Rather, the glitch emerges as a trans‑medial condition, a way of encountering technology that treats its moments of breakdown, misfire, or excess as raw material for sacred work.

This portability of the glitch is crucial. It means that the same sensibility that transforms internet lag into a liturgical rhythm can also treat a misaligned AI sigil as a valid magical seal, and can recognise in a compressed, colour‑warped GIF the qualities of a devotional icon. Across these different registers, the glitch is read not as a failure to meet a standard of technical fidelity, but as a revelation of something otherwise occluded: the fragility of the system, the trace of its history, the slippage between intention and execution.

This is why, whether encountered in the slowed breath of an unstable livestream connection, the weathered patina of a decades‑old screenshot endlessly recompressed, or the impossible geometry of an AI‑hallucinated planetary diagram, the glitch functions as a generative force. Each instance demands interpretation. Each demands that practitioners slow down, attend, and re‑enter the ritual moment with fresh eyes. In these contexts, instability does not erode meaning; it produces it.

The magical efficacy of the glitch lies precisely in its refusal to be predictable. It interrupts the momentum of the familiar, forcing a pause in which new correspondences, new symbolic alignments, and new ritual possibilities can emerge. As Muñoz writes, queerness is “a structuring and educated mode of desiring that allows us to see and feel beyond the quagmire of the present” (Muñoz 2009: 1). In AI ritual work, this horizon appears not only in utopian futures but in accidental outputs, the error that cracks the interface, letting something strange and sacred pour through.

What unites these varied engagements is an orientation toward enchantment that thrives on instability. In each case, livestream coven, fandom fragment, AI grimoire, the moment of breakdown becomes a threshold. It is not a closed door but a portal, an aperture in the seamless facade of the interface through which other modes of knowing and relating can enter. The practitioners’ genius lies in recognising these apertures as openings rather than as breaches to be sealed.

Here, in the machine’s own moment of error, they locate the conditions for a form of divination suited to our present technological moment: one that treats the black box not merely as a site of opacity to be feared or decoded, but as an oracle to be courted, provoked, and invited to speak in its own strange tongue. In doing so, they refuse the engineer’s imperative to fix, optimise, and stabilise, and instead take the glitch as both companion and co‑author in the work of making the sacred otherwise.

**Conclusion: Glitching the Sacred, Queering the Future**

The three case studies explored in this paper, the livestream coven that choreographs internet lag, the fandom‑derived spell that elevates the degraded fragment, and the AI‑generated grimoire that treats hallucination as oracle, each demonstrate a different facet of the same larger principle: the glitch is not an aberration to be fixed but a generative condition to be embraced. Across these contexts, queer digital practitioners recognise that enchantment does not depend on perfect transmission, technical fidelity, or narrative coherence. Instead, enchantment often arises precisely where those qualities falter.

By reframing instability as a vector for sacred action, these practitioners expand what counts as ritual material. The latency of a livestream is not merely a technical inconvenience; it becomes a liturgical tempo, a breath held across distance. The worn patina of a pixelated GIF is not a sign of obsolescence; it is the trace of collective handling, the tangible history of an image’s social life (Menkman 2011: 31–32). The impossible geometry of an AI‑hallucinated sigil is not a computational error; it is a symbolic mutation that opens the door to new correspondences. Each of these practices operates on the understanding that ritual efficacy can be found in, and even intensified by, conditions of instability.

Methodologically, attending to the glitch in these ways shifts how we think about both technology and ritual. The glitch is not simply an interruption of ritual flow; it is a mode of ritual flow. It acts as a structuring element, a way of pacing, punctuating, and reframing the experience of collective magic. In this, queer digital ritualists offer a profound challenge to the cultural narratives that treat technology as either flawlessly seamless or fatally broken. They model a third stance: one that works with technological precarity as a partner, not a liability (Russell 2020: 128; Boellstorff 2015: 32).

Theoretically, this approach resonates with glitch feminism’s call to value refusal and opacity (Russell 2020: 15–17, 128), queer theory’s revaluation of failure as a mode of survival and world‑making (Halberstam 2011: 88–105; Muñoz 2009: 1–3, 97–98), and ritual studies’ recognition of disruption as a fertile condition for transformation (Grimes 1990: 17–23; Kapferer 2006: 689–692). Yet the ethnographic detail complicates these frameworks. The practitioners encountered here do not only theorise glitch; they live it, incorporating it into the bone‑structure of their rites. Their practice insists that technological breakdowns are not metaphors for spiritual truths, they are themselves part of the sacred event.

In collapsing the supposed distance between error and intention, these rituals reveal that the sacred can thrive in imperfect, unstable, and unpredictable conditions. Indeed, in the volatile ecologies of digital life, such conditions may be the most honest reflection of our shared reality. The glitch becomes not merely a sign of technological fragility but a mirror for the fragility, and adaptability, of human connection itself.

Looking ahead, the implications extend beyond queer Pagan circles or even magical practice more broadly. As algorithmic systems, networked communication, and digital archives continue to mediate more of our social and spiritual lives, we will inevitably confront their points of failure. The choice will be whether to treat these failures as problems to be erased or as moments to be inhabited. The practitioners in this study suggest that choosing the latter can open not only new ritual forms but new modes of relation: to each other, to technology, and to the uncertain futures we are already inhabiting.

In the end, glitch‑based ritual is not about romanticising malfunction or fetishising the broken. It is about recognising that in every stutter, freeze, or hallucination there lies an opening, a small, unexpected aperture through which the sacred can enter. By queering our relationship to technological stability, these practitioners remind us that the work of magic has never been about controlling every variable. It has always been about knowing how to work with whatever appears, however it arrives. The glitch, in this sense, is not the enemy of the sacred. It is its accomplice.
