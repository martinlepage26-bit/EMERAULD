The Scythe Already in Motion: Buffy, Queer Ritual, and the Politics of Glitch

Author: Martin Lepage, PhD​

Overview / Prospectus
This book examines the post‑canonical life of Buffy the Vampire Slayer fragments in queer
and trans magical practice. These fragments — moments of tonal rupture, ethical refusal,
camp disruption, and narrative misalignment — have escaped their original television context
to become portable, operational ritual technologies. They anchor vigils, puncture grief scripts,
protect against sanctimony, and keep ritual spaces open to new possibilities.
Based on multi‑year ethnographic fieldwork in Montreal’s queer Pagan and magical
communities (2020–2024) and sustained textual/media analysis, the book develops a hybrid
theoretical frame combining media and fan studies, queer theory, and ritual studies. It argues
that these “glitches” form a living, circulating architecture of queer ritual life.
By treating popular media not merely as symbolic reference but as operative infrastructure,
the work shifts how scholars think about the relationship between fandom and spirituality. It
reframes “failure,” “refusal,” and “misalignment” not as breakdowns but as generative modes
of magical practice.

Chapter Summaries
Introduction — The Scythe Already in Motion (not counted as a chapter)
Introduces “glitch” as a concept for studying media fragments in ritual contexts and “ritual
openings” as moments when these fragments become executable. Explains the method of
recursive ethnography, which tracks fragments across time and space in their various ritual
appearances. Positions the author between embedded practitioner and ethnographer.

Chapter 1 — Willow Rosenberg: Recursion and Saturated Grief
Willow’s resurrection chant in Bargaining operates as both diegetic spell and ethnographic
object. In canon, the chant resists closure, reflecting grief’s refusal to follow a tidy arc. In the
field, it appears as looped and distorted audio in all‑night vigils, creating a recursive

environment where grief is sustained rather than resolved. This chapter frames recursion as a
queer magical tactic, aligning with theories of non‑linear time and affective saturation.

Chapter 2 — Anya Jenkins: Ethical Misalignment and Mourning Without Script
Anya’s monologue in The Body rejects ritualised politeness in grief, making visible the
inadequacy of scripted mourning. In queer grief circles, the lines surface without attribution,
legitimising raw, unscripted speech. Misalignment here becomes a form of care — a
deliberate rupture in ritual choreography that opens space for truth‑telling.

Chapter 3 — Faith Lehane: Outlaw Kinship and the Refusal of Domestication
Faith’s persistent liminality — refusing to be fully integrated into Buffy’s circle — models a
kind of outlaw kinship. In the field, Faith fragments guard against moralising tendencies
within magical communities, often invoked to preserve the group’s appetite for risk and
transgression. Outlaw kinship emerges as a protective survival strategy against respectability
politics.

Chapter 4 — Harmony Kendall: Camp Disruption and Glamour as Ward
Harmony’s superficiality and comedic incompetence are read here as camp disruption. In
ritual life, Harmony‑based humour and glamour puncture the creeping heaviness of
ceremonial seriousness, acting as a ward against burnout. Glamour becomes not vanity but a
tactical form of magical shielding.

Chapter 5 — Systems of Recursion: From Glitch to Ritual Infrastructure
Synthesises the previous character‑based chapters into a systemic account of fragment
circulation. Shows how fragments move through rooftops, kitchen altars, grief spaces, and
encrypted chats, gaining power with each repetition. Argues that recursion operates as ritual
infrastructure in queer magical practice.

Chapter 6 — Methodological Loops: Recursive Ethnography in Queer Ritual Worlds
Reflects on the author’s dual position as participant and ethnographer. Explores ethical
tensions in documenting living ritual systems while maintaining community trust. Frames

recursive ethnography as both object and method — a way of moving through the research
that mirrors the looping structures under study.

Chapter 7 — Queer Ritual Systems Beyond Buffy
Extends the analysis to other fandoms and magical traditions. Compares Buffy’s ritual
afterlife to other operationalised media fragments in queer ritual systems. Offers a
transferable framework for studying post‑canonical media in spiritual and magical contexts.

Chapter 8 — Glitch Futures and Ritual Openings
Speculates on the future of glitch‑based ritual. Explores the impact of emerging media
platforms, shifting fandom ecologies, and cross‑fandom ritual collaborations. Argues that
glitch remains a durable queer tactic capable of adapting to changing technological and
cultural landscapes.

Conclusion — The Spell Was Never Meant to Close
Draws together the themes of refusal, recursion, misalignment, and disruption as sustaining
logics of queer magical survival. Calls for the recognition of media fragments as legitimate,
portable ritual technologies. Keeps the loop open, resisting the finality of closure.

Audience & Market Positioning

●​ Practitioners and cultural commentators engaged with contemporary witchcraft,

●​ Cross‑disciplinary audiences in performance studies, gender studies, and digital

Comparative Titles
●​ Louisa Stein, Millennial Fandom (University of Iowa Press, 2015)​
Examines media affect and participatory culture but does not theorise media as

operative ritual infrastructure.​
●​ Jack Halberstam, The Queer Art of Failure (Duke University Press, 2011)​
Frames failure as queer strategy; this book builds on that but grounds it in the
operational life of ritual practice.​
●​ Chris Klassen (ed.), Playing with Religion in Digital Games (Indiana University
Press, 2014)​
Explores religion in popular media, but positions it primarily as symbolic, not as
embedded technical ritual practice.​
●​ Birgit Meyer, Sensational Movies (University of California Press, 2015)​
Shows how media can shape religious experience materially; this book extends that
thinking into queer magical life.​
●​ Alex Owen, The Place of Enchantment (University of Chicago Press, 2004)​
Historical account of occultism; does not engage with digital, post‑canonical, or
queer ritual systems.​

Author Bio
Martin Lepage, PhD, is a scholar of queer ritual life, contemporary Paganism, and media
cultures. Their research blends academic rigour with embedded participation, producing
richly textured accounts of lived ritual worlds.
Lepage’s work has appeared in Religiologiques, Studies in Religion/Sciences Religieuses, The
Pomegranate, Anthropologica, and multiple edited volumes. Their background as both
practitioner and ethnographer grants them unique access to the operational life of queer
magical practices, enabling a nuanced account of how media fragments function as ritual
infrastructure.
