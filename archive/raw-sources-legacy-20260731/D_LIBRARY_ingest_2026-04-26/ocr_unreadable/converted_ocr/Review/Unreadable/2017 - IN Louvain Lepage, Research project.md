MOVE-IN Louvain 2017, Martin Lepage
Research project
Masculinities and gender normativity: Narratives of Socialization of Virility
a) Objectives: This postdoctoral research project will allow us to better integrate academia in order to
develop the skills necessary to eventually obtain a position as a tenured professor. This research aims to
answer three goals: 1/ develop new knowledge on the relationship between masculinities and behaviors
linked to virility within gay, bisexual, transgender and queer men’s life trajectories, 2/ study how religious
practices can constitute loci of oppression and/or emancipation from gender norms, 3/ improve the
diffusion of knowledge through the publication of articles and the participation to diverse conferences.
In the public sphere, as well as in theoretical debates in social sciences, a number of issues surrounding
sexual diversity and LGBTQ identities continue to rise daily. Questions around gender are more and more
put into perspective, through the testimony of popular transgender public figures or in reaction to the
everlasting stereotypical humor of many stand-up comedians, for example. Within social sciences, gender
norms are rather understood as social constructs transmitted from generation to generation (Butler, 2009),
conveyed by Western social structures. Many studies show how LGBTQ people negotiate with
stereotypical and patriarchal representations of gender. A few of them have aimed to understand how
hegemonic norms of virility affect men in a negative way, in general and within the LGBTQ community,
as they generate structural violence (Corbin et al., 2011; Jeffrey and Lachance, 2012). Some scholars have
shown that patriarchal norms of virility are deeply embedded in socialization during school (Welzer-Lang,
2010). Others have put into light how these virility norms have a direct effect on women’s oppression
(Chetcuti and Jaspard, 2007). As for sociologists of religion, most have been interested in power relations
between men and women who are part of religious institutions (Roy, 2001), or in sexual minorities inside
and outside those institutions (Thumma and Gray, 2004; Fedele and Knibbe, 2013). However, very few
attempts have been made to relate religious functions of culture to transmission of gender norms.
This research focuses on norms of masculinity and virility within popular culture and on the ways they are
received and experienced by men who identity with the LGBTQ community. We take for granted that
those virility norms constitute knowledge and practices transmitted mainly through social interactions
between boys as young as three years old (Goffman, 2002; Pease, 2000; Clare, 2004). Experts specify that:
1/ Socialization to virility norms begin at a very young age, and that, 2/ Men feel an obligation to prove
their virility in multiple situations of social interactions among men (Corbin et al, 2011). This research
will rather focus on the ways gay, bisexual, transgender and queer men receive norms of virility. How do
they integrate these norms? How to they transmit them? Furthermore, how is it that some of those men
come to critique and renew such a category assigned culturally? In that sense, how do men identifying as
LGBTQ experience such norms in different ways (exclusion, bullying, etc.)?
The goal of this project is to describe the many ways in which virility norms are inscribed in social
representations and practices of men identifying with the LGBTQ community. Through interviews, it will
expose how men perceive virility as well as diversity around masculinity. In fact, it aims to gain a better
understanding of the reasons why LGBTQ men accept, refuse and/or renew those norms. It will focus
specifically on certain social experiences and dynamics of exclusion through the transmission of virility
norms. Virility being the most common mode of expression of masculinity within LGBTQ men’s

b) Framework: This research will consider socialization processes to virility norms in a dual perspective
related to theories in religious studies (Ménard, 2006). The first angle is associated with virility rites
(Jeffrey and Lachance, 2012) that, through repetition, determine particular forms of masculine experience
of body appropriation (Wulf, 2003; Schechner, 2006). The second complementary angle is based on
systems of sacralized symbols, allowing a comprehension of a network of meanings that put into
coherence the experiences of specific groups (Lévi-Strauss, 1958; McGuire, 2008). It will thus consider
sacredness as base for patriarchal symbolic system. In addition to what religious studies can bring, this
research is supported by an intersectional theoretical framework (Crenshaw, 2005). Questioning
essentialist social categories like sex, sexual orientation, race or age, intersectionality supposes that they
vary according to historical and sociocultural contexts, and that they result in new forms of expression as
well as many forms of oppression (sexism, homophobia, racism, ageism, ableism, etc.) (Brotman and
Lévy, 2008; Dorlin, 2009). Following the premise that individual and collective experiences cannot be
understood by accounting for only one dimension of identity, intersectionality is interested in the crossing
of social positionings and in the interaction of forms of discrimination and subordination engendered by
these intersections (Krüger and Levy, 2001; McCall, 2005; Mc Andrew, 2010). This theoretical approach
is most pertinent to highlight the specific experience of LGBTQ men. It allows for a better understanding
of virility norms and their impact on the construction and performance of men’s gender identity. It also
recognizes nonetheless a diversity of experiences within observed groups, due to the multiple social
positionings of individuals (religion, age group, social class, etc.) as much as individual trajectories
themselves (McCall, 2005).
c) Methodologies: This research project is two-fold: 1/ To clarify the meaning of such concepts, 2/ To
gather data through interviews. The first axis will, first, deepen the many definitions of virility and show
why it is associated with sacredness, with prohibitions and mythic knowledge. Secondly, it will explore
the multiple behaviors of virility as documented in scholarly literature. Thirdly, it will focus on diverse
socialization, identification, integration and recognition processes of virility behaviors within masculine
experience. The second data-gathering axis of this research uses qualitative methodologies adapted to the
analyses of social processes and phenomena, as well as the meaning given by individuals to their actions
(Paillé and Mucchielli, 2012). It aims to recruit 40 gay, bisexual, transgender and queer men of different
age groups. The sample will be diversified in regards to conjugal status and sexual history, for example,
men who have experienced heterosexual marriage and fatherhood before coming out as LGBTQ. Such
prior experiences have inevitably influenced their relationship to patriarchal virility norms (Arber et al.,
2003). It will also see to diversify cultural origins in order to gain a better understanding of the complex
power relations coming into play in the transmission and reproduction of masculine identities in society
(Connell, 2005). Recruitment will rely mainly on associative organizations or groups like the Belgian
LGBT associations Homoparentalités and Rainbow House where the research project will be announced.
Because of the intimate nature of the research themes, these structures will promote the project through
multiple channels (posters, online, newsletters) at large and in an indirect manner (Breines et al., 2004;
Argenti and Schramm, 2009).
Collection of data will be conducted through individual semi-directive interviews. This research will
consider the participants’ life trajectories and the life narrative method (Bertaux, 2010). The interview
guide includes three themes. It will first interrogate the contemporary transmission processes and the ways
in which various norms are reproduced throughout the course of one’s life (sacredness of virility, virility

rites, prohibitions around virility, beliefs and myths about virility). It will then focus on the integration of
these norms within masculine identity and their (emotional, social and sexual) experience in regards to
those norms (practices and rites). Data analysis will then specifically turn to variations in behaviors and
attitudes throughout the course of their life in regards to virility norms (discrepancies and continuities). It
will borrow Rémy and Ruquoy’s (1990) four-step method, bringing to light recurring themes, comparing
significations, forming categories and obtaining more objective meanings (Paillé, 2006). A questionnaire
will be used to gather socio-demographic information on the informants. Each of them will receive a
symbolic monetary compensation of 30€ for their participation.
d) Research calendar and expected outcome: This project will start in June 2017. Two years will be
necessary to complete this research: literature review and ethical certification (June 2017 to September
2017), planning and execution of recruiting and collection of data (October 2017 to May 2018), data
analysis, followed by the production of academic papers and the presentation of research in various
conferences (June 2018 to October 2019). Two papers will be submitted to scientific journals, Social
Compass and Anthropologica, by the end of the year 2018. Finally, we will present the results of our
postdoctoral research during the International Society for the Sociology of Religion conference in 2018,
and at the American Academy of Religion, among other conferences.
