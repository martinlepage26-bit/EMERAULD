# SEALED CARD

## Extracted Text

Introduction
The glitch is the tell: the moment someone senses the machine in a work, flinches, and turns
that feeling into suspicion toward the maker. When meaning feels shaky, audiences reach for
purity tests. Is it real. Is it honest. Is it legitimate. Is it worth trusting.
The Sealed Card Protocol begins from that flinch and treats it as data rather than confusion.
The current controversy around generative AI is often framed as a dispute about capability.
Can the system produce plausible texts, plausible images, plausible voices. Yet the sharper
problem is institutional and cultural, not technical. The struggle is over responsibility and
legitimacy: who can be held accountable for what a hybrid pipeline produces, how
accountability is evidenced, and how recognition is converted into durable credit when
mediation becomes newly visible and newly nameable. In the Sealed Card Protocol, the
glitch marks the precise point where recognition either converts into legitimacy or is rerouted
into suspicion about the pathway.
This paper refuses a familiar shortcut in that debate. It does not ask whether AI corrupts
authenticity, as if authenticity were the absence of mediation or the presence of a pure interior
voice. It treats authenticity and legitimacy as negotiated readings of mediation, stabilized or
destabilized at interfaces: peer review, platform formatting, citation regimes, aesthetic
conventions, and disclosure rules. Under these conditions, the same artifact can be praised for
clarity while the pathway that produced that clarity is treated as contamination. The same
work can be received as convincing while its maker is rendered suspect. The glitch is the
moment this conversion is decided.
To name what is being decided, the paper works with charging as an action rather than charge
as a substance. Charging is the social work by which meaning is instilled and made to stick. It
is how an object, a text, or a practice gathers credibility or suspicion faster than anyone can
justify the judgment in words. Charging can hold, but it can also glitch. It can spike into
sudden authority, implode into instant delegitimation, or dissolve until the artifact remains
visible but socially dead. The analysis therefore tracks where charging holds, where it fails,
and how institutions and publics manage that failure at the seam.
Three arms are coupled to observe how legitimacy is manufactured across domains that are
often discussed separately. The first arm is artistic and it is placed first on purpose because
this is where the glitch is most quickly perceived. It treats two practices as the two sides of
one instrument. HEXA is a recursive writing pipeline that runs long-duration material
through cycles of revision, re-ingestion, and re-circulation, making authorship legible as
procedure rather than origin. Wheels of Will is an Instagram-based visual practice that
re-photographs the same ritual tools across the Pagan year, testing what reads as anchored
versus staged when objects circulate under platform conditions.
The second arm is academic and it tracks legitimacy as infrastructure. It treats
knowledge-making as genre-governed labor and asks how institutions reward fluency, tempo,

and performable coherence alongside argument. This arm uses autoethnographic procedure as
evidence, including the decision to provide a markdown trace of the human and AI
interaction chain as an accountability object. The trace is not included to perform innocence.
It is included because legitimacy is increasingly indexed to auditability while the moral
economy of authorship remains attached to fantasies of solitary production.
The third arm is ritual and it closes the analysis because it forces the most demanding
evidence question. Witch bottles are used as the crystallizing case, moving from
archaeological archetype to contemporary practice to display and misrepresentation. The
point is not to resolve whether ritual “works” under the criteria of institutional proof. The
point is to show that entire regimes already treat certain effects as socially real without
requiring experimental recovery, and to examine how charging is redistributed when ritual
objects are translated into galleries, feeds, and academic prose. The arm order follows the
reception order of contemporary judgment: surface first, institution second, evidence-limit
last.
Contemporary scholarship
Recent research on generative AI has converged on a point that matches the paper’s opening
premise about the glitch-as-tell: the controversy is less about whether AI can produce
plausible cultural objects than about how responsibility and legitimacy are assigned once
mediation becomes perceptible. When a reader senses the machine in a text, the evaluation
often shifts from content to pipeline. The question becomes who can be held accountable for
what was produced, how accountability is evidenced, and how recognition is converted into
durable credit when production is distributed.
Publication ethics guidance has been unusually explicit here, partly because authorship is a
liability-bearing category. Positions that deny AI the status of author do more than protect a
humanist ideal. They preserve a node that can be praised, punished, corrected, sued,
sanctioned, credited. Under this logic, disclosure is not a procedural footnote but the visible
edge of an epistemic sorting problem. When text is produced through hybrid pipelines,
institutions must decide what counts as labor, what counts as contribution, and what counts as
accountable agency. The author remains the seal that keeps responsibility legible even as
production becomes less visibly solitary.
Within this ethics-centered strand, transparency is often presented as a pragmatic
compromise. The core claim is stable across many contexts: AI may be used, but credibility
increasingly depends on auditability, what was delegated, what was verified, and where
human judgment was exercised. Yet the literature also shows the limit of transparency as
solution. Disclosure can stabilize procedure while leaving the deeper hierarchy intact: the
assumption that legitimate work must be traceable to a recognizable authorial pathway, and
that certain pathways are morally suspect even when openly declared.

Parallel debates in law and policy reinforce how strongly legitimacy is being tied to the
category of the human author. Where copyright or analogous regimes insist on human
authorship as a condition of protection, this functions as a boundary-maintenance technology.
It preserves a privileged space for human agency not only as romantic ideology but as a
mechanism for ownership and liability. For the purposes of this paper, the key point is not the
legal detail but the pattern: institutions repeatedly protect “the human author” because the
category allows consequences to land. A distributed pipeline strains that function and invites
policing, especially in domains where the author has historically been imagined as origin
rather than coordinator of mediation.
In art scholarship and creative industries research, the center of gravity shifts from
responsibility to authenticity, aura, and entangled agency. A recurring move is to revisit
accounts of mechanical reproduction to make sense of generative production, while noting
that generative systems introduce a different configuration of agency than earlier
reproduction technologies. Empirical work on artists using generative tools tends to describe
practice as negotiation among intention, system affordances, and data histories, producing
expanded or entangled authorship rather than a clean division between human and machine.
Systematic syntheses of this research repeatedly return to the same contested questions:
originality, value attribution, provenance, labor visibility, and the legitimacy of AI-assisted
creativity.
One useful synthesis emerging from this literature is the tension between process-value and
artifact-value. In many art worlds, authenticity has historically been anchored to traceable
processes: training, technique, apprenticeship, the mark of the hand, and the social legibility
of effort. High-output generators destabilize this anchoring by making the cost of production
harder to read at the surface, even when substantial conceptual and curatorial labor persists
upstream. The resulting disputes are rarely only aesthetic. They are moral economies about
what kinds of work deserve recognition and what kinds of mediation must remain invisible to
remain acceptable.
Disability studies and queer theory introduce a further critical dimension that mainstream AI
debates often treat as secondary: the politics of tempo, fluency, and legibility. Crip time has
become foundational for showing how institutions assume stable bodies, stable schedules,
and stable cognition, and how those assumptions operate as structural filters rather than
neutral expectations. In queer disability scholarship, this is linked to critiques of normative
productivity and the demand that legitimacy be performed through particular bodily and
temporal forms. In practical terms, the popular language of “shortcuts” often presumes a
stable baseline of time and capacity. Disability-informed scholarship undermines that
baseline by treating time as socially organized, unevenly distributed, and routinely
weaponized as a credibility test.
Within this frame, AI can function as a legibility prosthesis inside infrastructures that already
distribute credibility through fluency and tempo. This does not imply automatic
democratization. It implies a re-routing of compliance: drafting, reformatting, genre

stabilization, and iterative rewriting can be redistributed. Yet the same re-routing can
intensify capture if institutions reward only the final smoothness of the object while erasing
the embodied constraints that made smoothness newly possible. The result is not simply
“debate about ethics.” It is contest over who is permitted to be readable without being
punished for the tools that made readability achievable.
A final body of scholarship, relevant to the ritual arm, concerns the interplay between digital
mediation and contemporary religion. Here the literature is less useful as trend-reporting than
as analytic permission. It opens a route for treating ritual as an epistemic domain rather than
private belief content, and for treating re-enchantment as a cultural dynamic rather than a
regression. In a digital ecology, ritual instruction can circulate as tutorials, altars can circulate
as images, and experiences can circulate as testimony, each format reorganizing what counts
as evidence, what counts as authority, and what can be trusted. This matters for the Sealed
Card Protocol because the paper’s central concern is not whether ritual is “true,” but how
charging is allocated and contested when practices are translated into public-facing media and
judged through alien proof regimes. In practice, this means spells circulate as scripts, rituals
as step-by-step posts, altars as images, and efficacy as testimony, each format producing its
own credibility cues and its own failure modes.
Across these literatures, the shared analytic target is legitimacy conversion: the social and
institutional process that decides when recognition is allowed to become durable credit.
Taken together, these strands converge on a structure that matches the paper’s design.
Institutions attempt to stabilize accountability through disclosure norms and authorship rules.
Art scholarship negotiates reconfigured agency and contested authenticity under conditions
where process is increasingly unreadable at the surface. Disability and queer theory make
visible the politics of tempo and fluency that shape who can be recognized as a creator of
knowledge in the first place. Scholarship on digital religion provides a language for taking
ritual seriously as practice, authority, and evidence. The next section introduces the interlude
that names the title’s logic of containment and consequence, then specifies the conceptual
vocabulary and method used to observe legitimacy conversion across the three arms.
Interlude: The Sealed Card logic
The Sealed Card Protocol takes its title as a theory of power rather than a decorative
metaphor. In Sakura Cardcaptor, a child becomes responsible for forces she did not create.
Those forces are elemental, unstable, and consequential. The story is not about mastery in the
heroic sense. It is about containment, release, and re-sealing under conditions of asymmetry.
A small body becomes the custodian of a system whose outputs can exceed intention, exceed
comprehension, and still demand accountability.
That structure maps sharply onto contemporary generative infrastructures. Models amplify
production while also amplifying anxiety, suspicion, and institutional policing. In this
ecology, “AI anxiety” is not simply an emotion circulating around new tools. It is a
governance condition. It shapes disclosure demands, reorganizes what counts as credible

labor, and sharpens the conditions under which recognition is converted into legitimacy. The
author function hardens into a seal because it remains the institution’s favored site for
consequences to land.
This is not an analogy for content but a model of containment and consequence, a way to
describe power that amplifies through mediation while responsibility is still demanded at the
level of the person. Within this paper, the Sealed Card Protocol names a practice-based
inquiry into whether mediated work can still be charged in ways that survive evaluation
without collapsing into purity tests. It also names the risk that the seam will be treated as
contamination the moment it is sensed. The glitch becomes the operational trigger: a
perceptual stop that converts detection into suspicion, and suspicion into a legitimacy
decision.
In sum. this interlude clarifies the conceptual logic of the paper’s title: power that amplifies
through mediation, accountability demanded at the level of the person, and legitimacy
distributed through the policing of pathways.
Conceptual framework and Methods
This article treats legibility as a power relation rather than a neutral property of texts, images,
or bodies. Legibility is produced through institutions, platforms, and subcultural publics that
decide what counts as evidence, what counts as work, what counts as a credible voice, and
who counts as a creator of knowledge. The framework refuses a familiar split between
authentic experience as interior truth and legitimate knowledge as exterior form. That split
allows legitimacy to present itself as neutral evaluation while quietly policing style, tempo,
and the visible performance of solitary authorship. The Sealed Card Protocol replaces it with
an interface account: authenticity and legitimacy are negotiated at the sites where mediation
happens, peer review, platform formatting, citation regimes, aesthetic conventions, disclosure
rules, and now generative AI. The question is not whether AI corrupts authenticity. The
question is how evaluative grammars decide which mediations are acceptable technique and
which are treated as contamination, and how quickly that decision attaches to the producer
rather than the artifact.
The conceptual hinge is charging as an action. Charging is the social work of instilling
meaning so that an artifact can survive circulation as something real enough to trust. It is not
a substance that objects possess. It is a public allocation of credibility or suspicion that often
attaches faster than explanation. Charging can accumulate through repetition, through marks
of use, through narrative framing, through aesthetic coherence, and through procedural
traceability. Charging can also glitch. The glitch is the operational trigger in this framework:
a perceptual stop, a cognitive dissonance, an aesthetic incoherence that makes mediation
audible and turns that audibility into a legitimacy event. The unit of analysis is the seam: the
moment detectability becomes felt, named, and converted into a decision about legitimacy.
In the moment of glitch, recognition and legitimacy can split. An artifact can be compelling
while the pathway that produced it is treated as disqualifying.

A disability-informed account of time, labor, and evaluation sharpens what is at stake.
Academic legitimacy often presumes a normal tempo and a normal body for producing
scholarship: extended endurance, consistent output, and the capacity to sustain the
performance of authorial coherence. Disability and queer disability scholarship reframes time
as infrastructural and unevenly allocated rather than personally deficient. Under such
conditions, “rigor” is often silently equated with a specific tempo of production and a specific
style of self-presentation, making disability a hidden sorting mechanism for credibility.
Generative AI can disrupt this sorting by externalizing and redistributing parts of the labor
chain: drafting, reformatting, genre stabilization, iterative rewriting, and the compression of
revision cycles. Yet the same redistribution can trigger suspicion precisely because it makes
mediation visible and because it reveals that many institutional judgments were never only
about ideas. They were also about who could perform the right kind of labor in the right kind
of time.
The Sealed Card Protocol treats AI as an amplifier of existing power relations. AI can widen
access to legible form while also tightening institutional control over what counts as
legitimate production. The protocol is designed to observe that ambivalence empirically. It
asks how legitimacy is assigned, withdrawn, and weaponized when mediation becomes
detectable, and how charging behaves when the seam is sensed.
Methodologically, the Sealed Card Protocol uses a triangulated, practice-based design.
Triangulation here makes explicit coupled evidentiary regimes to expose the same conversion
mechanism: how recognition becomes durable legitimacy or fails to become it. The analysis
proceeds in the order the protocol unfolds in lived reception.
The first arm concerns artistic practice and reception. It appears first because this is where the
glitch is most rapidly perceived and translated into a legitimacy judgment. This arm treats
two practices as two sides of one instrument. Wheels of Will is an Instagram-based
photographic practice that re-photographs ritual tools across the Pagan year under shifting
aesthetic registers while preserving anchor images that track marks of use. HEXA is a
recursive writing practice that treats text as archive and experiment, running long-duration
material through cycles of production, revision, re-ingestion, and re-circulation. The HEXA
figure is the author’s embodied interpretation of Hex A. Decimal, a fleeting entity that
structures the protocol as a whole.
This arm is also anchored in ReBoot, which offers, since the 1990s, a prefigurative media
theory of legitimacy under computational governance. Set within the digital city of
Mainframe and ruled by the unseen “User,” the series dramatizes identity as something
continuously evaluated inside externally imposed rule sets. Each descending Game Cube
functions as an episodic test: characters must perform under algorithmic constraint, and
survival does not equal sovereignty. One may win the game and still remain structurally
subordinate. The series makes visible a conversion problem that the Sealed Card Protocol
tracks directly. Recognition occurs at the surface, during performance. Legitimacy is

conferred only when mediation is deemed acceptable within the governing architecture. As
with peer review, hiring committees, and AI provenance audits, the decisive question is not
simply whether something works, but whether it was produced through sanctioned pathways.
Hexadecimal is crucial to this architecture. Operating beyond binary stability, she exceeds the
system’s grammar. She is not illegible because she lacks structure. She is illegible because
she destabilizes the criteria by which structure is recognized. In this sense, Hexadecimal
anticipates the glitch as theorized here: the moment mediation becomes perceptible and
triggers suspicion about the pipeline rather than engagement with the artifact. Read alongside
an autoethnographic analysis of institutional containment, ReBoot becomes more than a
reference point. It becomes structural metaphor. The stutter, like the glitch, interrupts fluency
and exposes the evaluative norms embedded in institutional listening. HEXA, named from
Hexadecimal, inherits not chaos but diagnostic excess: a refusal of binary authentication
regimes that confuse smoothness with legitimacy. The series stages, in advance, the core
wager of this paper. Authenticity is not purity. Legitimacy is not survival. Both are negotiated
within architectures that reward protocol over transformation.
Within this arm, Wheels of Will functions as the site where charging and glitching become
materially legible under platform conditions. The repetition is not redundancy. It is a
controlled loop designed to make mediation visible without collapsing into a purity claim. By
holding the same objects steady while the surrounding style changes, the practice isolates
what charging looks like as it moves across circulation regimes: devotional documentation,
contemporary art display, lifestyle branding, and platform-native content optimized for scroll.
The protocol reads these shifts as tests of legitimacy. The question is not whether the objects
are authentic in an interior sense, but how their mediation becomes socially stabilized, and
where that stabilization breaks.
Instagram functions here as an evaluative interface. It compresses time, rewards immediate
readability, and amplifies suspicion when an image appears too staged, too polished, or too
strategically aesthetic. Wheels of Will therefore tracks how doubt attaches at the seam where
mediation becomes perceptible: the moment the viewer senses the apparatus, the edit, the
register shift, the pipeline. The anchor images operate as an evidentiary counterweight. Marks
of use, residue, wear, and material fatigue become legibility signals that can convert
recognition into conditional trust, not by proving truth, but by making continuity visible
across the year. In this arm, charging names the practical work of stabilizing meaning across
repeated exposures. Glitching names the moment that stabilization fails, when the object is
recoded as prop, commodity, or performance. The protocol treats that recoding as data,
showing how legitimacy is not a property of the artifact but an outcome of
platform-conditioned reading practices, where visibility can be granted quickly while
endorsement remains contingent on the perceived pathway.
The second arm is academic procedure and institutional evaluation. It treats
knowledge-making as genre-governed labor and legitimacy as infrastructure. The academic
pipeline is operationalized as data: outlining, targeted source ingestion, genre-constrained
drafting, iterative revision, formatting, and response tracking. Institutional reactions are

treated as evidence of what is actually rewarded and what is punished: clarity versus
compliance, argument versus fluency performance, originality versus genre obedience. This
arm includes a methodological intervention into the disclosure debate through the provision
of a markdown trace of the human and AI interaction chain, treated as an accountability
artifact that exposes distributed labor rather than hiding it behind solitary-author mythology.
It is treated as a governance artifact, a chain-of-production object that makes distributed labor
inspectable without restoring the myth of solitary origin.
The third arm is ritual practice and evidentiary limits. It closes the analysis because it forces
the strongest evidence question. Ritual is treated as epistemic practice rather than private
belief content, a technology of agency that converts injury into form, form into relation, and
relation into survivable continuity. Witch bottles serve as a crystallizing case because they sit
at the intersection of archaeology, display cultures, and contemporary practice. They are
materially legible and historically traceable while remaining resistant to auditability in the
institutional sense. In this arm, the protocol tracks how charging is redistributed when ritual
objects and practices move across proof regimes, and how legitimacy is reallocated through
display, aestheticization, and platform circulation.
The protocol’s materials are deliberately heterogeneous because legitimacy is enforced
through multiple grammars and across multiple evaluative interfaces. Primary textual
artifacts include selected HEXA texts, including the 2008 Ovid and Pygmalion piece and its
later refitting, treated as longitudinal evidence of how a text’s charging, and the suspicion it
attracts, shift across time and circulation regimes. The pair is not used to stage a simple
before-and-after in quality. It is used to isolate the seam where conditions of making become
perceptible, and where readers recode the same intensity as either attachment, indulgence,
strategy, or contamination depending on the register through which they audit legibility.
Primary visual artifacts include a set of Wheels of Will bottle photographs treated as a
sequence that stages controlled variation in aesthetic register while holding the object-family
constant. Read as a controlled variation sequence, the images preserve anchor shots that track
marks of use and continuity across the Pagan year, while shifting lighting, framing, and
stylistic cues to observe how charging travels under platform conditions. The method treats
Instagram not as a neutral repository but as an evaluative interface that compresses time,
rewards immediate readability, and amplifies suspicion when an image appears too polished,
too staged, or too deliberately aesthetic.
Alongside these artifacts, the protocol retains procedural traces as chain-of-production
objects that make the distribution of work inspectable: drafts, revision histories, prompts
where relevant, capture settings, sequencing decisions, and circulation metadata. These traces
are not appended as transparency theater. They are used analytically to track when visibility
of the pipeline increases trust and when it triggers legitimacy collapse. Institutional feedback
is treated as data about legitimacy conversion rather than as neutral assessment. Comments,
refusals, endorsements, and requests for framing are read as signals of which mediations are

sanctioned, which are tolerated, and which are treated as contamination once they become
detectable.
Analytically, the paper uses close reading and interface analysis across arms. In the artistic
arm, the method is comparative close reading of textual versions and close visual analysis of
the bottle sequence, focusing on anchors, seams, and glitch triggers. In the academic arm, the
method is procedural autoethnography constrained by traceability, attending to decision
points where compliance is produced and where legitimacy becomes contestable. In the ritual
arm, the method is evidentiary critique, tracking how ritual objects and claims are stabilize as
real through pattern and repetition while being structurally vulnerable to institutional
demands for proof and to representational violence in display and platform regimes.
Because the data are situated in one trajectory and its associated practices, the protocol does
not claim representativeness. It claims analytic precision about legitimacy at the level of lived
procedure: what gets rewarded, what gets refused, what gets erased, what becomes credible
through form, and what becomes suspect through detectability. It refuses curative fantasies in
which technology repairs the “problem” of non-normative time or cognition. The ethical
commitment is accountable mediation: traceable procedure and real responsibility without
granting institutions the right to monopolize what real work is allowed to look like.
Analysis and Results
The Sealed Card Protocol treats AI as a medium of crossing rather than a discrete object of
debate. The aim is to track what changes in the conversion from recognition to legitimacy
once mediation becomes detectable, nameable, and therefore policed. Across all three arms,
the same mechanism reappears: artifacts can be recognized quickly while legitimacy remains
conditional, and the condition is often the pathway.
The artistic arm begins at the surface because this is where evaluation happens first and
fastest. Wheels of Will makes charging visible as an action performed through anchors rather
than claims. The photographic setup is deliberately spare, forcing attention onto seals,
bindings, residues, density of contents, and the visual economy of containment. A
wire-wrapped neck reads as constraint and decision. Screws suspended in dark liquid read as
weight and consequence. A tall vial bound in red borrows authority from a specimen
aesthetic without becoming scientific, a cross-regime citation of evidentiary style. An amber
bottle holding plant matter shifts the grammar toward infusion and slow time, inviting both
care and trivialization depending on the viewer’s threshold. A pendant and pigment smears
pull the object toward identity and gesture, increasing the likelihood that it will be read as art
object first and ritual second. Wax drips and a skull accelerate recognition by offering
immediate archetype cues, and that acceleration is double-edged. It can spike credibility in
one audience and trigger dismissal in another. Across these images, charging is not inside the
bottle. Charging is what happens to the bottle when its pathway is inferred from material cues
and judged as anchored or staged.

HEXA runs the same test through language. Where Wheels of Will re-photographs objects
across the year to track how charging changes under shifting circulation regimes, HEXA
re-processes texts across time to track how a voice survives contact with evaluative
interfaces. The point is not improvement in an abstract sense. The point is how an artifact is
re-coded when its pathway becomes visible, and how that re-coding converts recognition into
endorsement or into suspicion.
The 2008 Ovid and Pygmalion piece charges itself through drift, reverence, pastiche, and an
unarmored intimacy with lineage. Its intensity is not delivered as thesis. It is delivered as
atmosphere. The sentence is permitted to wander until it becomes a room, and the room
becomes an archive: a space where attachment is not declared but enacted as form. The text
does not present itself as a disciplined argument with a single spine. It moves by accretion,
cadence, and association, the way devotion or memory moves. In institutional registers, that
method can be misread as a lack of rigor because it refuses the usual legitimacy signals: overt
framing, explicit stakes, a clean conceptual scaffold, the quick presentation of an animating
claim. The problem is not that the text lacks structure. The problem is that it carries structure
in the wrong place. Its architecture is rhythmic and affiliative rather than propositional, which
makes its charging legible to a reader trained to feel tone, but illegible to a reader trained to
audit argument.
The later refitting is a survival move under platform conditions. It tightens cadence, segments
pacing, sharpens hinge lines, and seals itself for circulation through framing and tags. This is
not merely editing for clarity. It is a re-engineering of legibility. The platform demands
scan-readability. It rewards clean segmentation, quotable lines, visible structure, and explicit
cues about what the text is doing. The refitted version therefore makes its scaffolding
readable. It signals intention. It tells the reader how to read. It anticipates misrecognition and
preemptively corrects it. In exchange, it risks being read as constructed in platform registers,
not because it is less intimate, but because it displays its conditions of making. The seal
becomes visible.
The comparative result is not a simple before-and-after in quality, authenticity, or force. It is a
paired demonstration of how legitimacy judgments attach to different kinds of mediation. The
earlier text risks being coded as indulgent because it does not perform institutional
argument-spine conventions. The refitted text risks being coded as strategic because it
performs platform conventions too cleanly, too knowingly. In both cases, suspicion attaches
at the seam where mediation becomes perceptible: where the artifact reveals its pathway, its
recalibration, its survival tactics.
This is the coupling insight that the protocol treats as diagnostic rather than moral. Mediation
becomes controversial not when it exists, but when an audience decides it should have
remained invisible. The scandal is not mediation. The scandal is detection. When the reader
can see the method, the interface, the optimization, the constraint, the ritual of revision, the
evaluation shifts from what the text does to how it was made and whether it was made “the
right way.” HEXA therefore functions as a language-based stress test of legitimacy regimes.
It shows that charging is not only an affective intensity in the artifact. Charging is also the

work of aligning that intensity with the reading protocols of a given environment, and
glitching is what happens when those protocols refuse the alignment and recode the same
force as contamination.
The academic arm shows how that same flinch becomes rule. Academic legitimacy is a
genre-governed economy that rewards certain tempos, certain tonalities, and certain
coherence performances. Under those conditions, AI does not merely speed up writing. It
alters which parts of the labor chain are visible and therefore which parts can be policed. A
paper can meet structural expectations and still trigger legitimacy withdrawal if the pathway
is deemed unacceptable. The withdrawal is rarely framed as disagreement with ideas. It is
framed as authorship anxiety. Who counts as an author functions as the institution’s seal
because it anchors liability, credit, and sanction. This is also why the protocol’s decision to
provide a markdown trace of the human and AI interaction chain matters methodologically. It
is not staged innocence and not confession. It is an attempt to relocate legitimacy from purity
to traceability by turning a hybrid pipeline into an inspectable procedure. Yet the arm clarifies
a limit that the wider transparency discourse often softens: disclosure can regulate procedure
while leaving intact the deeper hierarchy that equates rigor with visible strain and legitimate
authorship with solitary origin.
The ritual arm closes the analysis because it forces the strongest version of the evidence
problem and because it makes the digital ecology unavoidable. This arm tests what happens
when proof is demanded by platforms and institutions while efficacy remains structurally
resistant to audit. In contemporary magical practice, credibility is not produced primarily
through institutional certification. It is produced through recurrence, embodied uptake, and
relational proof, a practice that becomes “real enough” because it holds across time and
through the person who emerges from it. Yet digital mediation reorganizes where proof is
expected to appear. Ritual instruction circulates as tutorials. Tools circulate as aesthetic cues.
Testimony circulates as narrative. Efficacy circulates as before-and-after. Authority is rebuilt
as visibility: who can demonstrate competence through recognizable signs, who can narrate
transformation without being dismissed as performance, who can keep charge from
evaporating into content cadence.
Witch bottles crystallize the problem because they sit at the intersection of archaeology,
practice, and display. As archaeological archetypes, they are recognized through patterned
deposition and recurring form, but their efficacy cannot be recovered through institutional
proof because the decisive outcome is often negative, a harm prevented. The bottle persists
anyway as a material claim that a practice existed and that protection could be enacted by
means not reducible to verification. That same logic returns online under different
constraints. Objects and practices circulate as images and scripts that can be adopted,
remixed, branded, and judged at speed. The risk is not only skepticism. The risk is
mischarging through translation. A gallery can elevate the bottle as aesthetic sign while
evacuating the practice. A museum can reframe it as quaint folklore. A platform can flatten it
into content. Each frame keeps the container and edits the claim. Wheels of Will makes this
legible by showing how quickly containment becomes style, how easily residue becomes

decoration, and how often legitimacy flows toward the artifact while being withdrawn from
the practitioner. This is the ritual version of the academic conversion failure: acceptance of
the product, refusal of the pathway and the epistemic authority the pathway asserts.
Taken together, the three arms expose a shared feedback loop. Translation produces
recognition. Recognition triggers policing. Policing injures. Injury demands recomposition.
AI amplifies the translation phase by accelerating movement from lived archive to legible
form, which increases recognition events and multiplies opportunities for legitimacy
withdrawal at the seam. The central finding is therefore not that mediation has become newly
suspect. The finding is that legitimacy has long been managed through selective tolerance of
mediation, and generative AI makes that selectivity perceptible. The glitch is not merely an
error in the work. It is the moment an audience decides whether mediation is acceptable
technique or disqualifying contamination.
Conclusion
This paper began from a small, almost bodily event: the glitch as perceptual tell. Not a
technical failure, but the moment someone senses mediation, pauses, and turns that pause into
an evaluation of trust. The Sealed Card Protocol treated that pause as a legitimacy event and
followed its consequences across three coupled domains where work is made, circulated, and
judged. By naming the glitch as a governance event and charging as a social allocation, the
protocol shifts AI debates from tool permissibility toward the legitimacy machinery that
decides whose mediated work is allowed to count.
The analysis showed that the decisive struggle is not whether generative AI can produce
plausible artifacts. The struggle is how recognition is converted into legitimacy when
production is distributed. In the artistic arm, Wheels of Will demonstrated that charging is
performed through anchors rather than claims. Seals, bindings, residues, and containment
cues operate as condensed pathways that invite inference about how an object came to be and
whether it deserves trust. The same cues can also be reclassified as theatrics the moment an
audience decides the labor should have remained invisible. HEXA showed the parallel
dynamic in language. A text refitted for circulation can preserve attachment while changing
its risk profile. Drift can be punished as indulgence. Scaffolding can be punished as
construction. In both cases, suspicion adheres to the seam where the artifact reveals its
conditions of making.
In the academic arm, the same flinch becomes rule. Institutions can accept an artifact and still
refuse the producer’s claim to legitimate authorship because authorship functions as a seal for
liability, credit, and sanction. Under these conditions, AI intensifies pre-existing gatekeeping.
It lowers the cost of genre compliance while sharpening the policing of pathways, especially
when clarity appears without the visible struggle that institutions quietly treat as evidence of
rigor. The decision to provide a markdown trace of the human and AI interaction chain was
introduced as a methodological counter-move against purity metaphysics, relocating
legitimacy from interior authenticity toward accountable procedure. Yet the analysis also

[...]

clarified the limit of that move: traceability can satisfy disclosure norms without guaranteeing
recognition if the deeper hierarchy remains intact, the expectation that legitimate work must
look like solitary origin and bear a recognizable cost.
The ritual arm pushed the argument to its evidentiary edge. Ritual practice was treated as an
epistemic technology of agency rather than as belief content. In digital ecologies, authority
and proof are repeatedly rebuilt as visibility, tutorialization, testimony, and aesthetic cueing,
and that rebuilding makes practice vulnerable to mischarging through translation. Witch
bottles crystallized the point because they survive as objects while their original scenes of
efficacy cannot be recovered through institutional verification. The outcome that would
matter most is often negative, a harm prevented. The bottle persists anyway as a material
claim that protection could be enacted by means not reducible to proof. Contemporary
circulation repeats the conversion problem under new conditions. Display regimes and
platforms can elevate the container while editing the claim, accepting the artifact while
withdrawing legitimacy from the pathway and the epistemic authority it asserts.
Taken together, the three arms support a single claim. Generative AI does not simply increase
output. It reorganizes where legitimacy is assumed to reside and sharpens the conditions
under which recognition is allowed to become durable credit. By amplifying translation from
lived archive to legible form, AI increases recognition events and multiplies opportunities for
legitimacy withdrawal at the seam where mediation becomes audible. The glitch is therefore
not marginal. It is governance: an audience, an institution, or a platform decides whether
techniques are mediation or disqualifying contamination, and if those who wield them are
allowed to count as a creator of knowledge. The recurring finding is that legitimacy is less a
verdict on artifacts than a policing of pathways, and AI intensifies that policing by making
seams easier to sense.
The paper’s philosophical wager is to refuse a purity model of authenticity: authenticity is not
unmediated interior truth but a socially stabilized, accountable reading of mediation, with
legitimacy as its institutional form that decides what counts as evidence, work, and
responsible authorship. To charge something, or someone, names how that stabilization is
practically produced, and glitching names the moments it breaks, spikes, implodes, or
dissolves.
The open question that remains is the one the protocol keeps approaching without closing:
what happens when charging glitches at scale. When it spikes, credibility can outrun
explanation. When it implodes, legitimacy can collapse around a single seam, leaving the
artifact intact but socially dead. When it dissolves, work can remain visible while being
treated as weightless, unworthy of memory, unworthy of citation, unworthy of care. Those
outcomes are not aesthetic footnotes. They are the emerging terrain of knowledge-making,
art-making, and ritual meaning-making under generative conditions. The Sealed Card
Protocol does not propose a new purity test to manage that terrain. It argues for an explicit
politics of mediation with teeth: accountable procedure, real responsibility, and a refusal to let

legitimacy remain a disguised morality play about who is allowed to take which pathway into
recognition without being punished for the route itself.
