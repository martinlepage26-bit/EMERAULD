EDITORIAL BOAD REPORT - 2024
I.

SPECIAL ISSUE PROPOSAL:

Sorcellerie 2.0 : un domaine émergent - Witchcraft 2.0: an emerging field
I.

GUEST EDITORS :
Olivier SERVAIS : UCLouvain, Belgique
Lionel OBADIA : Lyon 2, France

II. BOARD’S GENERAL DECISION :
The current proposal is compelling as it focuses on a timely topic that is likely to gain in
significance in the future, and aims to do so through the digital culture, which is very original.
The Board has accepted the proposal with a few minor amendments, clarifications concerning
the articles (summary, methodology, language of publication) and a few suggestions. The Social
Compass editorial team suggests publishing this special issue in December 2025.

The board therefore invites the guest editors to consider the following elements to
strengthen the current proposal:
- In the introduction, it would seem appropriate to explain why the authors choose to discuss
witchcraft and not neo-witchcraft (or vice versa); to show the connections between witchcraft
and digital culture in the social world and the importance of researching them; to contextualise
witchcraft and online witchcraft by outlining the number of groups and individuals involved,
drawing on their own data or that available in the existing literature; to point out the influence
of witchcraft in the social and political world, in gender relations and in tourism to name but just
a few examples; and finally, to consider the conflicts and adaptations between witchcraft and the
use of the media (how collective practices may or may not develop online, for example).

- If feasible, include one or two articles from regions outside Europe and North America,
preferably from South America, Asia or Africa. One of the reviewers suggests contacting
Veronique Giménez-Beliveau to ask for the contact details of potential contributors.

III. COMMENTS OF THE BOARD MEMBERS

-

The global relevance of the special issue topic

Reviewer 1
This Thematic Issue is of great interest and value. It deals with an extremely important topic in the
contemporary debate of social sciences of religion because in the panorama of contemporary
spirituality, neo-paganism - witchcraft in particular - is a phenomenon that is becoming increasingly
widespread and growing, even in its online version. In every national context, we are witnessing,
especially in recent decades, the re-invention of ethnic religious traditions and their readaptation into
typically modern spiritual forms. Neo-druidism, ethenism, neo-witchcraft, wicca, Roman traditionalism,
neo-Hellenism, core shamanism, rhodnoveria, and Kemetism are just some of the many currents that
make up the variegated field of neo-paganism and that are appearing, with greater or lesser force, in
the virtual world.
Thus, the proposal interweaves the study of (neo?)witchcraft with that of the digital, revealing how
witchcraft itself has found in the digital ecosystem the conditions for a revival that, the authors write,
is unprecedented. In this sense, the authors well highlight how witchcraft recalls the study of central
themes in contemporary society connected to its ‘hypermodern themes’: spirituality, femininity,
ecologism, social engagement.

-

The special issue’s rational

Reviewer 1
The proposal starts from a pivotal point in the analysis of the phenomenon in question: neo-paganism
- witchcraft - as a response to the processes of globalisation and mediatisation of society. This insight
allows the authors to highlight how these processes have profoundly affected the sense of self on both
an individual and collective level, leading to a cultural homogenisation in Western society and an
uprooting of individuals with respect to inhabited time and space: witchcraft is an expression of a
critical stance towards mainstream society, with respect to which it offers a counter-current imagery.
This imaginary is based on the recovery of a more authentic relationship with nature perceived as a
re-enchanted place and as a refuge from urbanised society; on the creation of alternative forms of
community-based sociality; on the rediscovery of knowledge and cultural models inherited from
tradition and dismissed by modernity. Leveraging this imagery, witchcraft embodies a worldview
capable of attracting a growing number of practitioners and of engaging in dialogue with the rest of
society on issues such as ecologism, digital culture, the civil rights of LGBTQIA+ movements, and
alternative political identities. The innovativeness of the proposal derives from the fact that, while this
phenomenon, in its traditional forms, is now investigated in the academic perspective of pagan studies,
sociology and anthropology (especially in the Northern European and American area), there is little
research that focuses on its relationship with the digital, in order to show how many ways this tradition
finds shape in the online.
Since the proposal is a very good one, I would only make a few suggestions that the authors might find
interesting for their own purposes. I would suggest, in the Introduction, to contextualise the
relationship between witchcraft and digital culture within a broader framework that takes into account
the fact that witchcraft (I would speak of neo-witchcraft, and in any case the issue needs to be
thematised) is a product of contemporary culture and its diffusion is largely due to the ability to adopt
the language conveyed by the new media: popular culture products such as, for example, metal music,
fiction and television series (and not only digital channels) constitute one of the privileged channels for
its circulation and for the approach, especially by the younger generations, to its spiritual universe.
Moreover, in order to show the relevance of the topic treated (often ridiculed by mainstream
sociology), it would be useful if in the Introduction the authors devoted a few lines to show an estimate
of the actual size of witchcraft in the world (in presence/online) through the reconnaissance already
available (albeit partial) - which shed light on active groups and members, as well as the online presences
of the many solitary practitioners - or their elaborations. Similarly, again in the Introduction, it would
be useful to recall the social impact of neo-witchcraft, i.e. the relationships and reciprocal influences it
has with different spheres of society, paying particular attention to its relationships with popular
culture, economics and marketing, politics, forms of communitarianism, tourism, communication and

Finally, one last suggestion. Neo-witchcraft, like many other contemporary religious movements,
cannot avoid confronting the market. This is a theme that the authors (especially in Obadia's essay)
recall but, in my opinion, needs to be made more explicit and addressed. On the one hand, notoriety
and market success enable the legitimisation of neo-witchcraft leaders and organisations, giving visibility
to this spirituality and attracting new practitioners. On the other, the fact that gatherings, rituals,
trainings and other experiences are accessible for a fee, albeit often unrewarding, exposes participants
to criticism related to the idea that spirituality should be free. In this landscape, the advent of social
media and the possibilities of enjoying witchcraft online, as a primarily private matter, has generated
further ‘generational’ tensions between different ways of understanding and practising neo-paganism.
As a result of the Covid-19 pandemic, moreover, some of the activities traditionally offered live, such
as courses and rituals, have been realised online, expanding the target audience but also requiring ritual
forms and practices to be adapted to the affordances of the platforms used.
In other words, the authors should consider the difficult relationship with the market and social media
on the part of a neo-pagan current - that of neo-witchcraft - for which in-presence practice
nevertheless remains central (given the relevance of the collective ritual dimension), in contrast to the
various forms of neo-paganism that develop mainly online and which now dominate social media.
The weakest point, according to this reviewer, is the dimension of the scope. I suggest to broadening
the geographical diversity of research to include other areas and make the dossier effectively
geographically global in its scope. The authors intend to compare different forms of online witchcraft,
so increasing the instances of comparison will serve their purpose. In general, the abstracts of the
articles should develop more theoretical and methodological aspects.
In line with the Journal’s policy, guest editors will collect a bilingual dossier in the sense that – if I
understand well – some papers will be in English while some others in French.
Reviewer 2
The proposal appears well-grounded in theoretical considerations. It draws on established concepts
from digital religion studies (e.g., Heidi Campbell's work) and applies them to the realm of witchcraft
and magic. Furthermore, the proposal highlights the need to distinguish digital witchcraft as a unique
field of study separate from digital religion.
The special issue can make a significant contribution to an emerging field by extending current research
on digital witchcraft, which has so far been fragmented. It proposes to explore a variety of aspects,
including the socio-political engagement of digital witchcraft, its aesthetic and economic dimensions,

The articles’ selection

Reviewer I
As it turns out, the articles deal with the phenomenon from many angles and facets and share a
qualitative methodology, but the abstracts are too shallow to comment on the theoretical-analytical
intentions.
Reviewer 2
The article selection is adequate and interesting. The abstracts are a bit too short to really evaluate
the articles. I would encourage editors add some article from Latin America or Asia. As it stands now,
it’s much based in Europe.
