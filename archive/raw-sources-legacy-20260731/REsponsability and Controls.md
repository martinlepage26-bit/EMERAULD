Scenario: Imagine a delivery drone that is programmed to deliver packages to customers' homes. During one of its deliveries, the drone malfunctions and crashes into a parked car, causing damage.

Relevance: This example is relevant because it highlights the complexities of assigning moral responsibility when an autonomous system makes decisions without direct human intervention. 

Breakdown:

Backward-Looking Responsibility: In this case, we might ask, "Who is to blame for the crash?" Is it the drone's manufacturer for not ensuring the drone's reliability? Is it the software developer who programmed the drone? Or is it the company that operates the drone service?

Forward-Looking Responsibility: Moving forward, we need to consider what measures should be taken to prevent such incidents. Should there be stricter regulations on the testing of autonomous drones? Should companies be required to have human overseers monitoring drone operations?
Moral vs. Legal Responsibility: While the manufacturer might be held legally responsible for the malfunction, the moral implications are more complex. The company operating the drone may face public backlash, affecting their reputation and future business, even if they are not legally liable.
This example illustrates the challenges of determining responsibility in the context of AI and autonomous systems, emphasizing the need for clear ethical guidelines and regulations as technology continues to evolve.

Understanding Moral Responsibility: A Simple Explanation

In our discussion, we explored the idea of moral responsibility, which is about whether someone should be held accountable for their actions. Just because something causes an accident, like an iceberg sinking the Titanic, doesn’t mean it is morally responsible. This leads us to the important idea that to be morally responsible, a person or entity must have the ability to choose differently.

Think of it like this: if you see a dog that accidentally knocks over a vase, you wouldn’t blame the dog because it didn’t mean to do it and doesn’t understand the consequences. Similarly, if a person is forced to make a choice under extreme pressure, like being threatened, we might say they didn’t really have a choice, and thus, they shouldn’t be held morally responsible for their actions. The key takeaway is that having the ability to choose is essential for moral responsibility.

Causality refers to the relationship between cause and effect. If something causes an event, it is said to be causally responsible for that event. For example, if a driver runs a red light and causes an accident, the driver is causally responsible for the accident.

Key Points:

Not All Causes Are Morally Responsible: Just because something causes an event doesn’t mean it is morally responsible. For instance, an iceberg caused the Titanic to sink, but it cannot be held morally responsible because it cannot make choices.

Choice Matters: Moral responsibility requires that the person or entity had a choice in their actions. If someone is forced into a situation where they cannot choose differently, they may not be held morally responsible for the outcome.

So far, we've been talking about who's responsible for something that's already happened, like who's responsible for an accident caused by a self-driving car? This is another way to understand responsibility. In addition, to looking back and trying to find out who's to blame when something went wrong. We can also look forward and think about what we can do now to avoid things going wrong in the future. 
Saying that we want AI development to be safe, we don't want anyone to get hurt physically or mentally by AI applications. We want AI devices to work for the benefit of humans with no harmful side effects. Saying that we want the democratic processes to gain from AI, we don't want the elections to be fraudulent or that AI technology is used to give some people more influence at other people's expense. We want AI to help us get a better democracy, not destroy it. Who then is responsible for making sure that AI develops in this direction? That's a question of forward-looking responsibility. It's important to point out that our interest here is primarily on moral responsibility. 
This means, we are discussing who should, from a moral point of view, be responsible for making sure that AI development is safe and beneficial. This indicates that we're not looking for one certain who here, but for many different categories of people who contribute to this development in different ways, and thereby on a position to influence the direction of AI development.
