## Background and context

The problem this paper sits inside is not simply that generative AI exists, or that it can now produce passable academic prose, or that it can mimic style well enough to trigger anxiety. The deeper problem is that the infrastructures we use to decide what counts as knowledge are built to reward a very specific kind of legibility: smooth, stable, coherent, and temporally “normal.” Those infrastructures were already fragile and already unequal before AI became widely usable. AI did not invent the politics of recognition. It made their seams easier to feel.

The “glitch” is the tell because it names the moment those seams become perceptible in the body. Someone senses the machine in a work, flinches, and translates that sensation into suspicion toward the maker. In that instant, legitimacy stops being an abstract ideal and becomes a reflexive sorting action. A purity test appears. Is it real. Is it honest. Is it legitimate. Is it worth trusting. The important thing about that purity test is not whether it is correct. The important thing is what it reveals: legitimacy is not only a judgment about content. It is a judgment about mediation, about pathways, about how a thing got here and whether that route is allowed to count.

That is why the paper refuses the familiar frame that asks whether AI corrupts authenticity. That frame tends to smuggle in a fantasy of unmediated creation, as if legitimate knowledge emerges from an interior self and then gets expressed outward. The more accurate frame is procedural and infrastructural. Knowledge becomes legitimate through institutional grammars that decide what counts as evidence, what counts as work, what counts as authorship, and who counts as a creator of knowledge. These grammars operate through mundane technologies of evaluation: peer review, platform formatting, citation regimes, genre conventions, aesthetic expectations, and the tacit moral economy of effort. The accusation “you didn’t write it” becomes legible here as more than a factual claim. It becomes a governance move, a withdrawal of credit from a pathway.

The paper also insists that this governance operates across domains that are often kept artificially separate. Academia is not the only place where legitimacy is policed. Platform-based art worlds do it, too, through attention economies, through expectations of visible struggle, through authenticity scripts that decide what looks staged versus what looks anchored. Subcultural and spiritual worlds do it as well, though often with different metrics and different stakes: lineage, tradition, embodied transformation, survivability, and the ethics of practice. The point is not to collapse these domains into one. The point is to show that they share a recognition pipeline, a set of conversions where a work becomes countable, a person becomes credible, and a mediation either passes as acceptable technique or gets named as contamination.

This is where the paper’s language shifts on purpose. Instead of treating charge as an attribute that objects possess, it treats charging as an action that worlds perform. Charging is the social work through which meaning is instilled, through which an artifact is made to carry credibility or suspicion as it circulates. It is an ensoulment by proxy, not because an object contains an essence, but because social worlds decide, quickly and often viscerally, what feels real enough to trust and what feels too smooth, too recent, too mediated to be safe. Charging is therefore not decorative vocabulary. It is the mechanism by which legitimacy becomes sticky.

Ritual practice sharpens this, because it makes the evidence problem explicit. Witch bottles are a useful anchor here precisely because they are materially present and historically legible, yet their “effect” is not the kind of effect institutions can verify. Archaeology and historical records can show that such bottles existed, how they were assembled, where they were placed, what patterns recur, and how the practice persists. Yet nobody is there to measure what they did to the house they were meant to protect, or to the witch they allegedly shielded against dark influence. The bottle just is. We accept it as magic anyway, sometimes because we believe, sometimes because we believe proof is not the point, sometimes because the social fact of believing is what matters. In other words, the bottle’s legitimacy as a magical object does not depend on an experimentally recoverable outcome. It depends on patterned form, contextual placement, and collective recognition. Witch bottles operate like archaeological archetypes: forms that stabilize meaning through repetition across time even when the original experiential scene is no longer retrievable.

This is not a detour into folklore. It is a way of saying the quiet part out loud. Many systems already operate on the assumption that effects do not have to be measurable for an artifact to be treated as real, meaningful, and socially operative. The legitimacy problem begins when we selectively forget that, and pretend that some domains require purity and auditability while other domains get to rest on pattern and trust.

The rise of generative AI intensifies the need to confront this selectivity, because AI makes mediation newly visible and newly contestable. It increases the probability that a work will be received through suspicion, not because it is worse, but because it is legible in a way that triggers the flinch. The glitch becomes the scene where charging is interrupted. A work that would have carried credibility can suddenly carry suspicion. A work can still be read as compelling while its maker is treated as disqualified.

The paper locates itself inside that scene, not as an external commentator, but as a situated experiment. This matters because a large part of the current debate is built on abstraction and moral theatre. People argue about disclosure as if it solves legitimacy, or about authorship as if it is a simple property of a text. This paper does something more awkward and more concrete. It makes its production pathway visible. It offers a markdown trace of the interaction chain between me and AI-Agatha as part of the evidentiary architecture. That trace is not included to perform innocence. It is included because the question is increasingly procedural: how was this made, what constraints shaped it, where did the thinking happen, what does accountability look like when writing becomes hybrid.

The two case studies in the artistic arm are designed to make this same recognition machinery visible from opposite ends. Wheels of Will stages object-legitimacy under platform conditions. It asks whether the mark of use can still anchor an object when style shifts and when viewers judge authenticity through images. HEXA stages language-legitimacy under hybrid writing conditions. It asks what happens when a detectable seam triggers suspicion and when the same voice must travel across time through recursion, revision, and re-ingestion. The paper’s analysis will place specific artifacts into the reader’s hands: a HEXA piece alongside an earlier version from youth, and images of witch bottles from Wheels of Will. These are not illustrations. They are recognition scenes. They will be unpacked as moments where charging is attempted, interrupted, validated, or refused.

That is the context. We are not simply watching a new technology arrive. We are watching older legitimacy systems reveal themselves, because they are being stressed. The paper’s task is to map that stress, to show the mechanisms that convert recognition into durable credit or into disqualification, and to treat the glitch as what it is: a governance moment disguised as an aesthetic intuition.
