## Conclusion: What the paper did, what it shows, what it changes

This paper began from a small, almost bodily event: the “glitch” as a perceptual tell. Not a technical failure, but the moment a reader, a viewer, an evaluator senses mediation, flinches, and translates that sensation into suspicion toward the maker. The wager was never to decide whether AI is good or bad, real or fake. The wager was to track what happens to legitimacy when mediation becomes newly visible, newly nameable, and therefore newly policed.

To do that, the article staged a three-arm design that treats legibility as infrastructure. The academic arm frames knowledge-making as a genre-governed institution that rewards fluency, tempo, and performable coherence as much as it rewards argument. The artistic arm is one instrument with two faces: Wheels of Will, an object-practice built for Instagram where ritual tools are re-photographed across the Pagan year in shifting styles while keeping one image per cycle anchored to the mark of use, and HEXA / The Hex(A)Decimal, a writing practice that treats text as archive and experiment, running long-duration material through a recursive pipeline where outputs can be reworked and re-ingested. The third arm, ritual and spiritual, refuses to remain metaphor. It insists on ritual as epistemic technology, a method for metabolizing injury into form, form into relation, relation into survivable agency.

The connective tissue across these arms is not a substance called charge. It is the act of charging. The practice of instilling meaning, of loading an artifact with credibility or suspicion as it travels. A kind of ensoulment by proxy, not because an object contains an essence, but because social worlds read mediation and decide, often instantly, what feels real enough to trust, what looks like work, what is allowed to stand in for lineage, legitimacy, or transgression. The paper’s claim is that the glitch is where this charging becomes audible. It is the seam where the proxy-work of mediation shows through, and where the institution or the audience rushes to test purity.

This is why the paper treats documentation as part of method rather than an appendix-level afterthought. The experiment is not only that AI can generate prose. The experiment is that a hybrid pipeline can be made legible in public. For that reason, the article offers a markdown trace of the interaction chain between me and AI-Agatha, not as spectacle, and not as confession, but as evidence. It is a way of refusing the convenient fog in which legitimacy debates often hide. If the accusation is “you did not write it,” the response here is not a defensive claim to interior authenticity. The response is procedural visibility: here is how the work was produced, revised, constrained, argued with, and made accountable.

Across the analysis, the same mechanism returns in different costumes, but it is always a recognition mechanism masquerading as a moral one. In academic settings, “you didn’t write it” often functions less as a descriptive claim than as a refusal to credit a redistributed labor chain. In artistic settings, the parallel refusal becomes “you didn’t make it,” which usually means: you did not make it in the right tempo, with the right visible strain, through the right aesthetic idiom of effort. In ritual settings, the refusal changes shape again, because the evidence of transformation is cumulative and embodied, but once translated into images or text it becomes subject to evaluative grammars that were never built to recognize somatic proof. The paper therefore does not ask who counts as an author in the narrow sense. It asks who counts as a creator of knowledge when knowledge is produced through mixed infrastructures of cognition, platforming, and care.

### Ritual and spiritual practice as an evidence problem

Witch bottles sharpen the paper’s philosophical stakes because they sit at an uncomfortable intersection: they are materially present, historically documented, and still conceptually alive, yet their “effect” is not the kind of effect institutions can verify.

Archaeologically, witch bottles are recognizable because they are patterned deposits. They recur in specific contexts, often under hearths, thresholds, foundations, chimneys, and sometimes in waterways, and they recur with unusual and repeated contents: pins, needles, nails, sometimes hair, nail clippings, fabric, and other elements conducive to sympathetic magic. On page 1 of *Buried Bottles: The Archaeology of Witchcraft and Sympathetic Magic*, Manning synthesizes this as both a material definition (what they contain) and a functional one (counter-magic, protection, curing misfortune), while also showing the evidentiary triangulation that makes them legible as a category: archaeology, documentary record, and folklore.

The paper leans on that triangulation because it clarifies a core point about charging. We accept witch bottles as magical without being able to run the experiment again. We do not have the house as it was, the fear as it was, the alleged witch as it was, the illness as it was. We have the bottle. We have the pattern. We have the record. We have the persistence. We have, in short, an archeological archetype: a form that stabilizes meaning through repetition across time, even when the original experiential scene is not retrievable. *Buried Bottles* makes this explicit by mapping how documentary sources describe construction and use, while archaeology shows repeated placement and repeated contents across finds, including the striking laboratory analysis of the Greenwich bottle as an instance where the sealed object could be studied with modern techniques.

But the philosophical knot is this: the “truth” of the bottle is not reducible to whether the house was protected. The bottle’s reality is distributed across belief, pragmatism, and the decision that it is not important to prove. Sometimes people believe. Sometimes they believe it is safer to act as if it might be true. Sometimes they believe that the social fact of believing is what matters, not the metaphysical question. Witch bottles are therefore not just artifacts. They are compact theories of legitimacy. They show how an object can be allowed to stand in for protection, lineage, and agency without needing institutional verification.

This is where Wheels of Will becomes more than an art series. It becomes a contemporary scene in which that archetype is re-staged under conditions of platform visibility. The witch bottle photograph is not only an image of a thing. It is an event where charging happens in public: an audience reads the bottle, reads the aesthetic choices, reads the mark of use, reads the tradition, and decides whether the object feels anchored or staged. The paper makes room for images of the witch bottles precisely because the visual does not illustrate the argument. It is the argument’s pressure point. The images will be unpacked as recognition scenes.

### What the HEXA example does in the structure

HEXA is the mirror-side of that same mechanism, except the object is language. Here, the glitch is not in a bottle, it is in a sentence. Detectability becomes a trigger. Suspicion becomes a verdict. The paper therefore makes room for a HEXA piece in two layers: a contemporary iteration produced inside the recursive pipeline, and an earlier version written “when I was young,” carried forward as raw archive. This is not nostalgia. It is method. The juxtaposition lets the paper track what changes and what persists when charging moves through time. It also lets the reader see that the question was never whether the voice is “mine.” The question is what kinds of mediation the institution will allow the voice to travel through without withdrawing legitimacy from the maker.

### What changes, and what the implications are

The paper’s core claim is not a soundbite about acceleration. It is a re-description of legitimacy as a conversion problem. Recognition is cheap and plentiful. Legitimacy is conditional and policed. Hybrid pipelines increase the number of moments in which someone is seen, read, assessed, and sorted, but they also tighten the informal conditions under which that recognition is allowed to become durable credit. The same work can be accepted as an artifact while the person is discredited as a producer of knowledge. The same legibility can be enjoyed while the pathway that produced legibility is treated as contamination. The glitch is the moment this conversion either happens or fails.

This matters because it shifts where we should look for ethics. Disclosure norms and audit language can manage liability, but they do not necessarily change the deeper moral economy that equates “real work” with a narrow performance of struggle and a narrow tempo of authorship. The disability-informed edge of the paper is that tempo is not neutral. Tempo is governance. If the system rewards steadiness and penalizes variability, then a tool that compresses editorial labor can widen access to legible form while intensifying suspicion toward those who needed that compression to participate at all.

What this paper offers, finally, is not a defense of AI and not a lament for lost authenticity. It offers a diagnostic and a proposal. The diagnostic is that charging, and the policing of charging, is already how legitimacy operates across academic, artistic, and ritual worlds. The proposal is that we stop pretending mediation is either pure or corrupt, and start treating it as a site of accountable practice. The markdown trace is one form of that accountability. The close reading of a HEXA piece across time is another. The unpacking of witch bottle images as public recognition scenes is a third. Together, these moves keep the paper honest about its own conditions of production while insisting that legitimacy cannot remain a purity test disguised as ethics.

The open question, which the paper leaves deliberately alive, is what happens when charging glitches at scale. Sometimes credibility spikes and outruns explanation. Sometimes it implodes from a single seam of detectability. Sometimes it evaporates, quietly, when work cannot be made to look like the right kind of work. Those are not aesthetic footnotes. They are the terrain on which academic-artistic legitimacy is being rebuilt under generative conditions.

## Introduction: The problem, cleanly posed, with room for the examples to do real work

The “glitch” is the tell: the moment someone senses the machine in a work, flinches, and turns that feeling into suspicion toward the maker. When meaning feels shaky, audiences reach for purity tests: is it real, honest, legitimate, worth trusting?

This paper takes that flinch seriously, not as a confusion to correct, but as an evaluative reflex that reveals how legitimacy is produced. In contemporary knowledge cultures, “realness” is not only a property of what is said or made. It is a property of how an artifact is processed, mediated, and accepted as such. Institutions, platforms, and subcultures do not merely host work. They sort it. They decide what counts as evidence, what counts as labor, what counts as a credible voice, and crucially, who counts as a creator of knowledge.

The central question is therefore not “Is AI authentic?” because that question smuggles in a purity fantasy that collapses under ordinary cultural life. The question here is more precise: what kinds of mediation are considered acceptable routes into credibility, and what kinds are treated as contamination once they become perceptible. The glitch matters because it is the instant when mediation becomes perceptible. When it does, recognition can split. A reader can recognize the object as meaningful and still refuse to recognize the maker as legitimate.

To observe that split rather than merely theorize it, the paper uses a three-arm design. The academic arm tracks institutional legibility as a gatekeeping technology. The artistic arm functions as a research-creation laboratory with two faces. Wheels of Will (conceived 2023; initiated 2024-09-24; Montréal, QC) is an ongoing Instagram photo series that re-photographs the same ritual tools across the Pagan year in different styles, while keeping one image per cycle anchored to the mark of use. It stages a public test of what reads as authentic versus staged, made sharper because these objects come from contemporary Pagan traditions often treated as “less real,” and because institutional recognition can hinge on artist-status thresholds. HEXA / The Hex(A)Decimal is a writing practice that treats text as archive and experiment, running multi-decade material through a recursive pipeline where outputs can be reworked and re-ingested. Here, the glitch names the seam where AI becomes detectable and triggers suspicion about authorship.

The third arm is ritual and spiritual practice, treated not as decorative belief content but as a method for composing agency and survival. Witch bottles anchor this arm because they are both materially legible and epistemically inconvenient. Archaeology and historical record can show that they existed, what patterns they follow, what they contain, where they are placed, how they are discussed, and how the practice persists into contemporary adaptations. *Buried Bottles* offers exactly this kind of triangulated account, including the recurring contents and placements that make the category recognizable, alongside documentary descriptions of construction and use.  Yet no one is there to assess the effect on the house it protects, or on the witch it allegedly shields against dark influence. The bottle just is. We accept it as magic anyway, sometimes because we believe, sometimes because it is safer to act as if belief is not the point, sometimes because we believe that the social fact of believing matters more than proof. That tension is not a side-note. It is the same legitimacy problem, expressed in another register.

The paper’s conceptual move is to treat charging as the common mechanism across all three arms. Charging is the social work through which an object, a text, or a practice is made to carry credibility or suspicion as it circulates. It is a practice of instilling meaning, a kind of ensoulment by proxy, and it is also a site where institutions intervene. When the charging glitches, when the mediation becomes audible, the object can remain compelling while the maker becomes suspect.

Methodologically, the paper makes a further commitment: it refuses to speak about hybrid production in abstractions while hiding its own pipeline. The article therefore includes a markdown trace of the interaction chain between AI-Agatha and me as part of the evidentiary architecture. This is not offered as total transparency or as moral theatre. It is offered because the question of legitimacy increasingly turns on how work was made, who did what, and what kinds of mediation are permitted to count as knowledge-making rather than fraud.

Images of witch bottles from Wheels of Will will appear not as illustration but as recognition scenes to be unpacked. The academic arm will keep returning to the same point, but from inside procedure: not “what is authorship,” but how legitimacy is converted, refused, and policed when mediation becomes visible, and when the glitch turns into a verdict.

Background and problem statement: three legitimacy regimes in collision

This article begins from a practical, politically loaded observation: in contemporary knowledge cultures, “realness” is not only a property of what is said or made, but of how it is mediated, formatted, and recognized. What counts as authentic, rigorous, or legitimate is produced inside specific regimes of legibility. In SAKURA, those regimes are not treated as passive “contexts” around an object; they are treated as active infrastructures that sort bodies, genres, methods, and voices into credible or non-credible forms (Butler 2004; McRuer 2006; Kafer 2013).

The project formalizes this claim through a three-arm experimental design. The first arm is academic: knowledge-making as an institutional practice, with its genres (article, review, “method”), its evaluative rituals (peer review, editorial gatekeeping), and its implicit norms of fluency, pace, and professionalism. The second arm is artistic: HEXA and Wheels of Will as a research-creation laboratory that tests contested authenticity in the age of digital production, asking what an “artifact” becomes when materiality, authorship, and aura are increasingly mediated (Benjamin 1936; Haraway 1985). The third arm is ritual and spiritual: contemporary witchcraft as a technology of transformation and survival, where lived experience is not supplemental to theory but functions as a mode of knowing, repair, and agency (Schechner 2002; Butler 2004).

The core question is not “Is AI real?” It is: What kinds of realities are recognized as real, by whom, and under what evidentiary economies? Your own formulation makes this problem empirical. You describe an AI-generated portrait as “definitely me, even though it was not me” (Author fieldnotes 2026). The point is not whether the image is technically accurate. The point is that recognition still happens. Social perception still confers objecthood. The artifact becomes public-facing evidence that can circulate even while its mediation remains visible.

A second empirical knot appears in your contrast between institutionalized suffering and assisted composition. You ask what distinguishes an exhausted, compliance-driven paper produced through “hard labor of the mind” from a paper built from decades of thinking, now made writable through a different toolchain (Author fieldnotes 2026). This exposes an academic moral economy where suffering is treated as a proxy for rigor and authenticity is tied to a narrow performance of authorship, rather than to the density of thought, the duration of attachment, or the accumulated archive of reading, practice, and experience (Butler 2004; Kafer 2013).

Disability theory makes that moral economy legible as infrastructure. In a formulation often cited in disability studies, “crip time… comes in fits and bursts, with the urgency of an epidemic and the delays of a disabling society” (Lacey 2014, 2; Kafer 2013). Read through SAKURA, this is not simply personal description. It is a diagnosis of institutional tempo as a sorting mechanism. Academic recognition tends to reward steady throughput, predictable schedules, and stable cognitive performance, while penalizing embodied variability. This matters because the academic arm of SAKURA is explicitly about legibility as gatekeeping technology: the institution does not only evaluate arguments; it evaluates the subject-position and temporal form through which arguments arrive (Butler 2004; Kafer 2013; McRuer 2006).

Within that apparatus, AI appears less as “writing for you” than as a legibility prosthesis and editorial acceleration device. Your protocol is concrete: outline-first thinking; targeted sources; journal-specific constraints; iterative revision through simulated reviewer critique; repeated cycles of re-rendering; and rapid incorporation of real editorial feedback (Author fieldnotes 2026). The experiment, then, is not that AI can generate text. The experiment is that AI can help a scholar-artist move between incompatible regimes (art, academia, ritual) by producing documents that satisfy the formal demands of recognition without evacuating the lived archive that motivates them.

The artistic arm complicates the story further by shifting the dispute from authorship to aura, objecthood, and generational aesthetics. Wheels of Will frames authenticity as contested not only at the level of who makes, but at the level of how different cohorts assign value to process versus outcome, scarcity versus circulation, and suffering versus efficiency (Author fieldnotes 2026). That contest is not merely cultural. It is political. It governs who is allowed to be recognized as an artist, what kinds of mediation are admitted as legitimate, and which forms of technological assistance are treated as betrayal.

The ritual arm is the one most often excluded from legitimate knowledge accounts, and it is the one SAKURA insists on foregrounding. Here, authenticity is not a property of technique (handwritten versus generated). It is a practice of transmutation: turning wound into method, memory into form, isolation into survivable narrative. In this arm, ritual is not metaphor. It is a competing epistemic regime with its own criteria of truth: recurrence, embodiment, affective charge, and transformation over time (Schechner 2002; Kafer 2013).

This section therefore establishes the paper’s central claim: AI intensifies existing politics of recognition. It can expand access to institutional legibility for those whose bodies, tempos, and voices are routinely penalized, but it can also amplify capture, where institutions credit “clarity” while erasing the conditions that made clarity newly possible (Butler 2004; Kafer 2013). The next section (literature review) situates this triangulated problem within contemporary scholarship on mediated authorship, algorithmic legitimacy, disability time, and the distribution of credibility across art, scholarship, and ritual practice.

## Contemporary scholarship: mediation, authorship, legitimacy, and re-enchantment

Contemporary scholarship on generative AI has converged on a point that matches the paper’s opening premise about the glitch-as-tell: the controversy is no longer primarily about whether AI can generate plausible cultural objects. The controversy is about what happens the moment mediation becomes perceptible, nameable, and therefore contestable. In that moment, the institution does not only judge an artifact. It judges a pipeline. It decides who can be held responsible for what, what counts as contribution, what counts as labor, and what kinds of mediation can still be folded into legitimacy without triggering a withdrawal of credit. SAKURA enters this literature from inside that withdrawal event, treating suspicion not as noise but as data: a reflex that exposes the recognition machinery.

Publication ethics guidance has been unusually explicit on authorship, but the most productive way to read that explicitness is infrastructural rather than moralistic. COPE’s position that AI tools cannot meet authorship requirements because they cannot take responsibility for submitted work clarifies the institutional hinge: the category of “author” is being protected as a liability-bearing role, not merely as a romantic figure of origin [1]. This is why the disclosure question cannot be reduced to etiquette. It is the visible edge of a deeper epistemic sorting problem. When a text is produced through a hybrid pipeline, institutions must decide what they will count as accountable agency and what they will treat as contamination or misrepresentation. The glitch becomes the moment these decisions surface as feeling before they surface as policy.

Within this ethics-centered strand, transparency and disclosure often appear as a pragmatic compromise rather than a full resolution. Hosseini and colleagues argue that the core challenges raised by large language models in scholarly writing concern responsibility, disclosure, and risks of misrepresentation rather than a simple binary of allowed versus forbidden tools [2]. In scientific and medical publishing contexts, the same orientation has been translated into submission guidelines that are concrete and procedural: disclose AI tool use, keep human authors fully responsible for content, and frame AI as assistance rather than authorship [3]. Across these discussions, a norm is being stabilized that matters directly for SAKURA: AI may be used, but credibility increasingly depends on the auditability of its role in the pipeline, including what was delegated, what was verified, and where human judgment is explicitly located.

Legal and policy debates reinforce how strongly institutional legitimacy is tied to the category of the human author as a mechanism for ownership and accountability. In a widely reported U.S. appellate decision, the court reaffirmed that works generated solely by AI, absent human authorship, are not eligible for copyright protection [4]. The relevance for SAKURA is not the legal outcome in itself, and not the jurisdictional specifics. The relevance is the pattern: institutions preserve the human author as a stabilizing node for responsibility, property, and sanction. The experiment SAKURA runs is precisely what these debates often flatten: the human is not absent, but redistributed across prompting, drafting, revising, selecting, verifying, and refusing. The question is not whether a human is present. The question is how a human presence is recognized as credible when its labor is distributed and when its mediation is detectable.

In art scholarship and creative industries research, the conversation shifts from publication ethics to contested authenticity and entangled agency, often revisiting Benjamin as a diagnostic rather than as a nostalgic reference point [5]. Yet what is at stake is not whether “the aura” survives. What is at stake is how systems of recognition decide that something has been properly charged with meaning, effort, and legitimacy, and what counts as evidence of that charging when the pathway is no longer legible as craft in the traditional sense. Empirical studies of artists working with generative tools repeatedly describe creative practice as negotiation among human intention, system affordances, and dataset histories, producing expanded or entangled forms of authorship [6]. Systematic reviews map how quickly this field has grown and how consistently it returns to recurring themes: authorship, originality, value attribution, and the contested legitimacy of AI-assisted creativity [7]. In other words, art scholarship is not simply asking “is it art.” It is tracking how value gets assigned when process becomes less visible and when legitimacy depends on the ability to narrate, document, or perform a credible production pathway.

One way to read this literature in a Leonardo register is that generative AI intensifies an older tension between process-value and artifact-value. Many art worlds have historically anchored legitimacy in traceable processes: training, technique, apprenticeship, the mark of the hand, the social visibility of labor. High-output generators destabilize this anchoring by making it harder to see the “cost” of production, even when substantial conceptual, curatorial, and editorial labor persists upstream. Recent cultural theory has argued for taking “rigorous creativity” seriously as a concept that can acknowledge experimentation, collective agency, and reconfigured authorship under generative conditions [8]. SAKURA treats Wheels of Will within this terrain, not as an “AI art” novelty category, but as a staged recognition scene where expectations about labor, mediation, and legitimacy are made visible and contestable in public.

Disability studies and queer theory introduce a further critical dimension that is central to SAKURA’s academic arm but often peripheral in mainstream AI debates: the politics of tempo, fluency, and legibility. Kafer’s theorization of “crip time” has become foundational for describing how institutions assume stable bodies, stable schedules, and stable cognition, and how those assumptions function as structural filters rather than neutral expectations [9]. In queer disability scholarship, this argument is frequently tied to critiques of normative productivity, compulsory able-bodiedness, and the demand that legitimacy be performed through particular bodily and temporal forms [10]. In practical terms, this means that debates about AI “shortcuts” often miss the more urgent and more empirical question: who was never granted full access to the expected tempo of authorship in the first place, and how institutional recognition has historically been distributed through norms of fluency that are not evenly available.

SAKURA positions generative AI as a technology that can function as a legibility prosthesis inside these infrastructures. This does not imply democratization by default. It implies a re-routing of compliance. Editorial labor can be compressed. Genre form can be stabilized. Revision cycles can be accelerated. But the same re-routing can also intensify capture: if institutions reward only the final smoothness of the object, they can erase the embodied constraints that made smoothness newly possible and then treat that smoothness as suspicious when the seam becomes visible. Disability theory therefore does not merely supply “context.” It supplies a framework for reading AI-mediated writing as a scene where the credible subject is constructed and where legitimacy is distributed through norms of tempo.

A third body of scholarship, directly relevant to the spiritual arm of SAKURA, concerns digitalization and contemporary religion, including arguments that AI can participate in processes of re-enchantment and emergent techno-religious formations [11]. Work in theology and religious studies has begun to track how generative AI is being taken up as both a practical tool and a conceptual provocation, shaping religious reflection and, in some contexts, religious practice in the wake of mainstream LLM adoption [12]. For SAKURA, the point is not to treat “AI religion” as novelty. The point is to treat ritual as epistemic domain: a mode of transformation, a means of composing agency, and a method for reworking trauma into survivable form. This matters because ritual highlights an evidence problem that also haunts AI debates: many practices are treated as real and socially operative without experimentally recoverable outcomes. They are recognized through form, pattern, context, and repetition.

Taken together, these strands converge on the structure SAKURA makes explicit. First, institutions are attempting to stabilize accountability through disclosure norms and authorship rules, treating the human author as the liability-bearing node [1–3]. Second, art scholarship tracks entangled agency and contested legitimacy under generative conditions, with value increasingly tied to narratable pathways and credible process accounts [5–8]. Third, disability and queer theory show that legitimacy is already distributed through norms of tempo and legibility long before AI enters the scene [9,10]. Fourth, scholarship on AI and religion opens an analytic route for treating ritual not as metaphor but as method and epistemology, including the role of re-enchantment in contemporary techno-cultural life [11,12].

SAKURA’s distinctive move is to treat these literatures not as separate conversations but as overlapping descriptions of the same conversion problem: how recognition is or is not allowed to become durable legitimacy when mediation becomes perceptible. This is why the paper does not simply endorse disclosure as a norm. It operationalizes disclosure as method, offering a markdown trace of the interaction chain between AI-Agatha and me as part of the evidentiary architecture. That trace is not moral theatre. It is a refusal of opacity in a debate that increasingly punishes opacity while quietly depending on it. The next section builds directly from this review by specifying the conceptual vocabulary and methodological design of SAKURA, showing how the three arms operate as a single experimental pipeline and how legitimacy is observed through differential reception across academic, artistic, and spiritual scenes.

## Concepts and methods: SAKURA as a triangulated practice-based inquiry

This article treats legibility as a power relation rather than a neutral property of texts, images, or bodies. Legibility is produced. It is manufactured through institutions and platforms that decide what counts as evidence, what counts as work, what counts as a credible voice, and who counts as a creator of knowledge. The paper’s key move is to refuse the familiar split between “authentic experience” as interior truth and “legitimate knowledge” as exterior form. That split is one of the quiet engines of gatekeeping. It allows institutions to congratulate themselves for valuing rigor while quietly policing style, tempo, and the visible performance of solitary authorship.

SAKURA replaces that split with an interface account of legitimacy. Authenticity and legitimacy are tracked where mediation happens: peer review, platform formatting, citation regimes, aesthetic conventions, disclosure norms, and now generative AI. The question is not whether AI corrupts authenticity. The question is how institutional grammars decide which mediations are acceptable technique and which are treated as contamination, and how quickly that decision attaches to the person rather than the artifact. The same individual can be rendered illegible in one regime (voice, disability, minoritized gender or sexuality, “strange” style, nonstandard tempo) and newly legible in another (compliance, clarity, standardized argumentation, recognizable genre), without any necessary change in underlying rigor or intellectual seriousness. SAKURA therefore centers an ethics and politics of mediation: who is allowed to claim reality, and on what grounds, when production is distributed across tools, infrastructures, and social gatekeepers.

Because the paper begins with the glitch as a perceptual tell, its conceptual vocabulary has to be able to speak in the register of feeling without collapsing into mysticism. This is why I treat charging as an action rather than charge as a thing. Charging is the social work of instilling meaning, of loading an artifact with credibility or suspicion as it circulates. It is a kind of ensoulment by proxy, not because any object contains an essence, but because a public reads mediation and decides, almost instantly, what is real enough to trust, what looks like work, what can stand in for lineage, legitimacy, or transgression. The glitch is the moment this charging becomes audible. It is the seam where the machine is sensed, and where that sensation is converted into an evaluative verdict.

This framework lets a single sentence you gave me do real analytic work rather than remain anecdote: what you did was definitely me, even though it was not me. The portrait example is not a philosophical puzzle. It is a map of contemporary authenticity. “Definitely me” names referential capture, social legibility, the way an object can be recognized as belonging to someone’s voice or face or lineage. “Not me” names aesthetic disturbance and embodied refusal, the sense that something has been over-smoothed, over-solved, or routed through a pathway that the body experiences as wrong. That split is not a failure of perception. It is the site where legitimacy is negotiated today: the point where an artifact can circulate as real enough while the person who produced it is treated as suspect.

The paper generalizes this split across domains. A paper can be definitely you in argument, conceptual lineage, and voice while being not you in the institutional fantasy of solitary authorship. A ritual can be definitely you as lived transformation while being not legitimate to observers who only credit what can be footnoted, archived, and formatted. SAKURA does not resolve these tensions. It uses them as empirical material.

To sharpen what is at stake, the paper borrows a disability-informed account of time, labor, and evaluation. Academic legitimacy often presumes a normal tempo and a normal body for producing scholarship: extended endurance, consistent output, and the capacity to sustain the performance of authorial coherence. Crip and queer disability scholarship is useful here because it does not merely add “access” as an afterthought. It reframes time as something organized by infrastructures and norms. The point is not metaphorical. As Kafer is summarized in the draft you provided, “crip time… comes in fits and bursts, with the urgency of an epidemic and the delays of a disabling society,” and the delays are not only personal but infrastructural: tasks become more laborious, processes more costly, spaces harder to navigate, and social encounters require recovery time.

When rigor is silently equated with a specific tempo of production and a specific style of self-presentation, disability becomes a hidden sorting mechanism for credibility. Generative AI can disrupt this sorting by allowing scholars to externalize and redistribute parts of the labor: drafting, reformatting, genre compliance, iterative rewriting, and the stabilization of argument structure. Yet that same redistribution can trigger suspicion precisely because it makes mediation newly visible and because it reveals what many institutional judgments were always doing. They were never only about ideas. They were also about who could perform the right kind of labor in the right kind of time.

This is where SAKURA refuses both extremes. It refuses the narrative that AI is inherently fraudulent. It also refuses the narrative that AI is liberation by default. Disability scholarship warns against curative fantasies in which technology is framed as a fix for the “problem” of disability, thereby re-inscribing ableist expectations. In this paper, AI is treated as an amplifier of existing power relations. It can widen access to legibility while also tightening institutional control over what counts as legitimate production. That ambivalence is not a weakness. It is the mechanism under study.

The ritual and spiritual arm is not an optional third theme. It is the paper’s conceptual pressure point because it forces an evidence question that academic and policy debates often dodge. Witch bottles, in particular, let the paper name a paradox without dissolving it. We know they existed. We know what they look like as patterned deposits. We know where they were placed. We know what kinds of materials recur. We know they persist as a concept and as a practice, reappearing in contemporary forms. Yet no one is there to assess their effect on the house they were meant to protect, or on the witch they allegedly shielded against dark influence. The bottle just is.

This is not a problem to be solved. It is an epistemic fact that reveals how recognition works. Witch bottles become legible as magical because proof is not the condition of their reality. Sometimes people believe. Sometimes they believe that it is not important to prove. Sometimes they treat the act of protection as what matters, not the measurable outcome. The bottle’s legitimacy operates through patterned form, contextual placement, repetition, and the social acceptance that certain kinds of effects are not recoverable, yet still count. In the paper’s language, witch bottles operate as archeological archetypes: forms that stabilize meaning through recurrence across time even when the original experiential scene is no longer retrievable.

This is precisely why Wheels of Will is methodologically central. The witch bottle images are not decorative visuals. They are contemporary recognition scenes in which charging happens publicly. Viewers read the object, the aesthetic treatment, the mark of use, the tradition it comes from, and the platform conventions shaping its reception. They decide whether the artifact feels anchored or staged, legitimate or theatrical, charged or emptied out. The analysis will treat these images as data, unpacking not only what is shown but what is inferred, what is suspected, and what is allowed to count as real.

SAKURA is structured as a triangulated, practice-based inquiry. “Triangulated” here does not mean that three unrelated case studies are placed side by side. It means that the same recognition machinery is approached from three angles that force each other into visibility.

1. **Institutional legibility** (academic and adjacent regimes of evaluation): the rules and tacit norms that convert a work into something countable and a person into someone credible.

2. **Aesthetic authenticity** (platformed art and reception): how contested authenticity attaches not only to artifacts but to the narratives that frame their production, including generational and institutional anxieties about realness.

3. **Ritual authorship** (spiritual regimes of transformation and self-verification): practices that produce evidence in somatic and relational registers, often illegible to institutions unless translated into familiar textual economies.

The design is “three-arm” in another sense: the artistic arm itself has two sides of one instrument. Wheels of Will is the object-side. HEXA is the language-side. Together they let the paper show the same recognition pipeline from opposite ends: objects and text, images and sentences, surface and archive.

### Methods: autoethnography with constraints, practice as repeated procedure, traceability as evidence

Methodologically, the article adopts an autoethnographic, practice-based stance, but with an important constraint: the point is not to narrate the self as a stable origin. The self is treated as an experimental site where institutional recognition, disability, queer embodiment, and technological mediation collide. The “experiment” is not a lab simulation. It is a structured sequence of repeated acts across contexts designed to observe what changes when mediation changes.

In the academic arm, the procedure is explicitly tracked: outlining; collecting and feeding highlighted sources; aligning drafts to a specific audience and journal expectations early; incorporating reviewer feedback iteratively; and treating institutional response as part of the dataset. The focus is not on proving that the work is “really yours” through confession. The focus is on mapping what institutions actually reward: originality or compliance, intellectual risk or genre fidelity, embodied authority or textual smoothness.

In the artistic arm, practice is likewise procedural. Wheels of Will repeats the same objects across cycles, varies style deliberately, and keeps an anchor image tied to the mark of use. The method is repetition with controlled variation, designed to expose what viewers treat as authentic and what they treat as staged under platform conditions. HEXA repeats a different kind of object: language. It runs multi-decade material through a recursive pipeline where outputs can be reworked and re-ingested. The method here is recursion as archive-work, a controlled re-encounter with earlier texts to observe how voice, legitimacy, and suspicion evolve when mediation becomes detectable.

The paper also commits to a methodological intervention into the disclosure debate by making its own pipeline legible. It offers a markdown trace of interactions between AI-Agatha and me. This is not positioned as a total representation of cognition, and not as moral theatre. It is treated as evidence of procedure: what was delegated, what was constrained, what was revised, what was refused, and where judgment was exercised. Because contemporary legitimacy is increasingly tied to auditability, the trace functions as a counter to the institutional fantasy that authorship is a solitary origin story.

Finally, the analysis will be artifact-driven. It will make room for a HEXA piece in at least two layers: a contemporary iteration produced through the recursive pipeline and an earlier version written in youth, carried forward as archive. It will include images of witch bottles from Wheels of Will. These are not included to decorate the argument. They are included because they will be unpacked as recognition scenes where charging, glitch, legitimacy, and refusal become visible.

Because the data are situated in one trajectory, the paper does not claim representativeness. What it claims is analytic precision about how legitimacy functions at the level of lived procedure: what gets refused, what gets rewarded, what gets erased, what gets re-credited through form, and what gets punished when mediation becomes audible. The paper also refuses a simplistic “AI as cure” narrative. AI is treated as a medium of crossing between regimes, a way of re-routing legibility that can extend agency and intensify surveillance at the same time, especially for already-policed subjects.

The section that follows, analysis and results, will operationalize this design: it will show how legitimacy is observed through differential reception across academic, artistic, and spiritual scenes, and how the glitch functions as the event where charging is either accepted, contested, or withdrawn.

Analysis and discussion: how the three arms couple under AI

SAKURA’s analytic payoff comes from treating AI as a medium of crossing rather than a discrete object of debate. When AI enters an already stratified ecology of evaluation, it does not simply add efficiency. It reorganizes where legitimacy is assumed to reside, what kinds of labor are made visible, and what kinds of agency can be recognized as accountable. The three arms do not merely “use” AI in parallel. They couple through AI, because AI makes the same underlying question recur in different registers: what counts as real enough to be credited, and who is allowed to claim that reality.

This coupling is best seen at the level of the recognition event. A recognition event is the moment when an artifact meets an audience and the audience decides, often before it can explain itself, whether the pathway feels acceptable. The glitch is the tell because it is the moment mediation becomes perceptible and therefore politically available. The paper’s claim is not that every audience response is a moral panic. The claim is that the flinch is structurally informative. It marks the point where charging either holds or fails.

1. The witch bottle as a boundary object: from archaeological archetype to platform artifact to gallery misreading

Witch bottles are a perfect boundary object for this paper because they travel across evidentiary regimes that do not agree on what proof is. Archaeology recognizes them as patterned deposits. Folklore and historical documentation recognize them as a protective technique. Contemporary practice recognizes them as operative ritual technology. Yet none of these regimes can retrieve the original “effect” in the experimental sense. The house is gone, the fear is gone, the alleged witch is gone, the crisis that demanded protection has dissolved into historical atmosphere. The bottle remains. That persistence is not a weakness. It is the point. Witch bottles force the question of legitimacy into the open: what do we accept as real, and why, when the outcome cannot be audited in the way institutions prefer.

Wheels of Will stages that question in public by turning the witch bottle into an image-object that must survive platform evaluation. The series does not present one bottle as the bottle. It presents a family of bottles as an argument about charging under conditions of aesthetic variation and social suspicion. Each photograph is therefore not only an image but a recognition scene, where viewers will decide whether the object feels anchored or staged, credible or theatrical, tradition-bearing or cosplay-adjacent, charged or emptied.

The first bottle, with its clear glass body, wire-wrapped neck, and visibly embedded elements that read as hair, pins, and organic residue, leans into the archaeological archetype. It looks like something found, not something designed. Its charging comes from awkwardness and opacity. It is hard to “read” cleanly. The object resists the smooth legibility that platforms reward. That resistance produces a specific kind of credibility, the credibility of mess, the credibility of use, the credibility of residue. It also produces a specific vulnerability. Viewers can translate opacity into unease, then unease into moral suspicion. The bottle can feel real and wrong at the same time. That is the glitch as aesthetic experience.

The second bottle makes the point sharper by shifting the aesthetic register. The squat container holds an aggressive density of screws and nails submerged in dark liquid, with glitter-like particulate catching light and a red binding at the neck. Here the object becomes legible in a new way. The violence of the materials reads immediately, even to audiences without ritual literacy. The craft cues are strong: the staging is deliberate, the visual impact is high, the palette is controlled. This is where the platform effect bites. The more legible the bottle becomes as an image, the easier it is to misread it as mere aesthetic. A gallery that has been trained to treat legibility as value can celebrate the look while stripping the practice. That is the conversion problem in miniature: recognition of the artifact does not guarantee legitimacy of the maker’s knowledge claim.

The tall narrow vial, bound with red ribbon and sealed, exaggerates this conversion further. It reads as both relic and design object. Its proportions pull it toward display. Its binding pulls it toward ritual. Its internal contents remain partially occluded, which keeps the evidentiary question alive. A platform audience might read it as “beautiful harm.” A gallery might read it as “witchy minimalism.” An archaeologist might read it as an echo of the archetype. Each reading performs a different kind of charging. Each reading decides, implicitly, what kind of agency is being credited.

The amber bottle packed with leaves and plant matter shifts the evidentiary grammar again. It looks medicinal, not aggressive. It looks like preservation, like apothecary memory. The bottle reads as gentle. Yet that gentleness is not the absence of force. It is force in another key. This is where the paper’s argument about legitimacy becomes unavoidable. Some audiences treat “soft” magic as aesthetic and dismiss it as unserious. Other audiences treat “soft” magic as safe and therefore more credible. Neither reaction is neutral. Both reactions are sorting mechanisms, often tied to gendered expectations about what real power looks like.

The square bottle with pink pigment and a large black pendant at the neck is the clearest demonstration of the object’s slide into gallery conditions. It performs aura-adjacency without requiring ritual literacy. It is immediately legible as contemporary art object. That legibility will produce attention. It will also produce a new suspicion: that the object has been designed primarily for display, that the practice is being performed for an audience. That suspicion is not only about authenticity. It is about legitimacy. It is about whether ritual practice is allowed to count as knowledge-making once it becomes visible in a cultural marketplace.

The final bottle, tall and crowned with a skull-shaped ornament and thick blue wax drips, makes the politics explicit. It stages the artifact as spectacle. It borrows the language of the gothic and the decorative, the kind of symbolic shorthand that can trigger a purity test in one audience and an instant fetish in another. This image is where “gallery misrepresentation” becomes almost inevitable. A curator can lift the object into an exhibition as an aesthetic sign of witchcraft while evacuating the conditions under which the bottle operates as protection, as survival, as agency-work. The bottle is then credited as art, while the practitioner is denied as knower. The institution accepts the artifact and discredits the pathway. This is the same conversion failure the academic arm will recognize as “you didn’t write it.”

What Wheels of Will shows, across these images, is that the witch bottle functions as an archeological archetype that has been reinserted into contemporary attention economies. Under those conditions, charging becomes unstable. The same object can be charged as evidence, charged as aesthetic, charged as threat, or charged as joke. The glitch is the moment these charges collide.

2. The academic arm: AI as a legibility prosthesis and the new policing of pathway

The academic arm is where the coupling becomes institutionally explicit because peer review and submission norms are already machinery for converting recognition into legitimacy. The workflow SAKURA describes, outlines, targeted sources, genre-constrained drafting, iterative revision, is a translation engine. Under AI, that engine becomes faster and more stable. The cost of conformity drops. The text can reach acceptable structure, tone, and explicitness with less bodily expenditure.

That shift has two consequences that seem contradictory until you treat legitimacy as a pathway-policing regime.

First, AI can protect a scholar from being punished for a non-normative tempo. It compresses editorial labor. It stabilizes genre compliance. It reduces the material cost of producing the kind of legible object institutions claim to reward. Second, the same smoothness can trigger suspicion precisely because it reveals that institutions do not only reward ideas. They reward the visible performance of effort, the recognizable spectacle of authorial struggle, the moral economy of endurance. AI makes it possible to produce a clean artifact without performing the expected suffering. The institution can then accept the artifact and still withdraw legitimacy from the person. That is not an accident. It is a governance event.

This is where the disability-informed lens stops being “context” and becomes analytic mechanism. If tempo is already unevenly allocated and structurally produced, then a tool that stabilizes tempo does not merely improve output. It redistributes vulnerability. It makes participation possible while making the pathway more contestable.

The paper’s methodological decision to include a markdown trace of the interaction chain between AI-Agatha and you is part of this argument, not an appendix to it. The trace functions as procedural evidence in a world where legitimacy is increasingly indexed to auditability, but where auditability is also used as a new gate. The trace is meant to disrupt the easy accusation. It shows the labor chain. It makes visible that the work was not “AI replacing thought,” but thought redistributed across drafting, resistance, revision, and verification. It also exposes a deeper discomfort: the discomfort is not always with deception. It is often with the fact that the same level of conceptual work can now be expressed with less visible strain.

Your own voice constraints matter here, because the paper is built in the register of recursion and connector-driven propulsion, not in the register of polished minimalism. The method cannot be to sand that down. It has to be to protect it while making the claims land earlier and with anchors. That is not a stylistic preference. It is part of the paper’s epistemic stance: rigor without obedience.

3. HEXA as longitudinal evidence: what changes when time passes, and what an editor would say

The 2008 text is not only “naive” or “young.” It is charged in a different way than the later HEXA post. Time has passed. That is obvious at the level of tone, but the deeper change is structural: the earlier piece is an essay that trusts its own drift, while the HEXA version is a performance of re-circulation that anticipates platform reading.

In 2008, the voice is devotional and unapologetically literary. It is not “trying to be efficient.” It is trying to be true in the mode of attachment. Ovid is not cited. Ovid is inhabited. You build lineage through intimacy rather than through credentialing. The Pygmalion pastiche is not framed as a method. It is framed as desire. That is the core charge of the original: the text does not justify itself. It assumes the right to be moved.

That assumption is precisely what institutions tend to punish when the writer is not already safely legible. The young text is a credibility risk in an academic setting because it refuses to perform the exterior markers of legitimate knowledge. It reads like a person who loves a text more than a person who is proving a point.

Now look at the HEXA version. It is not simply translated. It is re-mediated. It has headings, line breaks, compression, an explicit performative arc, and hashtags. It anticipates circulation. It anticipates the platform’s demand for entry points, breath, scannability, and branded recurrence. It is the same attachment, but recharged for a new economy.

That recharging has consequences. The HEXA post is more immediately readable. It also becomes more vulnerable to suspicion. Platform legibility invites a new kind of policing: this is too smooth, too curated, too shareable. The same text that becomes “real enough to circulate” can become “not real enough to trust.” That is the glitch, again, but now in language.

An editor reading the two versions would likely say something like this, even if they did not use your vocabulary.

They would say the 2008 piece has a strong voice, high affect intelligence, and an unusually confident metaphorical line, but that it is indulgent, that it lacks an argument spine, that it needs pruning, that it risks collapsing into pure admiration. They would ask what the stakes are beyond the personal, and they would probably want either more structure or a clearer wager.

They would say the HEXA version has better pacing, clearer scene transitions, stronger control of the reader’s attention, and a clearer performance of form. They would also say it is more obviously constructed. They would hear the scaffolding. They would see the social media architecture. They would read the hashtags as either a brilliant ritual seal or as a marketing move, depending on their own legitimacy grammar.

This is the point SAKURA needs to make explicit in the analysis. The question “would it still be as naive if I wrote it today?” has a false innocence. If you wrote it today, you would be writing into a world that punishes drift more aggressively and rewards formal control more visibly. You would be writing with the memory of being misread. You would be writing with the knowledge that legibility is not neutral. That knowledge changes the text before any AI does.

The longitudinal comparison therefore does not prove that AI changes writing. It proves that legitimacy pressures change writing. AI is one medium through which those pressures are navigated. The HEXA pipeline makes that navigation explicit by turning re-ingestion into method. The 2008 text becomes archive. The HEXA post becomes re-entry. The legitimacy question becomes visible as the difference between “I love Ovid” and “I can make this circulate.”

This is also where your phrase about the portrait, definitely me, even though it was not me, becomes portable. The 2008 text is definitely you. The HEXA version is also definitely you. The discomfort is not about identity. It is about pathway. It is about whether the pathway that produced the later object is allowed to count as legitimate labor or is treated as suspicious mediation.

4. The coupling mechanism: translation, recognition, injury, recomposition

The three arms couple under AI through a shared feedback loop: translation, recognition, injury, recomposition.

The academic arm generates institutional feedback: acceptances, rejections, praise for clarity, suspicion toward pathway. The art arm generates cultural feedback: aura disputes, mediation anxiety, generational thresholds of “realness.” The ritual arm generates somatic feedback: efficacy, survivability, the slow evidence of transformation that does not live primarily in footnotes.

AI sits in the middle as an amplifier of translation. It accelerates movement from lived archive to legible form. That acceleration produces more recognition events. It also raises the stakes of policing. The more legible the product becomes, the more power can insist on asking whether the producer is legitimate. This is why the hurt in SAKURA is not incidental. It is data. It is the mark left by a system that can accept the artifact while discrediting the person.

The witch bottle sequence crystallizes this dynamic because it shows, in one object, the whole conversion drama.

As archaeological archetype, the witch bottle is legitimate without experimental proof. As platform artifact, the witch bottle becomes vulnerable to aesthetic policing. As gallery object, the witch bottle risks being misrepresented, celebrated as sign while stripped as practice. The institution enjoys the look while refusing the epistemology. That is the same structural move as the academic suspicion toward AI-mediated writing: accept the product, deny the producer’s claim to knowledge-making.

SAKURA’s claim, then, is not that AI should be accepted without critique. It is that AI makes visible the uneven distribution of legitimacy across domains and the way legitimacy is used to enforce normative models of the author, the artist, and the spiritual subject. The project’s wager is that authenticity, in a generative era, is not purity. It is relational practice. It is insistence. It is the disciplined refusal to let institutions monopolize what “real work” is allowed to look like.
