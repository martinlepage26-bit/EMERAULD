Many are skeptical of the idea of artificial consciousness being possible even in principle. But a number of philosophers and their researchers are either optimistic or worried that consciousness will be or already has been created artificially. We'll take a look at some philosophical theories of consciousness and their respective stance on artificial consciousness. Theories of consciousness can be divided into two main categories. Dualism and Monism. Dualist theories say that there is some fundamental difference between the physical and the mental, so that either can't be reduced to the other. 
Dualist theories can be either substance dualist or property dualist. Substance dualism claims that the mental, including consciousness is a special kind of substance like a soul. This view doesn't give much room for artificial consciousness, as we are unlikely to find a way to manufacture immaterial souls. Property dualists say that the mental somehow emerges from the physical as a different kind of property, just like waves are an emergent property of water and not strictly the same as water. If consciousness can emerge from brains, in principle, it could emerge from silicon chips as well. So the prospect of artificial consciousness is quite good. We only need to pin down the exact mechanisms for consciousness to emerge from matter. 
One problem for dualism is that it's hard to describe causality to consciousness, so if artificial consciousness appeared, it would likely be a byproduct of serving no function. The monist theories in turn claim that there's only one fundamental kind of thing in the world. Which is either mental as an idealism or material as in physicalism. Idealism states that consciousness is the only thing that exists. The universe is consciousness. So in idealism, the idea of creating consciousness is moved as everything is consciousness. Rather, the issue would be whether some specific aspect of cognition or experience could be created artificially, such as feeling pain or having a sense of self. 
In science today, the physicalist stands dominates. This is because it's difficult for dualism to explain how consciousness would emerge from the physical world and idealism is often seen as too weird to be taken seriously. Physicalism presumes that there is a tangible physical reality which obeys physical laws and the scientific quest is to explain consciousness within that framework. There're different takes on the possibility of artificial consciousness among physicalists. Some believe that in order to realize artificial consciousness, we would need to make an exact replication of the physical processes that make up biological consciousness. So we would have to build hardware with artificial neurons connected through artificial synapses. But for other physicalists like eliminatorist or behaviorists, consciousness is not really an issue. 
It's seen as a misconception or something that shouldn't be addressed by science. A quite different approach to all the previous ones is functionalism, which is compatible with many of the other theories. Its central claim is that a specific experience can come about in many different ways. For example, several different activity patterns in my brain can correspond to the subjective experience of feeling tired and both me and a dog can have a functional equivalent experience of being happy even though our brains and bodies are different. This is called multiple realize ability and as a consequence, functional is is very much in favor of artificially created consciousness according to functional is an artificial system that exerts the same functions as a biological system can be assumed to have the same experiences. A famous argument against functionalism and also against the turing test and the very idea of artificial consciousness is the Chinese room argument. 
This argument builds on a thought experiment where a non-chinese speaker is put in a room containing piles of paper with Chinese symbols and books with instructions. The person receives additional chinese symbols through a slot in the world and their job is to follow the instructions to output other chinese symbols through the slot. The point of the thought experiment is that given good enough instructions, it would look to an outside observer as if the person in the room understood chinese and responded to requests consciously. The entire room is conscious.
