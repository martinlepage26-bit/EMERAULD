AI is not the moral agent. People are.

The people building it. Deploying it. Procuring it. Regulating it. Using it.

And right now, they are not being held accountable enough.

Three risks stood out to me because they still do not get enough attention in mainstream AI governance.

First, cognitive atrophy. As we delegate more thinking to AI, we may lose the skills needed to challenge its outputs. That matters because scrutiny is not automatic. It has to be practised.

Second, epistemic decay. Our ability to distinguish real content from synthetic content is weakening. The consequences are already visible in the spread of AI-generated misinformation, while platforms and regulators struggle to keep up.

Third, representation and access. The global south and other underrepresented groups risk being left behind twice: first through gaps in training data, and again through unequal access to the benefits. Healthcare AI makes this especially stark. Models fail people they were never properly trained or validated to serve.

These are not isolated risks.

They are systemic. They compound. And voluntary self-governance has not been enough.

That is why one audience response stayed with me.

When asked whether frontier AI models should face something like pharmaceutical licensing, 80% agreed.

That is not a fringe position.

It is a sign that people are ready to talk seriously about enforceable accountability.
