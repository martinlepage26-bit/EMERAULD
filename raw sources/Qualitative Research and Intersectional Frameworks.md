## 11. Inductive Analysis, Writing, and the Construction of Meaning

One of the clearest threads in the reference list is the francophone qualitative-methods tradition associated with Pierre Paillé and Alex Mucchielli. Their work is important because it resists the simplification of qualitative inquiry into mere coding technique. In this tradition, analysis is an interpretive labor that develops through repeated engagement with material, conceptual refinement, and writing. Paillé and Mucchielli's *L'analyse qualitative en sciences humaines et sociales* appears in the list as a core anchor for this approach, and it can be read alongside Mucchielli's constructivist account of qualitative methods and Paillé's later reflections on the conditions of qualitative analysis and the use of software (Mucchielli, 2005; Paillé and Mucchielli, 2003; Paillé, 2011).

This matters for handbook design because it clarifies a common confusion: software can help store, sort, retrieve, and compare excerpts, but it cannot replace analytic judgment. Qualitative knowledge emerges when the researcher moves between excerpts and concepts, between raw material and interpretive language. Blais and Martineau's discussion of *l'analyse inductive générale* sharpens this point by describing a structured but flexible way of deriving meaning from raw data without prematurely locking the material into rigid categories (Blais and Martineau, 2006). Together, these works support a practical rule: code provisionally, memo constantly, compare relentlessly, and let writing do part of the analytic work.

For a 2026 researcher, this means that analytic rigor comes from transparency of process rather than from pretending neutrality. Keep a trail of analytic decisions. Record when a code became a concept, when a pattern turned into a theme, and when a contradiction forced a category to split or be renamed. Treat writing not as the final polishing stage, but as a site where interpretation becomes accountable.

## 12. Life Stories, Narrative Reconstruction, and Sociological Saturation

The strongest methodological cluster in the source list concerns life-story and narrative inquiry. Daniel Bertaux is central here with *Le recit de vie: l'enquete et ses methodes* (Bertaux, 2010). His contribution is especially useful because he distinguishes between collecting a narrated life and reconstructing a social trajectory. A narrated life gives access to self-understanding, identity work, memory, and sequence. A reconstructed life-history widens the lens to include archives, institutional traces, and third-party accounts. This distinction helps researchers decide whether they are studying narration itself or the wider social world through which a life moved.

Several entries in the list help deepen this area. Barrick's and Burrick's work on an epistemology of life stories, Galligani on moving from interview to life-story, and Brun on the place of life-story in the social sciences all point toward the same lesson: narrative is never just content. It is form, ordering, justification, omission, and positioning (Barrick, 2010; Burrick, 2010; Galligani, 2000; Brun, 2003). A participant does not simply "tell what happened." They organize events into a plot, choose scenes of suffering or triumph, establish causality, and present a self that is legible to the listener.

This is why the handbook should warn against flattening stories into themes too quickly. If the material is biographical, sequence matters. Turning points matter. Temporal gaps matter. Repeated metaphors matter. Contradictions between archive and narration matter. Bertaux's notion of sociological saturation is especially helpful here: the sample becomes sufficient when new interviews no longer yield new structural features of the milieu under study. Saturation, then, is not achieved by counting cases mechanically. It is reached when the analytic structure stabilizes.

In applied terms, narrative and life-story methods are strongest when the research question concerns trajectories, conversion, coming out, migration, illness, vocation, political awakening, or ritual initiation. They are also particularly useful when the researcher needs to connect agency and constraint: how people act within social structures, and how they narrate that action afterward.

## 13. Ethnography, Participation, and the Discipline of Field Presence

The reference list also strongly supports ethnographic work. Beaud and Weber's *Guide de l'enquete de terrain* remains a practical anchor for how to produce and analyze ethnographic material in the field (Beaud and Weber, 2003). Bastien's article on the distinction between participant observation and observing participation is especially useful because it reminds us that participation is not a simple switch one turns on. It is a methodological stance that must be justified, calibrated, and reflected upon (Bastien, 2007).

Ethnographic material is rarely neat. Fieldnotes contain fragments, overheard talk, embodied impressions, spatial arrangements, and shifting relations of trust. This is why ethnography requires a specific discipline of description. Hastrup's work on moving between experience and theory helps frame the ethnographic problem well: the researcher must be present enough to grasp the practical logic of a setting without dissolving entirely into it (Hastrup, 1995). Favret-Saada's classic work on witchcraft in the bocage pushes this even further. Her lesson is not only that belief systems must be taken seriously on their own terms, but that researchers are sometimes drawn into the force fields of the worlds they study (Favret-Saada, 1977). Distance and involvement are therefore not opposites; they are a tension to be managed.

In handbook form, the implication is simple. If the material consists of fieldnotes, ritual observation, embodied participation, or community events, the method should not default to thematic analysis alone. It should preserve the situational density of scenes. Ask what happened, who was present, what the sequence of acts was, what objects mattered, what the atmosphere was, what was said, what was withheld, and what the event required from bodies in space. Ethnographic analysis begins with the thickness of the encounter.

## 14. Language, Discourse, Performativity, and Social Order

Another major thread in the bibliography concerns language as action. Austin's *How to Do Things with Words* matters here because it shows that speech is not merely descriptive; it does things. It promises, authorizes, excludes, injures, names, and transforms (Austin, 1962). Bourdieu extends this insight by showing that speech acts draw force from social position and symbolic power. Not everyone can say the same words with the same consequences (Bourdieu, 1982).

Foucault deepens this further by shifting attention from isolated utterances to the historical organization of discourse. In *Les mots et les choses*, *Surveiller et punir*, and *Histoire de la sexualite*, discourse becomes a way of producing subjects, categories, and regimes of truth (Foucault, 1966; 1975; 1976). Butler's works on sex, gender, and discursive limits build directly on this inheritance by showing that identity is not simply expressed through language but constituted through repeated norms and performances (Butler, 1986; 1993; 2005). Baril, Allard-Poesi and Huault, and De Lauretis help situate these debates in feminist and queer theory in ways especially useful for French-language readers (Baril, 2007; Allard-Poesi and Huault, 2012; De Lauretis, 2007).

For qualitative research, the practical lesson is that discourse analysis is not just about vocabulary counts or rhetorical ornament. It asks how categories become authoritative, how norms appear natural, how identities are stabilized or contested, and how institutions define the sayable. Goffman's work on presentation of self can be read alongside this tradition as a micro-sociology of interaction: people do not only speak within discourse, they stage themselves before others, manage impressions, and negotiate face in concrete encounters (Goffman, 1959; 2004).

This section is especially important when the material consists of interviews about identity, institutional texts, media coverage, ritual language, or self-descriptions shaped by norms of gender, sexuality, respectability, deviance, or expertise. In such cases, the right question is not only what participants mean, but what discursive conditions make their meanings possible.

## 15. Intersectionality, Queer Theory, and the Critique of Single-Axis Analysis

The master reference list contains a rich set of intersectional and queer sources that can substantially strengthen the handbook. Crenshaw's *Cartographie des marges* remains decisive because it shows why single-axis frameworks fail to capture how race and gender interact in lived experience and institutional harm (Crenshaw, 2005). Anthias adds a sociological account of social stratification and identity, while Bilge both elaborates feminist theorizations of intersectionality and warns against versions of the concept that strip it of political force (Anthias, 2005; Bilge, 2009; 2013). Dorlin's work on sex, race, class, and feminist theory adds a francophone vocabulary for understanding domination as co-constituted rather than additive (Dorlin, 2008; 2009).

Queer theory in the reference list is equally useful. Butler, De Lauretis, Lamoureux, Eribon, and Baril provide tools for thinking beyond fixed identities and toward normativity, performativity, embodiment, and social intelligibility. These works are not simply "about LGBTQ topics." They are methodological tools for asking how categories are made, how bodies are regulated, how recognition operates, and how deviance or legitimacy are assigned. In that sense, queer theory belongs in a methods handbook not only as substantive content, but as an analytic sensibility.

A strong qualitative project therefore should not treat intersectionality as a decorative checklist. It should ask whether the material itself reveals overlapping regimes of power. Whose voice is heard as credible? Which bodies are read as safe, risky, sacred, deviant, healthy, productive, or authentic? Which forms of suffering are made visible and which are privatized? A study of religion, ritual, sexuality, disability, or community cannot answer such questions adequately with one axis of analysis alone.

## 16. Ritual, Embodiment, and Contemporary Spiritual Field Sites

The most distinctive strength of the source list is its concentration of works on ritual, contemporary spirituality, Paganism, Wicca, queer religiosity, and embodied forms of meaning-making. This cluster provides an excellent case study for how qualitative methods meet complex field material. Bell's work on ritual theory and ritual practice remains foundational because it shows that ritual should not be reduced to symbol alone; it is a strategic way of ordering bodies, space, time, and legitimacy (Bell, 1992). Houseman's studies of contemporary ritual and his essays on ritual deepen this by focusing on how ritual relations are composed and enacted rather than merely believed (Houseman, 2010; 2012).

Harvey, Greenwood, Pike, Fedele and Knibbe, and related authors show how ethnography can be used to study contemporary spirituality without collapsing it into either irrational belief or private identity (Harvey, 1997; Greenwood, 2000; Pike, 2001; Fedele and Knibbe, 2013). These works are methodologically instructive because they require the researcher to attend simultaneously to discourse, embodiment, aesthetics, kinship, gender, and experience. Ritual is never just said; it is staged, felt, inhabited, repeated, and negotiated.

The queer spirituality and neo-pagan references in the list add another dimension. Lepage and Gauthier's work on queer negotiations among neo-pagans in Montreal, Lepage's writings on authenticity and queer religiosity, and related work on rituality and gender performativity show how spirituality can become a site where identities are not simply expressed but actively reconfigured (Lepage, 2015; 2016; 2018; 2019). Browne, Munt, and Yip's *Queer Spiritual Spaces* similarly helps frame sacred space as contested, sexualized, and politically organized rather than neutral.

For the handbook, this section offers a wider methodological lesson. Ritual, spirituality, and embodied communal practices are ideal sites for mixed qualitative design. They often require participant observation, fieldnotes, discourse analysis, narrative interviewing, and intersectional analysis all at once. If the material includes ceremonies, altars, dress, gestures, songs, gendered language, or stories of transformation, then the method must remain open to symbol, performance, bodily discipline, and social negotiation together.

## 17. A Material-First Research Workflow Based on This Bibliography

Using the works above, a material-first workflow becomes easier to define.

If the material is interview-based:
Use thematic analysis for recurring patterns, narrative inquiry for trajectories, or life-story methods for social reconstruction.

If the material is field-based:
Use ethnographic description first. Do not strip scenes down to codes before preserving sequence, atmosphere, setting, and embodied interaction.

If the material is textual:
Use discourse analysis, critical discourse analysis, or performativity-oriented reading informed by Austin, Bourdieu, Foucault, and Butler.

If the material is ritual or spiritual:
Combine ethnographic observation with symbolic, performative, and interactional analysis. Bell, Houseman, Harvey, Greenwood, and Pike are especially useful here.

If the material concerns identity, marginalization, or normativity:
Use intersectional and queer frameworks not as add-ons, but as analytic structuring principles.

If the project crosses several of these materials:
Name the primary material first, then show how secondary materials extend or test the interpretation.

## Closing Note

What this bibliography makes especially clear is that qualitative research is strongest when it is materially precise and theoretically layered. The method should emerge from the kind of material in hand, but the interpretation should never stop at description. Life stories require attention to sequence and social structure. Ethnography requires presence, reflexivity, and thick scenes. Discourse work requires attention to power and performativity. Ritual and spirituality require embodied and symbolic analysis. Intersectional and queer frameworks require that no category of experience be treated as socially pure or analytically sufficient on its own.

That is the larger lesson of this expanded handbook: qualitative rigor comes from fitting method to material, then fitting interpretation to power.
