Misfit as Method: Ten Diagnostic Scenes of Institutional Failure
Abstract
This article challenges traditional understandings of failure in organizational life by reframing it as a cumulative diagnostic process rather than an outcome of deficient performance. Using a scene-based autoethnography of a 90-day probationary period in a large corporate setting marked by post-restructuring strain, the study analyzes ten field-note scenes as qualitative data that reveal how misfit is produced, stabilized, and finalized through everyday interactions. The paper offers a methodological argument: short, temporally sequenced scenes can function as diagnostic units, revealing organizational power in action, especially where evaluation is indirect, deniable, and dispersed. The analysis shows how assessment operates through ambient mechanisms that evade formal documentation: role opacity, procedural training without rationale, demands for autonomy under managerial absence, and relational withdrawal in place of feedback. These mechanisms constitute what the paper terms legibility governance, where institutions under conditions of saturation and reputational risk prioritize subjects who are quickly readable, affectively contained, and capable of absorbing ambiguity without naming it. When workers seek conceptual clarity, disclose difference pedagogically, or test managerial efficiency discourse literally, these actions redistribute interpretive authority and are often read as disruptions rather than contributions. Theoretically, the article builds on queer phenomenology and critical organizational scholarship to treat institutions as orienting environments that shape whose bodies, affects, and communicative styles are deemed competent. Substantively, the findings contribute to qualitative debates on power by specifying how “fit” is constructed through micro-sanctions, deferred clarity, and procedural mediation, allowing organizations to convert structural incoherence into individualized outcomes. The article demonstrates how scene-based qualitative analysis can expose the micromechanics of evaluative silence, offering a rigorous framework for studying diagnostic sorting in contemporary organizational governance.
Keywords: Diagnostic Sorting; Legibility Governance; Affective Regulation; Institutional Misfit; Pedagogical Disclosure; Futurity Discourse.
Introduction
Failure in contemporary organizations is rarely a singular, easily identifiable event. More often, it accumulates in small, invisible withdrawals of attention, vague reschedulings, micro-corrections delivered through tone, and the growing sense that decisions are being made without being formally named. While such trajectories are often retrospectively explained as poor fit, individual limitations, or misaligned expectations, organizational research has long demonstrated that failure is often the result of systems that distribute knowledge, authority, and accountability unevenly. These systems obscure contradictions and render their consequences administratively invisible (Perrow, 1984; Reason, 1990; Vaughan, 1996).
This article argues that failure should be understood as a process of diagnostic sorting rather than an outcome of individual performance. Misfit is often produced early and stabilized through subtle, everyday interactions, from role opacity and vague directives to ambient silences and procedural deferrals. The argument is developed through a scene-based autoethnography of a 90-day probationary period in a corporate environment marked by post-restructuring strain. The empirical material consists of ten field-note scenes written during entry and the final week preceding termination. Identifying details have been omitted or altered to protect anonymity while preserving analytic integrity.
This study builds on queer phenomenology and critical institutional scholarship to describe how organizations function as orienting environments, shaping the bodies, affects, and styles of communication that appear competent. Misfit emerges not through deviation but through disorientation, especially when institutional directives are followed too literally, making their incoherence visible. Queer theory and critical disability studies deepen this account by foregrounding the norms of intelligibility: difference is tolerated when it remains implicit and privately managed, but pedagogical clarity can be read as disruption because it redistributes interpretive authority (Butler, 2004; McRuer, 2006). The article also integrates scholarship on affective labor and organizational control to highlight how judgment operates not through articulated standards but through tone, restraint, and the ability to absorb ambiguity without naming it (Hochschild, 1983; Gregg, 2011; Gill, 2014). Under conditions of contraction and overload, organizations often rely more heavily on informal, deniable mechanisms of evaluation that allow judgment to operate without accountability (Crozier, 1964; Kühl, 2015; Vaughan, 1996). The article extends these insights into the realm of futurity discourse, showing how narratives of optimization reward initiative that aligns with institutional coherence and punish it when it makes infrastructural limits explicit (Beer, 2019; Zuboff, 2019).
The study treats scenes as diagnostic events, which are analytic data points where institutional power becomes visible. The analysis demonstrates how misfit is not found at the end of probation through a performance review but assembled over time through early legibility tests, affective cues, and relational withdrawal. The conclusion expands the implications of the findings, arguing that the probationary period functions less as a trial of competence and more as a diagnostic machine that sorts by legibility, affect, and interpretive control, then finalizes the sorting through silence and procedural closure.
Background and Context
The field notes analyzed here were written during a probationary period in a large corporate organization shaped by post-layoff strain. The built environment projected containment, quiet, minimalism, and calibrated calm, while ambiguity became costly and speech narrowed. Probation enabled evaluation without narration: criteria remained latent, judgments arrived without explanation, and newcomers were asked to perform “fit” without being told what fit meant.
This environment echoes Brown and Michael’s (2003) argument that future expectations and technological promises often shape organizational dynamics before practical utility is demonstrated. In the case of this corporate setting, the future-oriented expectations of smooth functioning and efficient integration were rehearsed through every ritual, but the operational reality was fragmented and increasingly incoherent. The built environment, designed to maintain an aura of efficiency, became a site of profound institutional uncertainty. Expectations of competence and compliance were not immediately clear; they were projected atmospherically, where ambiguity itself became costly (Beer, 2019). Expectation, as McRuer (2006) and Brown (2003) suggest, shapes both the institutional vision and the worker’s legibility, making it clear that misfit is not only an individual flaw but an effect of how the system fails to absorb uncertainty.
Early interactions made clear that legibility hinged not only on completing tasks but on fluency in an implicit organizational grammar. Security was framed as a systemic necessity, not relational investment. Training prioritized procedural reproduction over understanding, and requests for rationale were met with formulaic deferrals. Competence became a performance of memory, not sense-making. This echoes McRuer’s (2006) notion of compulsory able-bodiedness, which parallels the compulsory efficiency expected of workers in environments governed by uncertainty. Workers are trained not to question but to repeat, to perform their role without being told why.
The role was labeled autonomous but offered minimal support. Its core function, stitching together misaligned systems, was essential yet low-status, with accountability routed to the human interface when infrastructure failed. In this ecology, visibility, tone, and even clarity could register as liabilities. Difference was most tolerable when privately managed; pedagogical presence could read as excess. Here, Halberstam (2011) offers critical insight: failure in these environments is not incompetence but excessive visibility. Institutional control works by restricting what can be seen and what must remain ambient. Halberstam’s critique of failure as resistance repositions institutional misfit not as deviation but as a challenge to the accepted norm, making misfit the result of institutional limits.
These scenes are read not as narrative reflections but as diagnostic events, where misfit is assembled through ambient mechanisms, calibrated politeness, procedural opacity, silence, and resolved through withdrawal rather than correction. Though corporate in setting, the dynamics traced here extend across institutions. Similar patterns appear in academia, healthcare, and public sectors, where ambiguity is governed rather than clarified, and misalignment is personalized through deferral, not addressed. McRuer (2006) and Kühl (2015) underscore that institutional failure is less an isolated event and more a gradual accumulation of misalignments, each small, deniable calibration compounding the next.
Diagnostic sorting is thus not an outlier but a general institutional logic under conditions of overload and reputational risk. Both corporate and academic systems deploy this strategy of diagnostic sorting, often recasting the failure as individual misfit while obscuring the structural causes (Flanagan, 1954). Institutions, whether corporate or academic, manage ambiguity not by clarifying uncertainty, but by sorting it into categories that reify legibility (Ahmed, 2012). The institutional gaze operates through the discursive framework of the future: the promise of optimizing efficiency, despite the hidden fragmentation beneath.
Literature review
Work and organizational scholarship has long critiqued the tendency to individualize accounts of failure and underperformance, showing instead how misfit is often a product of institutional design, evaluative practices, and environmental conditions rather than a matter of isolated incompetence. Failure, particularly in complex systems, is often a predictable outcome of how knowledge, responsibility, and authority are distributed. When roles remain ambiguous and infrastructures are fragmented or loosely coupled, breakdowns become inevitable, and certain explanations for those failures are unavailable. As Perrow (1984) and Reason (1990) suggest, failure isn’t a deviation from the standard; it’s a systemic consequence of how organizations are designed and how they function under pressure.
This systemic breakdown is amplified in environments shaped by structural strain, whether through downsizing, managerial overload, or organizational change. As Crozier (1964) and Vaughan (1996) have described, institutions under strain tend to narrow their tolerance for ambiguity and increase reliance on tacit judgment. It is in these moments of institutional contraction that evaluation begins to proceed without explicit criteria, where fit is no longer an individual capability but a necessary shorthand for managing an organization’s limited capacity to absorb uncertainty. Legibility itself becomes the standard by which workers are assessed, not just for what they do, but for how quickly and smoothly they can be interpreted without generating interpretive costs.
In such an environment, expectations of competence shift from being an internalized measure of productivity to a performance of legibility, and affective norms are no longer optional virtues but rather requirements for employability. As Hochschild (1983) first identified, organizations govern through the regulation of tone, demeanor, and self-presentation, and these norms intensify under conditions of risk and disruption. In post-Fordist work environments, workers are required to manage their affect, to maintain restraint and flexibility, even when these internal states contradict the structures around them. This regulation is most often subtle, carried out through micro-signals and atmospheric cues rather than explicit corrective feedback, a discursive practice that allows evaluation to remain ambiguous and uncontested (Gregg, 2011; Gill, 2014).
This concept of legibility is further complicated by the transition from development to diagnostic modes of institutional operation. As Kühl (2015) argues, organizations often stabilize early impressions into durable judgments, thus foreclosing recalibration and turning misfit into an ongoing trajectory. Misfit becomes a cumulative product, an effect of deniable calibrations and subtle adjustments over time. The result is a shift in evaluation from growth-oriented development to a diagnostic sorting process, where workers’ ability to meet tacit expectations is judged long before any formal evaluation occurs. This process, as Kühl notes, is not just about identifying failure but about sorting it into manageable categories, sorting that is faster than accompaniment, where failure becomes structurally inevitable rather than an individual flaw.
It’s not just corporate organizations that use these dynamics to manage misfit; this diagnostic logic also extends into academic institutions, where inclusion often becomes a rhetorical device rather than a concrete practice. Ahmed (2012) demonstrates how organizations can adopt the language of diversity while reinforcing normative boundaries of belonging, tolerating difference only as long as it remains manageable and non-disruptive. This mechanism of institutional inclusion relies on conditional recognition, where the institution tolerates difference but only when it conforms to its predetermined idea of “fit”. The institutional framework of diversity manages to acknowledge difference while simultaneously maintaining the status quo, which suggests that the logics of diagnostic sorting do not just exist in corporate settings but are also widespread across institutional types.
This pattern of sorting by legibility is visible not just in the rhetoric of inclusion but also in the affective responses to difference. Berlant’s (2011) concept of cruel optimism adds another layer to this analysis by showing how institutions sustain attachment to their promises, whether of belonging, success, or efficiency, through deferred clarity. In such spaces, failure is not marked immediately but is slowly accumulated through a process of non-recognition. When employees or students fail to align with institutional expectations, this failure is not formally labeled but becomes embedded in the tacit agreements that govern institutional life. The promise of future belonging is often maintained even after the institution has already foreclosed that possibility.
Queer theory and critical disability studies further refine this understanding by foregrounding the norms of intelligibility that govern institutional assessments of competence. Butler (2004) suggests that norms of competence work most effectively when they are naturalized, making any deviation appear as personal failure rather than structural constraint. Similarly, McRuer’s (2006) work on compulsory able-bodiedness illustrates how competence is often defined against unspoken expectations of fluency, productivity, and comportment. Difference is tolerated only when it remains individualized and privately managed, but when difference is pedagogically disclosed, it becomes readable as disruption, a breach of the unspoken contract between employee and institution.
Halberstam (2011) reframes failure as a diagnostic lens through which we can understand the normative assumptions embedded in definitions of success and coherence. On Halberstam’s view, failure is productive: it allows us to see institutional limits and to identify the kinds of presence an institution can absorb without acknowledgment, and what kinds must be quietly removed to preserve the appearance of neutrality. In this sense, failure is not a deficiency but a site of resistance, revealing institutional boundaries and the limits of coherence.
Finally, scholarship on datafication, surveillance, and managerial futurity provides critical context for understanding how optimization and automation discourse function within institutional governance. As Beer (2019) demonstrates, efficiency narratives often obscure infrastructural fragmentation, shifting responsibility onto workers who must adapt to the ever-evolving and increasingly fragmented tools they are expected to manage. Similarly, Zuboff’s (2019) concept of surveillance capitalism clarifies how efficiency narratives can function ideologically, promising coherence while managing incoherence in the workplace. When workers report limits to these narratives, the issue becomes not one of failure to innovate, but rather the production of unwanted legibility, where infrastructural incoherence is exposed and cannot be easily erased (Beer, 2019; Zuboff, 2019).
Theoretical Framework and Method
This article approaches institutional failure as a cumulative process of diagnostic sorting rather than a singular moment of judgment. It combines queer phenomenology, affective labor, and critical organizational theory to explore how institutions direct bodies and attention toward prescribed lines of conduct, while keeping key evaluative criteria tacit and ambiguous. Failure, in this context, is not an isolated breakdown, but a continuum of misalignment produced by latent criteria, procedural opacity, and atmospheric evaluation.
At the heart of this framework is the idea that legibility becomes a key evaluative tool. Institutions, whether corporate or academic, do not simply assess what people do but how easily they can be interpreted. Legibility governance plays a critical role here: workers and students alike are evaluated not only on their actions but on their ability to fit into a pre-established narrative structure, where their actions are readable without requiring interpretive labor (Ahmed, 2012; Beer, 2019). Legibility, in this sense, becomes an institution’s way of measuring efficiency and fit, often at the cost of the worker’s or student’s agency and interpretative autonomy.
The intersection of legibility with broader dynamics of emotional labor and affective regulation further sharpens this analysis. As Hochschild (1983) and Gregg (2011) suggest, institutions increasingly require workers to manage affective states, to maintain a tone, demeanor, and emotional consistency that aligns with institutional expectations. This is especially evident in post-Fordist and precarious work cultures, where emotional restraint becomes not just a personal trait but a job requirement. These norms, which are often tacitly enforced, shape the contours of acceptable failure and success. Failure, in this frame, is not simply an absence of skill but a violation of the expected emotional and behavioral norm, policed through atmospheric cues and micro-signals of compliance (Hochschild, 1983; Gill, 2014).
The tension between institutional inclusion and misfit is a central theme in this framework, and it plays out across different settings. Ahmed (2012) shows how diversity initiatives often rely on conditional inclusion, accepting differences only when they do not challenge institutional norms. This creates a mechanism of diagnostic sorting, where institutions tolerate difference only as long as it remains manageable and non-disruptive. Once difference becomes visible or pedagogically significant, it shifts from being tolerated to being political, and often, erased. In this context, inclusion is not an open invitation but a conditional acceptance that enforces a boundary between acceptable difference and unacceptable misfit.
The concept of diagnostic sorting, as developed by Kühl (2015), extends these insights into the organizational process. Misfit, in this view, is not a discrete event but an ongoing process of calibration. Institutional failure becomes an accumulation of subtle adjustments, a trajectory of misalignments that occur over time, often prior to any formal evaluation. As Perrow (1984) and Reason (1990) argue, organizational failure typically results not from individual errors but from distributed responsibility and fragmented systems. These systems make failure a predictable outcome, especially in complex environments where misfit is not an anomaly, but a structural outcome, created by systems that privilege procedural certainty over contextual clarity.
To further refine this analysis, queer theory and critical disability studies provide essential tools for understanding the intelligibility that governs institutional assessment. As Butler (2004) argues, norms of competence work most effectively when they are naturalized, rendering deviation as personal failure rather than structural constraint. McRuer (2006) deepens this by showing how competence is often defined against unspoken norms, whether related to fluency, productivity, or comportment, which are often based on able-bodied or heteronormative standards. Difference, in this framework, is not simply tolerated but contained within a set of invisible norms that demand private management rather than public acknowledgment (Butler, 2004; McRuer, 2006).
In this light, failure is not merely about falling short of institutional expectations but about revealing institutional limits. Halberstam (2011) reframes failure as a diagnostic tool that exposes the normative assumptions embedded in institutional definitions of success. Failure here becomes productive: it allows us to see institutional contradictions, revealing what kinds of presence the institution can absorb and what kinds must be quietly removed to preserve its neutrality. This reframing shifts failure from a personal deficit to a site of resistance, allowing us to uncover hidden power dynamics rather than simply pointing to an absence of competency.
At the same time, scholarship on datafication, surveillance, and managerial futurity provides crucial context for understanding how optimization and automation discourses govern institutions. Beer (2019) discusses how efficiency narratives often function ideologically, masking infrastructural fragmentation and shifting responsibility onto workers who must adapt to fragmented tools. Similarly, Zuboff (2019) discusses how technological promises of efficiency rely on surveillance systems that distribute accountability while maintaining institutional coherence. Workers are positioned as adaptive interfaces who must bridge gaps between increasingly fragmented systems. When these tools fail to integrate, the worker’s initiative becomes a liability, exposing incoherence that institutions would rather keep hidden.
Thus, failure is not simply about incompetence or lack of initiative but about disrupting institutional coherence. Diagnostic sorting, as discussed through queer phenomenology and critical disability studies, reveals how institutions manage difference through procedural opacity, atmospheric regulation, and tacit exclusion. This framework shifts the focus from individual failure to the institutional mechanics that produce and sustain failure as a process.
Building on the conceptual framework, the methodology of this study employs scene-based analysis as a tool for understanding how institutional failure is produced and experienced. The scenes are analyzed as diagnostic units, which means they are treated as key data points that capture moments in which institutional expectations, affective norms, and evaluative silence become legible in practice. This approach allows us to track how misfit is assembled over time, often prior to any formal evaluation, and how judgment is communicated through deferral, withdrawal, and procedural mediation rather than through corrective feedback (Flanagan, 1954; Ellis, 2004). Each scene is viewed not as an isolated narrative but as a critical incident that encapsulates the power dynamics within institutions, allowing us to see how failure is produced through structural misalignments and procedural opacity.
The scenes are sequenced in such a way that they track the progression of failure, not as a single moment of judgment, but as a trajectory of misalignments, where early legibility tests and affective cues shape the perception of fit and misfit. This method allows the researcher to document institutional power in action, observing how institutions sort individuals into categories of success or failure through ambient mechanisms, often without the need for explicit feedback or formal evaluation.
Field Notes: Ten Diagnostic Scenes
The following scenes are presented here as analytic data rather than retrospective narrative: each scene records a moment in which institutional expectations, affective norms, and evaluative silence became legible in practice.
Scene 1. Week 1, Day 1, Monday Morning: Entry After the Cuts
The first morning arrives as a muted ritual. The glass lobby, soft lighting, and controlled silence project an image of corporate calm. The faint smells of disinfectant and coffee linger, suggesting a hygienic kind of control. People move with calibrated efficiency, coats folded over forearms, eyes forward, voices low. I arrive early, trying to take up as little space as possible. The lobby, designed to soothe, feels more like an instrument, its minimalism a shield against what has been lost. The aesthetic is clean, but the mood is controlled, braced, an attempt to appear untroubled by the remnants of recent restructuring. Calm is not natural; it is performed. When I introduce myself at reception, the receptionist offers a polite smile, but her expression reads like someone whose limits have been tested repeatedly. Her kindness is procedural, not personal. The elevator ride is brief, punctuated only by a woman who looks at my badge and asks, “You’re starting today?” Her response is neutral, even professional, but it is also absent of any elaboration. A simple, transactional exchange.
In this first encounter, I feel the institution already deciding my place. I was told the role was essential, insulated, protected. But walking in, I feel the opposite. Not rejection, but an unsettling sorting mechanism, the institution already classifies me before I can even make myself visible. This scene functions as a diagnostic unit in the Critical Incident Technique (Flanagan, 1954), capturing how institutional power is enacted through environmental design, affecting how newcomers are both seen and read. The silent evaluation of newcomers through the built environment becomes a subtle form of governance, where institutional expectations are scripted into the aesthetic and rituals of corporate life (Crozier, 1964).
Scene 2. Week 1, Day 1, Monday Midday: The Acronym That Does Not Exist
The onboarding room feels like a sterile version of welcome. The long tables, identical laptops, and welcome kits present a unity that feels forced. A slide deck loops through values, compliance, and belonging, and a voice says, “We’re happy to have you,” with a cheerfulness that seems too polished for comfort. I try to lighten the silence by speaking, not to dominate, but to make the experience feel more human. My fellow new hires respond with an odd deference, as though ease is misread as authority. When a senior leader enters and makes his way around the room, he stops at me and asks, “So what’s your role?” I answer quickly, but not quickly enough. I reach for the acronym that should make me legible: “I’m a CTC.”
His confusion is palpable. “A what?” he asks, but his smile doesn’t break. It tightens. I fumble, trying to clarify, but the correction lands messily. The silence between us thickens before he nods and moves on.
Later, another new hire whispers, “What does that stand for?” The question lingers, unresolved. My answer, both quick and uncertain, is a failure of institutional clarity, the acronyms and roles are only real on paper, not within the shared institutional narrative. The role exists in name only, not in understanding. This scene documents how legibility governance operates on multiple levels, through the language of the institution and the invisible criteria that regulate both performance and presence (Ahmed, 2012). The act of naming oneself becomes a test of fluency, where the inability to match the institutional script is coded as a failure to conform (Beer, 2019).
Scene 3. Week 1, Day 2: “You’re Safe” as an Accounting Story
The kitchen corner is a small domestic theater, trying to resemble normalcy. People move through the motions of making tea and rinsing mugs, but the intensity of their actions suggests displacement. The space is engineered to absorb emotion, as if the building itself is trying to stay neutral. Reassurance comes quickly in these early exchanges. “Don’t worry,” one colleague says, stirring tea. “Different budget.” The word "different" feels like armor. Another adds, “Your role is essential.” Essential. Protected. Insulated. The story becomes policy, a script repeated so often it begins to sound like fact, even though no document explicitly supports it. But my mind is attuned to what is avoided. This reassurance, too smooth and too quick, attaches to no human commitment. It speaks only to structure.
Later, when I ask a basic question about task ownership, I’m met with a vague response: “We just… do it this way.” The absence of explanation feels like permission to comply without understanding. My supervisor’s directive that I be “dependable” comes without context, as a blanket expectation that is as much about compliance as about capability. This scene isolates a key power dynamic where reassurance functions as a managerial tool that stabilizes compliance while withholding the conditions that would make safety meaningful (Crozier, 1964; Ahmed, 2012). The institution manages uncertainty by turning continuity into procedure, where the employee is framed as a cog within a system that requires little human connection (Hochschild, 1983).
Scene 4. Week 2: Training by Clicking, Without “Why”
Training, when it happens, is fragmented. It feels like borrowing time from a disaster, catching someone on the run. When we finally meet, it's via screen share, proximity without presence.
“Okay,” my trainer says, “so you click here.” She clicks. “Then you copy this.” She copies. “Then you save it here. Also here.” Her speed is the survival of someone who has learned to navigate a broken system. I write everything down like I’m preparing for an exam. The steps are clear. The purpose is unclear. I want to ask, “Why are we doing this?” but the pace discourages questioning. If I interrupt, it feels like I’m stealing air.
When I do ask, her hesitation is palpable. “Because… we always have,” she says. The answer is not wrong, but it is empty. What’s missing is not knowledge, it’s framing. The lack of overview is not oversight but a deliberate omission that renders the “why” irrelevant. This scene exposes procedural opacity as a form of institutional control (Flanagan, 1954; Beer, 2019). Training is delivered through repetition without context, positioning the worker as an interface, not an interpreter (Perrow, 1984). The organization insulates itself from critique by withholding reasoning, making compliance the sole metric of competence while eroding the worker’s agency (Hochschild, 1983).
Scene 5. Week 2, Thursday: “Be a One-Person Show”
The exchange comes as a directive, not as a relationship. “You need to be a one-person show,” my supervisor says, without elaboration. It is said with admiration, even trust, but there is no substance behind it. I try to perform the role, becoming available, writing careful, non-demanding messages, asking for clarification without sounding desperate. But no one comes. My attempts to assert myself are met with institutional indifference, I am visible but not seen.
In one meeting, I offer a suggestion, and the room goes silent for a moment before moving on. My suggestion, however well-intended, is ignored. The invisibility of the worker is not an accident, but a design. This scene highlights autonomy as isolation, a demand for self-sufficiency without the authority, access, or shared meaning to perform it effectively (Gill, 2014; Gregg, 2011). The institution isolates employees under the guise of empowerment, making failure appear as an individual flaw rather than a systemic design choice (Flanagan, 1954).
Scene 6. Week 3: The Work Is Email, Over and Over
The rhythm of the day is established in repetition, subject lines that don’t change, templates that get reused, and systems that don’t align. The work is not quite workflow, but not quite trance either. It feels like tracing the same loop over and over, checking, copying, pasting, and saving. The work demands vigilance, but not in a cognitive way, it narrows my attention. I become careful, but not in a creative or meaningful way. The work isn’t about solving problems; it’s about ensuring nothing goes wrong within a structure that barely holds together. The workload is high, but the impact is low, no one notices the thinness until the system collapses.
I realize the difference between low skill and low status: the work requires human judgment, but it’s treated as an inconvenience. I’m necessary to smooth the cracks, but my work is invisible. The institutional focus is on efficiency and smooth operation, while human judgment becomes a dirty secret, needed, but never acknowledged. This scene highlights legibility governance as an evaluative tool at the level of labor form. The work is structured to be easily auditable but minimizes the worker’s interpretive capacity (Beer, 2019; Zuboff, 2019). The judgment made by the worker is treated as a failure when it becomes visible, illustrating how institutions reward compliance rather than competence, reinforcing a cycle of invisible labor (Flanagan, 1954; Vaughan, 1996).
Scene 7. Week 3: Disclosing My Stutter
The moment when I meet a new group is always a calibration, a chance to decide what will be accepted and what will be visible. I preemptively explain my stutter, not because I want attention but because silence around it invites misunderstanding.
“Just so you know,” I say, “if you think I’m confused or the line is cutting, I’m not. I’m not high, I’m not drunk. It’s just my stutter.”
The laughter that follows is genuine, a brief moment of humanity. But then I notice my supervisor. Her expression flickers, surprise, a subtle shock at my openness. It’s not discomfort with my stutter but a reaction to my decision to name it. Her response feels like a silent judgment, not of my body, but of how I choose to present it. I realize later that by naming the difference, I’ve disrupted the institution’s control over how difference is managed. The institution would have preferred my stutter to remain ambient, unspoken, and individualized. By making it pedagogical, I redistributed interpretive authority: the difference was no longer my secret, but our understanding. This scene illustrates how disability (and difference) becomes institutionally meaningful only when it destabilizes the institution's preferred tempo and fluency (McRuer, 2006; Ahmed, 2006). The act of naming difference in this context is not merely personal but disruptive to institutional narratives of coherence. The institution’s reaction to disclosure exposes how legibility norms enforce normative standards that silence difference (Flanagan, 1954; Ahmed, 2012).
Scene 8. Final Week, Monday Daytime: Team Building as Sorting
We gather in a polished venue for a team-building event. The venue is curated, the agenda scripted, and the smiles are as rehearsed as the words about culture and agility. Innovation is framed as a goal, but there is no mention of what actually needs to change to make it possible. When we are sorted into groups, I end up in a musical exercise, despite my limited background. As I engage, I notice something strange: my questions and energy make the group more alive, but most of the others remain restrained, compliant with the tone set by the institution. I am visible, engaged, and enthusiastic. They are invisible, a restrained neutrality that blends in.
I start to feel the difference: their restraint isn’t passivity; it’s literacy. They know how to stay within the institutional affective bandwidth, the right level of engagement that doesn’t challenge the institutional script. My failure, if it is a failure, is not a lack of competence. It is a surplus of presence. This scene functions as diagnostic sorting, belonging in this institution is determined by the ability to perform within calibrated affective limits (Gregg, 2011; Ahmed, 2012). The exercise acts as a soft diagnostic tool, testing who can fit into the institution’s predetermined narrative without drawing too much attention to themselves (Flanagan, 1954; Halberstam, 2011). Enthusiasm and visibility become liabilities when they disrupt the institutional rhythm.
Scene 9. Final Week, Monday Evening: The Joke, the Eye Roll, the Silence
Outside the restaurant, waiting for taxis. There is a brief moment of real laughter, unstructured and unscripted. In this small window, the group almost feels human, but then I make a joke, a small defiance against the professionalism that has structured our time together. A supervisor rolls her eyes. The response isn’t a confrontation. It is an absence, a micro-sanction. She says nothing, but her body language speaks volumes. There is no reprimand, no meeting, no formal feedback. Instead, the silence expands, an absence that feels like a verdict.
The next day, she avoids eye contact. She doesn’t greet me. The atmosphere cools. I feel myself being erased, my presence unacknowledged. The organization has disciplined me not through a formal procedure but through social withdrawal, relational absence. This scene illustrates micro-sanction as a deniable governance mechanism. The silence and withdrawal communicate judgment without formal feedback, allowing the institution to enforce power without accountability (Ahmed, 2012; Hochschild, 1983). It highlights how social behavior can be used as a soft mechanism for exclusion, shaping compliance without ever needing to name it (Flanagan, 1954; Beer, 2019).
Scene 10. Final Day, Thursday: AI Discourse, Then Termination by Procedure
The final days are marked by a quiet shift, meetings are rescheduled, emails go unanswered, and communication becomes increasingly opaque. At a senior leadership event, AI tools are framed as an ethical imperative of efficiency, tools that optimize resources and reduce human intervention. The message is clear: if you’re not using these tools, are you really managing efficiently? I take the challenge literally. I try to automate my workflow, only to fail. The work requires more than automation; it requires human context, decision-making, and judgment. I report this back, explaining why the AI can’t function in the way promised. The response is muted, and within a few days, my access to the team begins to dwindle.
Later, I’m told that HR will be my only point of contact. The doors close without sound, leaving me with the sense that my termination was procedural, not evaluative. This scene ties into the concept of managerial futurity: AI discourse operates as a narrative tool, disciplining critique while masking infrastructural incoherence. When initiative exposes this incoherence, it becomes a liability, a risk for the institution that must be managed, not corrected (Beer, 2019; Ahmed, 2012). The termination process is not one of evaluation but of erasure, an outcome of procedural closure rather than evaluative judgment (Flanagan, 1954; Crozier, 1964).

Analysis and Findings
The following analysis unfolds the ten field-note scenes as a cohesive diagnostic arc, with misfit not being found at the end of probation through formal evaluation but instead being assembled early through orientation, stabilized through silence, and finalized through procedural closure. The focus across the scenes shifts from individual performance to the institution’s reading of presence, how the institution interprets, accommodates, and ultimately withdraws interpretive generosity as legibility is solidified. What emerges is less about the worker’s actions than how those actions are processed by the institution through a network of subtle cues, atmospheric regulation, and procedural opacity.
The first diagnostic mechanism that surfaces is atmospheric pre-diagnosis, an invisible sorting mechanism embedded in the institution’s environment. In Scene 1, the building itself functions as a containment mechanism, with its minimalist design and controlled silence serving as the first form of evaluation. The institution's performance of calm masks the latent tension of recent restructuring, and in this context, calm is not natural but performed under scrutiny. The key moment of evaluation is subtle: not hostile but restrained speech, the casual dismissal of “It’s been a week” encapsulates an early institutional tactic to manage narrative space and limit what can be said. This quiet regulation sets the stage for the institution to decide the “tolerable shape” of the new hire before any real interaction. Belonging here is not about welcoming but about preemptive calibration, deciding who fits before the role even begins.
In Scene 2, this early atmospheric regulation gives way to an explicit legibility test. The question, "So, what’s your role?" becomes a demand for the worker to name themselves inside the institution’s grammar, not simply by what they do but how they are legible within the institution's pre-set narrative structure. The misstep of the acronym, “I’m a CTC”, is not simply an error of knowledge but an error in legibility: it exposes that the institutional category I am meant to inhabit is itself unstable. The manager’s reaction, a tightened smile and a swift move-on, exemplifies how judgment is communicated without confrontation. It’s not about a personal failure but about a systemic failure to clarify roles, which shifts the instability from institutional design to the worker’s body. The worker’s misfit becomes not an individual flaw but a structural omission. This scene is an illustration of legibility governance, where the ability to fit into the narrative becomes a more significant measure than comprehension or competence (Ahmed, 2012; Beer, 2019). The act of naming oneself is the first test of fluency, with any deviation from the script marked as failure to conform.
Scenes 3 through 6 deepen the diagnosis of misfit by showing how misrecognition is reinforced structurally. In Scene 3, the institution provides reassurance framed in bureaucratic terms: the role is “essential”, protected by budgetary insulation. However, the absence of personal commitment in this reassurance signals that the institution’s real investment lies in procedural continuity rather than relational care. The absence of mentorship becomes a systemic problem, not one of personal inadequacy. The phrase “We just… do it this way” in Scene 4 functions as a governance formula that discourages inquiry, turning lack of explanation into a structural condition. The worker is trained to execute without understanding, reducing the role to a mechanical function, an interface, not an interpreter (Perrow, 1984). Similarly, in Scene 5, the injunction to be a “one-person show” paradoxically isolates the worker, demanding autonomy without authority. This contradiction becomes the vulnerability of the worker: the inability to inhabit contradiction smoothly is read as a personal failing, rather than a flaw in organizational design. Here, the institutional design failure, the lack of relational infrastructure, is reframed as an individual flaw. The worker is diagnosed as a failure not for lack of competence but for their inability to manage the invisible expectations of autonomy and independence (Gill, 2014; Gregg, 2011).
Scene 6 then presents the most direct contradiction of all: workers are required to engage in repetitive tasks that demand human judgment, but this judgment is neither recognized as expertise nor rewarded. This scene reveals the third diagnostic mechanism: indispensability as low status. Human judgment is necessary to patch the gaps in the fragmented systems, but it is invisible in practice. The worker’s efforts to reconcile discrepancies between tools are overlooked, their necessary contributions rendered unremarkable. In this context, failure is not a lack of skill, but an invisible labor that remains unacknowledged (Beer, 2019; Zuboff, 2019). This invisibility of labor is key to understanding legibility governance: the worker is positioned as necessary but undervalued, their expertise never recognized because smoothness, not critical thinking, becomes the preferred organizational outcome.
Scene 7 marks a critical shift, from interpretive uncertainty to affective exposure. In this moment of self-disclosure, I name my stutter as a means of reclaiming interpretive authority. By naming my difference, I challenge the institution’s habit of managing difference in silence, exposing its reliance on deniability to preserve its neutrality. This shift highlights how the institution only tolerates difference when it remains private and manageable. When difference is pedagogically disclosed, it disrupts the institution’s discretionary power and challenges its ability to control interpretation (Butler, 2004; McRuer, 2006). Naming my difference is not just an act of personal transparency but an institutional disruption: it forces the institution to reckon with the limits of its own tolerance.
In the final weeks (Scenes 8 to 10), the institution shifts from early diagnostic sorting to closure through procedural mechanisms. In Scene 8, the team-building exercise functions as an affective bandwidth test, where participation within institutional affective limits becomes a form of evaluation. Those who stay within the tonal limits pass, while those who deviate are marked as too visible, a failure of alignment with the institution’s affective narrative. The sorting is hidden beneath the guise of team-building and informality; informal settings become soft evaluative tools, testing who can fit within the institution’s emotional parameters (Gregg, 2011; Halberstam, 2011). Scene 9 reinforces this with micro-sanction by withdrawal, where relational absence becomes the key mode of discipline. There is no formal reprimand; instead, the worker is punished through social isolation. The institution governs not through formal feedback, but by managing the relational field around the worker, controlling visibility while maintaining the appearance of neutrality (Ahmed, 2012; Hochschild, 1983).
Finally, in Scene 10, AI discourse functions as a narrative tool that enforces institutional coherence by demanding alignment with the organization’s technological future. My failure to automate the work exposes infrastructural incoherence, making the gap between narrative and reality visible. This is the real threat to the institution: not technological failure, but the visibility of failure. The termination is then not a result of personal incompetence but a procedural erasure, where the institution maintains control by closing the relationship without explanation, a process of administrative neutrality that eliminates the possibility of contestation (Flanagan, 1954; Crozier, 1964).
Taken together, the scenes yield a compact set of qualitative results and inductive insights :
Failure is Diagnosed Early: Misfit is assembled before any formal performance evaluation, often through institutional orientation and role legibility rather than task-based outcomes.
Evaluation Becomes Affective: In precarious environments, the emotional tone, restraint, and invisibility replace explicit standards of competence, revealing how failure is not simply about skill but about fitting within affective norms.
Difference is Tolerated in Private: Pedagogical clarity and public acknowledgment of difference expose institutional limits, making transparency legible as resistance.
Initiative is Conditional: Innovation is welcomed when it reinforces institutional narratives; when it exposes infrastructural limits, it becomes a liability.
Termination as Erasure: Procedural closure, not evaluative judgment, marks the final step in the institutional process, where legibility is prioritized over competence.
This analysis reveals how misfit is not an isolated failure but an ongoing, systemic process, shaped by diagnostic sorting, legibility governance, and affective regulation. The scenes provide a qualitative map of how institutions manage difference, not by accommodating it, but by sorting it through procedural opacity and relational withdrawal.
This article has argued that failure in contemporary institutions is less an outcome of inadequate performance than a diagnostic process through which misfit is identified, stabilized, and resolved without being explicitly named. Drawing on a queer phenomenological, scene-based autoethnography of a 90-day probationary period in a multinational pharmaceutical corporation (anonymized), the analysis showed how misalignment emerged through role opacity, affective governance, and procedural silence rather than through articulated criteria or corrective feedback. Failure, in this account, is an institutional accomplishment that protects organizational coherence by relocating contradiction into the worker (Perrow, 1984; Vaughan, 1996).
Methodologically, the paper demonstrated the value of treating scenes as diagnostic events rather than illustrative anecdotes. Across entry after cuts, legibility tests in onboarding, procedural training without rationale, demands for autonomy under managerial absence, repetitive infrastructure-glue work, pedagogical disclosure of speech difference, team-building as affective sorting, automation discourse as futurity ritual, and termination by procedure, a consistent pattern became visible: institutions do not primarily fail to recognize competence; they fail to tolerate clarity. Initiative is rewarded when it affirms institutional narratives and neutralized when it renders infrastructural incoherence speakable (Kühl, 2015). Under such conditions, judgment does not require explicit articulation to be effective; it is conveyed through narrowed bandwidth, delayed responses, withdrawal, and the rerouting of relation into procedure.
Theoretically, the article contributes to queer phenomenology by specifying misfit as disorientation rather than deviation. Following Ahmed (2006), the analysis showed how people who follow institutional speech literally, by seeking role coherence, making difference intelligible, or empirically testing efficiency discourse, encounter the limits of institutional tolerance. These moments expose norms that remain hidden when one can move smoothly “in line,” and the friction produced by those norms is then attributed to the body that encounters it.
The paper also extends scholarship on affective labor by detailing how evaluation is enacted through tone, restraint, and silence. Affective neutrality emerged as an unspoken threshold of belonging, while visible engagement, pedagogy, and literalism were read as excess (Hochschild, 1983; Gregg, 2011; Gill, 2014). Crucially, the regulation of this excess occurred through deniable micro-sanctions rather than explicit correction, producing a form of accountability that operates without leaving a stable archive. Queer theory and critical disability studies sharpen this point: difference is most tolerable when it remains individualized and privately managed, whereas pedagogical disclosure redistributes interpretive authority and can be diagnosed as misfit rather than accommodated (Butler, 2004; McRuer, 2006).
Finally, the analysis complicates popular contrasts between corporate dehumanization and academic refuge by arguing that similar diagnostic logics operate across domains. In both settings, opaque evaluation, withdrawal of care, and retrospective narratives of “fit” can transform institutional incoherence into individualized deficiency, while institutional promises sustain attachment until clarity arrives too late to matter (Ahmed, 2012; Berlant, 2011). The broader implication is that contemporary failure is increasingly built into institutional design in contexts of precarity, fragmentation, and managerial saturation: diagnostic judgments stabilize early, travel through silence, and become administratively inevitable by the time they are enacted.
This article does not offer a reform program. Its contribution is diagnostic: to show how institutions manage difference and uncertainty by converting evaluation into procedure and making judgment deniable. What appears, in administrative language, as probation or fit functions less as development than as sorting, accomplished through the micromechanics of everyday organizational life: postponed meetings without agenda, narrowed affective bandwidth, social withdrawal in place of feedback, communication rerouted through intermediaries, access revoked as a technicality. In these sequences, procedure becomes the decision’s most durable disguise. The scenes end where institutions often end: not with an explanation, but with a closed channel. Silence, in this frame, is not the absence of judgment; it is one of its most reliable forms.
Conclusion
This article has traced the subtle and pervasive process of diagnostic sorting within institutional settings, reframing failure as a systemic outcome rather than an isolated judgment. By analyzing ten field-note scenes, the study has shown how misfit is not the result of an individual’s lack of competence but the product of latent institutional expectations, procedural opacity, and affective norms. Failure is assembled over time, in early moments of interaction, through role opacity, and finally through procedural closure, which leaves little space for correction or understanding.
The findings demonstrate how legibility governance operates as a central mechanism in this process, where institutions assess not only what individuals do, but how easily they can be read according to pre-established scripts. These scripts often favor compliance and visibility over critical thinking or conceptual clarity, especially when institutions are under strain or facing reputational risks. Misfit is diagnosed early, through seemingly benign mechanisms like ambient silence, vague directives, or procedural deferrals, before any formal performance review takes place.
In light of this, the article argues that failure is often not a singular event but a process of managing ambiguity, where organizations enact power by dictating what can be seen and what must remain hidden. The withdrawal of interpretive generosity and the reliance on informal, affective cues become crucial tools for controlling difference without ever naming it. By drawing on queer phenomenology, affective labor, and critical disability studies, this article contributes to our understanding of how legibility, inclusion, and difference are negotiated in institutions that maintain coherence through silent sorting rather than open evaluation. This framework extends beyond the corporate context, resonating with practices in academia and other institutions, where ambiguity is governed, and misfit is individualized through deniable mechanisms of judgment.
The methodological contribution of this paper lies in treating autoethnographic scenes as diagnostic events, revealing how institutional failure is produced and finalized through ambient mechanisms, such as calibrated politeness, deferred meetings, and procedural withdrawal, instead of through corrective feedback. This scene-based analysis provides an invaluable framework for studying institutional governance, offering a method to render the micromechanics of evaluative silence empirically legible.
