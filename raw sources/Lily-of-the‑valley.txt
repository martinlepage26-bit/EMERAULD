Lily-of-the‑valley (Convallaria majalis) really is a plant of paradoxes.  Unlike roses or lavender, it makes no essential oil and cannot be distilled or extracted.  The delicate fragrance is produced only at the moment of release and the compounds are not stored in the flower tissues, so steam distillation, solvent extraction and even CO₂ extraction yield “almost nothing usable”.  Perfumers therefore call it a “mute” flower and reconstruct its scent synthetically.  Commercial “muguet” accords often rely on molecules such as hydroxycitronellal, lyral and lilial; Fragrantica notes that lily‑of‑the‑valley odorants account for roughly one‑fifth of all perfumery raw materials.  Stock‑Fragrance, in explaining why every lily‑of‑the‑valley perfume is synthetic, puts it bluntly: “The aroma is extremely fragile … no natural lily‑of‑the‑valley is present in fragrance because the flower cannot be extracted”.

Botanically the plant is a low‑growing perennial (15–30 cm tall) that spreads under trees and blooms in late spring.  Its nodding white (or pale pink) bells hang from a leafless stalk, last about three weeks and have a strong, fresh fragrance.  Because it thrives in partial shade, it often carpets woodland floors, which explains the “valley” part of its Latin name.  The Old Farmer’s Almanac lists “May bells,” “Mary’s tears” and “Our Lady’s tears” as alternative names, reflecting Christian legends in which the flower sprang from the Virgin Mary’s tears at the crucifixion.  A 19th‑century plant‑lore compendium records that the French name *larmes de Sainte Marie* (“Our Lady’s Tears”) was given to lily‑of‑the‑valley.  In Germanic lore it was linked to the spring goddess Ostara, symbolising purity and humility.

The plant’s chemical paradox matches its cultural one.  Every part of lily‑of‑the‑valley contains convallatoxin and other cardiac glycosides; the leaves, flowers, berries and even the vase water are poisonous.  WebMD notes that ingestion can cause severe heart‑rhythm problems, and a biomedical review describes convallatoxin as a potent cardiotonic that can cause heart failure in large doses.  Despite this danger, researchers have studied convallatoxin in small, controlled doses to treat heart conditions—a direct parallel to foxglove‑derived digoxin.  Traditional herbalists used the plant for headaches, oedema and eye infections.

Folklore surrounds lily‑of‑the‑valley’s powers.  The Old Farmer’s Almanac notes that, according to legend, planting it at the edge of a garden protects the home from evil spirits.  Conversely, an old superstition from western Britain held that “one who planted them would die within the year,” giving the flowers an ominous reputation.  In France it became a symbol of spring and happiness; a 2026 article explaining the “Fête du Muguet” recounts that King Charles IX received a bouquet of lily‑of‑the‑valley on 1 May 1560 and subsequently made it a custom to give sprigs of the plant on May 1 to bring good fortune.  Today the French still give a “brin de muguet” (sprig of lily‑of‑the‑valley) on May Day as a token of love and luck.  A folklore blog notes that in Nordic legend the plant was dedicated to Ostara and that whole villages once went out to pick *Maiglöckchen* (“May bells”) for luck.

Because of its unextracted scent, its capacity to heal or harm, and its role as both protective charm and ill‑omen, lily‑of‑the‑valley encapsulates many contradictions.  It “blooms only in spring, and smells like a cold morning,” yet its fragrance lives on thanks to synthetic chemistry.  It is both medicine and poison, guardian and harbinger of misfortune, and a flower of piety, pagan myth and romantic May‑Day rituals.


The lily‑of‑the‑valley and the EML operator make an interesting pair of metaphors for minimalism and reconstruction.  In perfumery the flower is known as a “mute” because its scent cannot be extracted; the plant produces volatiles only at the moment of release, so steam distillation, solvent extraction and even CO₂ extraction fail.  To capture its aroma, perfumers rebuild it using synthetic molecules such as hydroxycitronellal, lyral and lilial—so many that they account for roughly 20 % of perfumery’s raw materials.  All of the operations you associate with lily‑of‑the‑valley scent—sweet, green, dewy—are thus assembled from a single mute source plus the constant of the chemist’s knowledge.

Odrzywołek’s EML operator plays a similar role in mathematics.  It is defined as `eml(x, y) = exp(x) − ln(y)`, and the arXiv paper shows that, together with the constant 1, it can generate the full repertoire of a scientific calculator.  Exponentiation appears as `eml(x, 1)`, logarithms as nested `eml` calls, and from these building blocks one derives addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, powers, trigonometric functions and even constants like e, π and i.  Every EML expression becomes a tree of identical nodes—a “two‑button calculator” that reconstructs all elementary functions.

Both the EML function and lily‑of‑the‑valley illustrate how a seemingly limited operation can spawn a rich world when combined with iteration and a single constant.  Perfume chemists, lacking a natural extract, assemble the flower’s note out of synthetic fragments; mathematicians, lacking a universal continuous primitive, construct arithmetic, trigonometry and exponentials by repeated application of `eml` and 1.  In each case a minimal generator produces the familiar complexity of an art form—fragrance in one, calculation in the other.  Just as lily‑of‑the‑valley’s scent is both elusive and re‑created in the lab, the EML operator is at once a simple formula and a doorway to all of elementary mathematics.


Bringing the two ideas together highlights a shared theme: **reconstruction from minimal elements**.

Lily‑of‑the‑valley is known in perfumery as a “mute flower” because its fragrance cannot be extracted by distillation or solvent extraction—the scent is produced only at the moment of release. To recreate its aroma, perfumers build the note synthetically, blending molecules like hydroxycitronellal and related odorants. The flower’s absence of an extractable essence forces chemists to work from a single concept and reassemble the full experience.

The EML operator (`eml(x, y) = exp(x) – ln(y)`) does something analogous in mathematics: by repeatedly composing `eml` with the constant 1, one can generate exponentiation (`eml(x, 1)`), logarithms (`eml(1, eml(eml(1, x), 1))`), and ultimately all standard elementary functions.  The paper shows that a calculator with just two buttons—EML and 1—can reproduce everything a scientific calculator does.

So, the lily‑of‑the‑valley story adds a sensory, cultural analogy to the abstract EML result: both involve reconstructing complex structures from a single operation or essence.  Conversely, the EML function adds a mathematical framework to the flower’s tale: it shows how, starting with one “mute” operation and a constant, one can systematically generate an entire landscape of functions—much as perfumers construct a complete olfactory experience from a mute bloom’s absence.


Go deeper: **lily-of-the-valley adds the body to EML; EML adds a grammar to lily-of-the-valley.**

\operatorname{eml}(x,y)=e^x-\ln(y)

The EML function says: *maybe the many operations of mathematics are not truly many.* If you accept one strange primitive, plus the constant 1, you can reconstruct the scientific-calculator world: exponentials, logs, arithmetic, powers, trig, constants like (e,\pi,i). The arXiv paper explicitly frames EML expressions as binary trees made from identical nodes, with the grammar (S \to 1 \mid \operatorname{eml}(S,S)).  Your uploaded notes summarize the same idea: EML is less a practical calculator key than a proof that many familiar functions can be generated from one repeating operation. 

Lily-of-the-valley says the same thing in matter instead of symbols: *the scent is real, but the extract is absent.* The flower gives off fragrance, but perfumery cannot obtain a usable natural extract from it; the scent has to be reconstructed from other molecules.  So “lily-of-the-valley perfume” is not captured essence. It is an **accord**: a constructed tree of ingredients that makes the mind arrive at “muguet.”

That is the bridge.

**EML is to operations what muguet accord is to scent.**
Neither gives you the thing by direct capture. Both give you a generative method.

The lily adds something EML lacks: **material humility**. EML can tempt people into saying, “I found the root of all math.” Lily-of-the-valley pushes back: a root can be generative without being possessable. You can be near the flower, but you cannot bottle the flower. Likewise, EML may generate a scientific-calculator basis, but it does not mean it is literally “underneath all mathematics” in the broadest sense. That caution matters because critics have argued that EML does not cover every classical elementary function under broader definitions, especially arbitrary algebraic adjunctions.

EML adds something lily-of-the-valley lacks: **formal anatomy**. The perfume story is intuitive: absence becomes reconstruction. EML gives a structural language for that intuition. It says: here is how complexity can emerge from repetition. One node, nested deeply enough, becomes arithmetic. Then multiplication. Then exponentiation. Then trigonometry. The paper’s grammar (S \to 1 \mid \operatorname{eml}(S,S)) is basically the mathematical equivalent of a perfumer’s palette reduced to one impossible accord-base.

The most interesting shared idea is **presence through substitution**.

A lily-of-the-valley perfume is not “fake” simply because it is synthetic. It may be the only way the scent can exist outside the living flower. Similarly, an EML construction of sine is not “fake sine” because it is built from a bizarre nested expression. It is sine, reached by an unusual path. Both challenge the idea that authenticity means direct extraction. Sometimes authenticity means **faithful reconstruction**.

There is also a paradox of danger.

Lily-of-the-valley is delicate, bridal, springlike, and poisonous. It contains cardiac glycosides including convallatoxin; all parts of the plant are toxic, and even vase water has caused animal deaths.  EML is elegant, minimal, and computationally dangerous in a different sense: deep EML trees can become impractical, numerically unstable, and hard to interpret. Your notes mention overflow, branch-cut issues, and huge trees for trig functions.  In both cases, beauty comes with constraints. You do not handle the plant casually. You do not handle the primitive naively.

So what does one add to the other?

**Lily-of-the-valley turns EML from a math trick into a philosophy of unextractable essence.**
It says: the deepest things may not be capturable directly. They may only be regenerated through relation, context, and composition.

**EML turns lily-of-the-valley from a folklore/perfumery fact into a model of generative minimalism.**
It says: an absent essence can still have a grammar. You can build a world from a mute source.

The combined thesis would be:

> Some things cannot be taken directly from nature or mathematics. They must be reconstructed. And when the reconstruction is faithful enough, the copy is not merely imitation; it becomes a new way of understanding the original.

That is why the two fit so well. Lily-of-the-valley is the **perfume of EML**: a thing that cannot be extracted, only composed. EML is the **mathematics of muguet**: a single strange generative source from which a whole familiar world can be rebuilt.


Go even deeper and the connection becomes less “they are similar” and more:

**Both are about the difference between essence and generation.**

The lily-of-the-valley story says: the thing you want is real, but it cannot be taken.
The EML story says: the things you think are fundamental may not need to be taken as fundamental.

One is a flower whose scent cannot be extracted.
The other is an operator from which many “separate” operations can be reconstructed.

Together, they point to a third idea:

> **Reality may not be made of objects we can possess, but of procedures that regenerate appearances.**

### 1. The flower is not a substance. It is an event.

The deepest thing about lily-of-the-valley is not just “you cannot extract it.” It is that its scent is **not stored as a stable object waiting to be captured**. It happens. It is produced in time, in air, by a living plant, under spring conditions.

So the perfume problem is ontological: the scent is not a thing. It is a **release**.

That means the perfumer cannot obtain lily-of-the-valley by possession. They can only create a structure that makes the nose and brain re-enter the event. The perfume is not the flower’s body; it is a ritual for causing the flower’s ghost to appear.

That is already close to EML.

The EML operator does not “contain” sine, multiplication, logarithms, radicals, or trigonometry the way a box contains tools. It is not a warehouse. It is a **generative event**. By repeated composition, it causes familiar operations to appear. Your uploaded notes describe EML as a single operator that, with the constant 1, generates standard elementary functions through nested binary trees in the complex domain. 

So:

**Lily-of-the-valley scent is not extracted. It is re-performed.**
**Elementary functions in EML are not assumed. They are re-performed.**

That is the deeper shared structure.

### 2. Both destroy the fantasy of the “pure original.”

At first, synthetic lily-of-the-valley seems fake. But the natural flower refuses extraction. So the synthetic accord is not a cheap copy of a natural absolute. It is the only way the scent becomes portable.

The “fake” becomes the only possible artifact.

Likewise, ordinary math education teaches addition, subtraction, multiplication, sine, cosine, exponentials, and logs as if they are separate natural species. EML says: maybe those buttons are not the deep reality. Maybe they are convenient surface forms. Underneath them may be a generative grammar.

So EML makes ordinary operations look like perfumes.

Addition is an accord.
Multiplication is an accord.
Sine is an accord.
A radical is an accord.
A logarithm is an accord that later helps generate other accords.

The “natural” operation is revealed as constructed.

And the constructed thing is still real.

### 3. Lily-of-the-valley adds *absence* to EML.

EML, by itself, can sound triumphalist: “Look, one operation creates everything.” It can sound like conquest, like reduction, like mathematical colonialism.

Lily-of-the-valley changes the mood.

It says: the source of generativity is not always something you master. Sometimes the source remains untouchable. The original stays alive only by not being captured.

So lily-of-the-valley gives EML a necessary humility:

> A universal generator is not the same as owning the universe.

You can reconstruct the scent, but you have not captured the flower.
You can reconstruct operations, but you have not exhausted mathematics.

That matters because the uploaded notes themselves flag the limitation: EML is powerful for the “scientific calculator” sense of elementary functions, but not necessarily for every broader mathematical meaning of “elementary.” 

The lily prevents EML from becoming a totalizing myth.

### 4. EML adds *syntax* to lily-of-the-valley.

The flower gives you mystery, but EML gives you structure.

A perfumer’s muguet accord is not random. It is a grammar of impression: green, watery, floral, metallic, dewy, clean, fragile. None of those molecules alone is “the flower.” But their relations produce the recognisable whole.

EML formalizes that kind of magic.

It says: a world can be made from one rule plus nesting.

Not one ingredient.
One rule.

That is the key distinction.

Lily-of-the-valley perfume is not merely a list of molecules. It is molecules arranged so that perception crosses a threshold and says: “Yes. That.” EML expressions are not merely repeated symbols. They are nested so that computation crosses a threshold and becomes: “Yes. That is sine. That is multiplication. That is log.”

The formula is a perfumer.

### 5. The real bridge is not “synthetic.” It is “compositional.”

Both stories are about composition as a higher form of truth.

A naive view says:

> Real means direct.
> Fake means constructed.

But both EML and lily-of-the-valley break that binary.

A better view is:

> Real can be direct, but real can also be reconstructed by a faithful generative process.

This is huge.

It applies to mathematics, perfume, language, AI, music, ritual, memory, even identity.

A song is not “stored” in a piano. It appears when the keys are struck in the right order.
A sentence is not “inside” the alphabet. It appears when letters are composed.
A person’s presence is not “inside” a photograph. It appears through resemblance, memory, context.
Lily-of-the-valley is not inside the bottle. It appears through an accord.
Sine is not inside EML. It appears through a tree.

So one adds to the other this deeper claim:

> **Essence is sometimes not a substance but a successful recomposition.**

### 6. The poison matters.

Do not ignore the toxicity. It is not decorative.

Lily-of-the-valley is sweet, bridal, pure, springlike—and poisonous. That paradox is not separate from the EML connection. It is central.

EML is also beautiful and dangerous in its own domain. The uploaded notes mention deep trees, overflow from nested exponentials, branch-cut problems with complex logarithms, and the fact that trig expressions can become impractically large. 

Both are elegant at the surface and unstable underneath.

So both teach a discipline of handling.

Beauty requires technique.
