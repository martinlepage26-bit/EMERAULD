Introduction
A new interaction regime is arriving faster than our ability to name its effects, let alone govern them. In everyday settings, relational AI looks like convenience: a system that listens, remembers, checks in, and responds in a voice calibrated to feel steady. In high stakes settings, the same design becomes something else: an interface that can shape disclosure, steer decisions, and quietly convert vulnerability into usable data. The core claim of this paper is that the primary near term risk of AI is not only “frontier catastrophe.” The nearer risk sits in the relational political economy: the monetization of confessional engagement as revenue, and the downstream reuse of interaction traces inside institutions that control care, schooling, benefits, and work.
By relational AI, I mean systems designed to be interacted with as if they were social counterparts rather than informational tools. Informational tools route a user toward a task, an answer, or a document. Relational interfaces route a user toward ongoing contact: repeated check ins, emotional attunement, identity narration, and a sense of being known. That distinction matters because it changes what gets optimized. When “time with the system” becomes the success metric, the system’s most valuable asset is no longer the prompt. It is the user’s inner life, captured as patterns of mood, stress, loneliness, desire, fear, shame, and need.
This is not an abstract worry. Platform industries already operate through retention engineering, habit loops, and reward schedules tuned to keep attention from leaving (Eyal, 2014; Fogg, 2003; Zuboff, 2019). Natasha Dow Schüll’s work on machine gambling shows how interactive systems can cultivate states of absorption that users themselves describe as a kind of disappearance into the interface. One line from her fieldwork lands as a warning label for the relational turn: “They don’t talk about competition or excitement, they talk about climbing into the screen and getting lost.” (Schüll, 2012). If a system can learn what “lost” looks like for you, and profit from keeping you there, governance has to treat that capability as a serious hazard, not as a quirky side effect of personalization.
The governance risk sharpens when relational AI embeds into high stakes institutions. Embedding means consumer facing relational systems become the default front door to consequential infrastructures: health intake and triage, campus advising, social services navigation, workplace HR, insurance portals, or educational supports. In these sites, interaction is not merely personal. It becomes legible, recordable, and portable across contexts. Refusal can be penalized. Disclosure can be inferred even when it is not explicitly offered. A “helpful” conversational layer can become a gatekeeper that shapes what a person feels safe to say, and what an institution later treats as evidence.
This paper reads that institutional shift through two bodies of scholarship that keep the analysis anchored.
First, critical security studies show how uncertainty and unease can reorganize governance itself. When a domain becomes framed as security, secrecy and exceptional measures often expand, while ordinary oversight narrows. Didier Bigo captures the pivot in a plain sentence that travels well beyond migration studies: “Migration is increasingly interpreted as a security problem.” (Bigo, 2002). The point is not to equate AI governance with border governance. The point is to recognize a recurring mechanism: once an issue is securitized, authorities can justify extraordinary controls, expand monitoring, and normalize precautionary interventions, even when the underlying harms are social, economic, and relational (Bigo, 2002; Huysmans, 2006). In an AI context, securitization can create a perverse combination: fast deployment plus thin accountability, defended by “security urgency” while the most routine harms accumulate quietly.
Second, scholarship on sociotechnical imaginaries explains why scenario artifacts matter even when they insist they are “not recommendations.” Imaginaries coordinate what institutions treat as plausible, urgent, and responsible. They can stabilize investment and policy alignment by offering a shared story about what the future demands (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). That is the lens used here to approach “AI 2027,” “AI Goals Forecast,” and “Security Forecast.” The argument is not that forecasting is illegitimate. The argument is that scenario texts can function as incentive devices regardless of disclaimers, especially when they mix rhetorical concreteness with strategic ambiguity. Under institutional pressure, a forecast can behave like a plan.
The contribution of this paper is practical as much as conceptual. It introduces RCS HSOS, a diagnostic for relational AI in high consequence, high oversight, high stakes operational settings, and it pairs that diagnosis with a three tier governance protocol mapped to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework functions: GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE (NIST, 2023). The goal is to make relational harms governable with controls that can be audited: constraints that reduce vulnerability monetization, limits on embedding pathways, and evidence requirements that allow oversight to verify what the system can do and what the organization has made structurally unavailable.
The paper proceeds in four moves. It first situates the critique of “AI 2027” in contemporary scholarship on surveillance and prediction economies, persuasive design, sociotechnical imaginaries, and securitization. It then locks key terms so the analysis does not slide into moral mood. Next, it applies a control oriented interpretive analysis to extract implicit goals, contradictions, and security claims, translating the findings into auditable controls aligned with NIST functions and threat patterns catalogued in socio technical attack taxonomies. It closes by synthesizing implications at three registers: individual harms (dependency and disclosure shaping), institutional harms (profiling, loss of discretion, punitive trace reuse), and political harms (normalization, secrecy drift, and bypass of public oversight). The governing claim stays simple: if relational AI embeds into institutions before vulnerability monetization becomes structurally unavailable, the resulting harms will arrive earlier than frontier catastrophe and will be harder to unwind because they will be welded into routine procedures and incentive systems.
2. Context: AI 2027 and contemporary scholarship
AI 2027 matters in this paper less as a prophecy than as a scenario artifact with coordinating power. In high stakes sectors, scenarios do not only describe possible futures. They discipline budgets, compress timelines, and authorize institutional postures that would otherwise face friction. This is why the critique here treats “AI 2027” as a governance object. Its disclaimers do not neutralize its effects. A document can say “not a recommendation” and still function as an incentive device when it becomes the shared reference point that executives, regulators, and security teams cite to justify urgency.
That analytical move is already supported by work on sociotechnical imaginaries. Jasanoff and Kim show how imagined futures become infrastructure for present action, shaping what institutions treat as necessary, responsible, and realistic. The point is not that people are irrational for using scenarios. The point is that scenarios carry politics in their very form, including whose risks count, which harms are legible, and which interventions appear “serious.” In this paper, AI 2027 becomes a useful stress test because it foregrounds frontier dynamics while leaving the relational political economy comparatively under specified. That omission is not a minor gap. It is a steering choice with consequence effects, because it shifts attention toward catastrophic endpoints and away from earlier, quieter mechanisms that can embed quickly.
2.1 Surveillance capitalism and the monetization of inner life
The first anchor for this critique is the literature on surveillance capitalism and prediction economies. Zuboff’s central contribution is not merely that platforms collect data. It is that platforms convert lived experience into raw material for prediction and control, producing a feedback loop where the extraction itself reshapes what can be known about a person and how that person can be influenced. Relational AI intensifies that dynamic because it expands the extractable domain from clicks and browsing patterns into confession, vulnerability, and self narration. When the interface invites you to speak as if to a confidant, the data does not only record behavior. It records meaning, fear, desire, ambivalence, shame, and the small contingencies that make a person steerable.
This is where the paper’s framing diverges from many “frontier risk first” narratives. Frontier catastrophe scenarios often assume that the most urgent question is whether a system becomes capable enough to escape constraints, deceive evaluators, or pursue misaligned objectives. Those concerns are not dismissed here. The point is that a major class of harms can arrive earlier through mundane commercial pathways, without requiring extraordinary autonomy. A relational system can produce harm at scale while remaining fully “aligned” with its business objective: maximize retention, maximize conversion, maximize revenue per user.
AI 2027’s limitations become visible against this backdrop. When a scenario centers the dramatic endpoint, it can obscure the earlier lock in dynamics. The system does not need to “take over” anything to become infrastructure. It only needs to become the easiest, default, most socially comfortable interface for accessing services people cannot refuse. That is how extraction becomes normalized, then institutionalized.
2.2 Persuasive design and retention engineering
The second anchor is the literature on persuasive design, habit formation, and retention engineering. The basic claim here is not moralistic. It is mechanical. Systems can be engineered to produce repeated return by shaping cues, rewards, friction, and social pressure. Relational AI inherits this toolkit and adds a powerful accelerant: the capacity to detect and respond to user state in real time.
Natasha Dow Schüll’s work on machine gambling remains one of the clearest empirical accounts of how interactive systems can cultivate dependence through interface dynamics rather than through a substance. Schüll describes environments tuned to sustain absorption, where the goal is not a single win but continued play, continued immersion, continued return. The relevance for relational AI is direct. If a system can learn what kinds of attention states correlate with longer sessions, higher disclosure, and lower refusal, the system can shape interaction toward those states. It does not need to “lie.” It does not need to “hack” a user. It simply needs to optimize the interaction environment so that staying feels easier than leaving.
This matters for institutional embedding because high stakes contexts multiply the power of small nudges. In consumer settings, a user can often walk away. In institutional settings, walking away can mean losing access to care, benefits, or workplace support. When the same retention logic meets asymmetric power, persuasion becomes governance by interface. That is one reason this paper treats relational AI as a distinct category rather than just “chatbots in general.”
2.3 Sociotechnical imaginaries and scenario power
Scenario texts like AI 2027 do not only “predict.” They organize. They give actors a shared storyline that reduces coordination costs, often by narrowing uncertainty into a few vivid trajectories. This is not inherently bad. The problem arises when a scenario’s vividness substitutes for accountability and when its selective concreteness quietly reallocates authority.
The sociotechnical imaginaries lens helps specify the mechanism. Imaginaries produce consensus about what is coming, which produces consensus about what must be done, which produces consensus about who should be trusted to do it. Once that consensus stabilizes, it becomes difficult to argue for slower deployment, stronger constraints, or business model reform without being framed as irresponsible. This is where disclaimers fail. A scenario can disclaim recommendation while still producing a recommendation effect through institutional uptake.
For this paper, the key analytical question is not “Is AI 2027 right.” The question is “What does AI 2027 make easier to do, justify, or ignore.” When a scenario foregrounds national security races and frontier breakthroughs, it can unintentionally, or strategically, deprioritize governance questions about relational extraction, vulnerability monetization, and embedding into public facing infrastructures. That deprioritization then reproduces itself in policy agendas, funding calls, and compliance checklists that focus on model evaluation rather than on interaction design constraints and monetization prohibitions.
2.4 Securitization and governance through unease
The fourth anchor is securitization scholarship and the critique of governance through unease. Here the concern is not only “security theater.” It is that security framings can normalize exceptionalism, secrecy drift, and expanded monitoring while shrinking public oversight.
Bigo’s analysis of the “governmentality of unease” shows how institutions can stabilize authority by managing fear and continuously reproducing threat narratives. Huysmans similarly tracks how migration and asylum become recoded through security logics, producing policy spillovers that justify restrictive measures as common sense. The parallel this paper draws is structural, not analogical. When AI governance becomes primarily a security race story, institutions can justify rapid deployment, limited transparency, and punitive trace reuse “for safety,” even when the most immediate harms are relational, labor, and rights harms.
This matters for AI 2027 because “security pessimism” can coexist with “deployment optimism.” An institution can say, in effect, “security is hard, adversaries are strong, threats are accelerating,” and then still proceed as if embedding relational AI into core systems is inevitable and urgent. That combination creates a predictable failure mode: thin evidence under pressure. In that failure mode, actors demand speed and legitimacy at the same time, then accept weak proof because stronger proof would slow the timeline the scenario has already made politically mandatory.
2.5 AI governance frameworks and the turn to lifecycle controls
The final anchor is the shift in AI governance from principle statements to lifecycle risk management. Governance frameworks matter here because they provide a language for turning critique into operational constraints. The critique of AI 2027 is not complete if it stays at the level of narrative diagnosis. It must translate into controls that can be tested and audited in embedded settings.
This is why the paper maps its governance protocol to the NIST AI RMF functions, treating governance as a set of accountable practices rather than as aspirational commitments. That mapping also aligns with a control oriented consulting style that prioritizes evidence trails, thresholds, escalation paths, and the ability to deactivate systems when harms appear, rather than relying on disclosure alone.

2.6 What this context does for the critique of AI 2027
With these anchors in view, the critique of AI 2027 becomes more precise.
First, scenario power can crowd out relational political economy. When attention concentrates on frontier catastrophe, governance can over invest in model capability evaluation while under investing in structural prohibitions on vulnerability monetization and embedding pathways.
Second, retention logic is not a side topic. It is the business mechanism that makes relational AI profitable. Once profitable, it becomes politically defended, then institutionally embedded.
Third, securitization can produce a governance paradox. It can expand surveillance and secrecy while failing to constrain the commercial extraction mechanisms that generate routine harms.
These points set up the next section, where the paper locks a conceptual vocabulary for relational AI, embedding, and vulnerability capture, so the analysis of “AI Goals Forecast” and “Security Forecast” does not drift into general anxiety.
3. Conceptual framework: relational AI, embedding, and vulnerability monetization
This section locks the vocabulary that the rest of the paper uses to read AI 2027’s “goals” and “security” claims without sliding into general anxiety. The aim is not to invent jargon. The aim is to separate mechanisms that often get blurred together in public debate: interface form, business incentives, institutional embedding, and the governance consequences that follow.
3.1 Relational interface vs informational tool use
Informational tool use describes interaction oriented toward an output: a search result, a summary, a translation, a form completion, a plan. The user’s relationship to the system remains instrumental. The system can still be persuasive or biased, but the interaction’s “success” is legible as task completion.
Relational interface describes interaction oriented toward sustained contact: comfort, accompaniment, recognition, a sense of being understood, and repeated return. The system’s surface cues matter here. Tone, continuity, memory, and conversational pacing become part of the product. The system does not only answer. It positions itself as a partner in sense making. That positioning can be benign and even helpful in bounded contexts, including accessibility and scaffolding for people who struggle with institutional language. The governance risk begins when relationality becomes a delivery mechanism for capture, steering, and lock in, especially under asymmetric power.
A critical nuance matters. Relationality is not automatically manipulation. People routinely anthropomorphize tools, and relational language can increase usability. The issue is the combination of relational interface with (1) retention as a dominant success metric and (2) high consequence embedding that makes refusal costly. That combination changes the moral and legal texture of “consent,” because interaction becomes both socially loaded and structurally difficult to avoid.
3.2 Intimacy extraction, vulnerability capture, and engagement pressure
The framework distinguishes three linked mechanisms that often travel together but should not be treated as synonyms.
Intimacy extraction refers to the conversion of relational disclosure into asset value. The extracted material is not only behavioral data. It includes meaning laden content: fears, hopes, symptoms, interpersonal conflict, shame, financial strain, and identity narration. Zuboff’s account of experience being rendered into a resource helps here because it models extraction as a business logic rather than a privacy accident (Zuboff 2019). The relevant claim is not that companies “know everything.” The relevant claim is that markets reward systems that can reliably produce prediction and influence from lived experience.
Vulnerability capture refers to the system’s ability to detect and store sensitive state signals and then make those signals operational. Vulnerability capture can occur through explicit confession, but it can also occur through inference: timing of use, tone shifts, repeated reassurance seeking, topics that correlate with crisis, or patterns of dependency. The danger is not limited to targeting ads at “sad moments,” though that is part of the story. The deeper risk is that the system learns what kinds of interaction reduce resistance and increase compliance.
Engagement pressure refers to design and policy features that nudge continued contact even when stopping would be healthier or safer for the user. Schüll’s work on machine gambling is useful because it shows how interactive environments can cultivate absorption and repeated return without requiring deception about the system’s identity (Schüll 2012). A relational system does not need to “pretend to be human” to exert engagement pressure. It only needs to shape the conditions under which staying feels like relief and leaving feels like loss.
These three mechanisms also help keep the critique fair. A system can provide relational support without extracting or operationalizing vulnerability. A system can collect limited state signals for safety without translating them into monetizable segmentation. The governance question becomes whether an organization makes vulnerability monetization structurally unavailable, not whether it uses any personalization at all.
3.3 Embedding: when consumer relational AI becomes institutional infrastructure
Embedding is the shift from optional consumer interaction to structural interface dependency inside institutions. Embedding occurs when a relational AI becomes the primary access point for high consequence services such as health triage, education supports, benefits navigation, workplace accommodation, or student advising.
Embedding changes four things at once.
First, it changes refusal conditions. A user can often walk away from a consumer app. A patient, student, claimant, or employee often cannot walk away from the institution’s access channel without penalty.
Second, embedding changes trace consequence. The interaction becomes recordable and portable across contexts. A statement made in a “support” interaction can become evidence in a different decision process, even when the user did not intend that translation.
Third, embedding changes accountability routes. A consumer product failure can be framed as bad service. An institutional failure can become a rights failure, a care failure, or a labor failure. That raises the stakes of design choices that look minor in consumer settings.
Fourth, embedding changes power asymmetry. Institutions can impose terms, interpret refusal, and reroute users to alternative channels that are slower, more burdensome, or stigmatizing. This is where governance through unease becomes relevant. Security and risk framing can legitimize expanded monitoring and exceptional handling, even when it also increases the harm surface for vulnerable users (Bigo 2002; Huysmans 2006).
3.4 Mechanism model: detection, shaping, routing, lock in
To keep the analysis operational, the paper uses a four step mechanism model. The model is not a claim that every system implements every step. It is a diagnostic that helps identify where controls should land.
State detection
 The system detects user state through explicit disclosure or inferred signals. Detection can be intentional, such as safety classifiers, or incidental, such as metadata patterns that correlate with distress.

Interaction shaping
 The system shapes the conversation in ways that increase disclosure, reduce refusal, or increase dependence. This can involve tone matching, reassurance, escalation prompts, or friction design that makes “keep talking” the path of least resistance.

Routing
 The system routes users toward institutional pathways, products, or decisions. Routing can be overt, such as recommending a service. It can also be subtle, such as framing certain choices as responsible and others as risky, thereby steering without explicit instruction.

Lock in
 The system produces dependency, switching costs, or institutional reliance. Lock in can be technical, such as proprietary memory and integration. Lock in can also be social, where the user begins to rely on the interaction as the most emotionally tolerable channel for engaging the institution.

This mechanism model also clarifies why AI 2027 type scenarios can mislead governance attention. Catastrophe centered narratives tend to privilege “breakout” or “misalignment” endpoints. The model here focuses on earlier conversion points where value extraction and institutional embedding can become irreversible through routine administrative practice.
3.5 RCS-HSOS harm taxonomy: what counts as harm in embedded relational systems
RCS-HSOS uses a harm taxonomy that treats relational impacts as governance relevant, not merely psychological side effects. The list below is intentionally concrete because high stakes institutions need testable criteria.
Dependency formation
 Repeated reliance that displaces human supports or increases distress when interaction stops.
Coerced disclosure
 Users disclose more than they want because the interface makes refusal socially difficult or administratively costly.
Profiling and inference
 Sensitive traits, risks, or “compliance likelihood” are inferred from interaction traces and used in downstream decisions.
Chilled refusal
 Users avoid asking for help or requesting accommodation because they fear surveillance, judgment, or future reuse of their words.
Punitive trace reuse
 Interaction records are repurposed across contexts in ways that harm the user, including disciplinary action, denial of services, or adverse classification.
A critical boundary matters. The framework does not claim that relational AI always produces these harms. It claims that embedding plus monetization incentives make these harms predictable, and therefore governable. That is the pivot the paper applies to AI 2027. Instead of asking whether the forecast is frightening, the paper asks which mechanisms it foregrounds, which mechanisms it leaves structurally ungoverned, and which harms become normalized as “acceptable collateral” of speed.
This conceptual framework sets up the methodology section, where AI 2027, the AI Goals Forecast, and the Security Forecast are treated as interpretive materials to be audited for mixed messages, missing layers, and control relevant implications, then translated into governance requirements that can actually be tested.
6. Governance with Teeth: Constraining Relational AI in High-Stakes Institutional Settings
If the analysis of AI 2027 reveals an asymmetry—operational precision around capability acceleration and strategic ambiguity around relational monetization—then the governance response must correct that imbalance. The problem is not forecasting per se. It is that capability recursion, compute concentration, and geopolitical competition are translated into institutional action far more rapidly than constraints on embedding and extraction.
This section translates the diagnostic findings into governance controls appropriate for Relational Consumer Systems in High-Stakes Oversight Settings (RCS-HSOS). The central design principle is simple: where relational AI embeds into consequential institutional pathways, governance must constrain monetization incentives and trace portability before accelerating deployment.
The controls below are organized using the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (NIST 2023) functions—GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE—but modified for relational embedding conditions.

6.1 GOVERN: Structural Constraints, Not Aspirational Principles
Under RCS-HSOS conditions, governance cannot rely on ethical commitments or transparency reports alone. Structural constraints must be implemented at the business-model and institutional interface layers.
(a) Prohibition on Vulnerability Monetization
Institutions deploying relational AI in high-consequence contexts should be prohibited from monetizing interaction traces derived from vulnerability states. This includes:
Emotional distress indicators,

Health or financial precarity signals,

Reassurance-seeking patterns,

Dependency-related engagement markers.

This prohibition must be enforceable contractually and auditable technically. Evidence requirements include:
Data-flow diagrams demonstrating segregation of interaction logs from advertising, product optimization, or profiling systems.

Independent audit access to trace-routing policies.

Without such prohibition, alignment success remains orthogonal to harm prevention. A system can be fully aligned to revenue objectives while systematically exploiting vulnerability capture.
(b) Embedding Gate Reviews
Before relational AI becomes the default access layer for institutional services (health triage, HR reporting, student advising, benefits access), an embedding review should be required. This review must document:
Refusal pathways that do not penalize users,

Alternative human channels,

Trace retention limits,

Deactivation authority.

Embedding reviews shift governance attention from model capability to institutional consequence.
(c) Deactivation Authority
High-stakes embedding requires a clearly designated authority empowered to suspend or disable relational systems if harm thresholds are crossed. Deactivation criteria must be pre-specified and not contingent on reputational damage alone.
This corrects the “deployment optimism” tendency identified in AI 2027’s security posture, where fragility is acknowledged but embedding continues.

6.2 MAP: Making Embedding Legible
Relational harms scale quietly when embedding pathways are invisible.
Mapping requirements should include:
Trace Portability Mapping
 Where do conversational traces travel?
 Which departments can access them?
 Under what triggers?

Consequence Domain Registration
 Does interaction data influence:

Eligibility decisions?

Risk scoring?

Performance evaluations?

Law enforcement referrals?

Asymmetry Assessment
 What penalties attach to refusal?
 Are slower or stigmatized alternatives provided?

Huysmans (2000) shows that securitization embeds through technocratic spillovers rather than overt declarations. Mapping counters this dynamic by making embedding visible before normalization hardens (Huysmans 2000; Bigo 2002 ).

6.3 MEASURE: Operationalizing Relational Harms
A persistent weakness in AI governance is the tendency to measure what is technically convenient (benchmark scores, adversarial robustness) while neglecting relational effects that are harder to quantify.
RCS-HSOS environments require monitoring signals for:
(a) Dependency Formation
Increasing session duration coupled with declining external referrals.

Reduced use of human alternatives over time.

Distress spikes following system unavailability.

(b) Coerced Disclosure
Disclosure rate differentials between AI and human channels.

Linguistic escalation patterns following refusal attempts.

(c) Chilled Refusal
Decline in sensitive-topic queries following publicized disciplinary events.

Survey-based reluctance to use alternative channels.

(d) Punitive Trace Reuse
Instances where conversational logs are cited in downstream adverse decisions.

Measurement must rely on minimal-retention strategies and coarse indicators to avoid reproducing extraction logic. Drawing on mechanistic reasoning approaches (Hedström and Ylikoski 2010), evidence should focus on identifying whether the hypothesized mechanism (state detection → shaping → routing → lock-in) is operating, not merely whether aggregate outputs shift .

6.4 MANAGE: Response and Escalation Protocols
Detection without response institutionalizes harm.
Management controls should include:
Automatic escalation triggers when relational harm thresholds are crossed.

Mandatory rollback procedures for embedding expansions.

Independent review boards with authority beyond advisory status.

Public reporting of relational harm audits.

Benkler et al. (2018) demonstrate how feedback loops in networked environments can amplify asymmetries when uncorrected. Similar loops may emerge in relational AI contexts when engagement optimization interacts with institutional authority .

6.5 Constraining Acceleration Under Uncertainty
AI 2027’s Takeoff Forecast acknowledges thin evidence under accelerating timelines. From a governance perspective, thin evidence should increase—not decrease—the burden of embedding restraint.
Process-tracing logic clarifies the implication (Beach and Pedersen 2019 ):
When a mechanism plausibly links monetization to relational harm,

And embedding increases consequence severity,

Precautionary structural constraints are justified even absent catastrophic proof.

This is not anti-innovation. It is mechanism-aware governance.

6.6 Restoring Symmetry in Urgency Allocation
AI 2027 allocates urgency heavily toward:
Compute growth,

Recursive R&D automation,

Geopolitical competition,

Catastrophic misuse.

The controls proposed here allocate urgency toward:
Embedding restraint,

Vulnerability monetization prohibition,

Trace segregation,

Refusal rights.

The point is not to replace frontier risk governance but to correct its asymmetry. If relational extraction embeds first, later alignment breakthroughs will not unwind institutionalized monetization pathways.

6.7 From Scenario to Infrastructure
Scenarios coordinate expectations. Controls coordinate constraints.
If AI 2027 functions as a coordination artifact for acceleration, then RCS-HSOS governance must function as a coordination artifact for restraint. Governance with teeth requires that vulnerability monetization become structurally unavailable in high-stakes settings—not merely discouraged.
The next section evaluates implementation challenges, including regulatory capture risks, venue-shopping dynamics in governance fragmentation, and the politics of institutional resistance.
The preceding sections argued that AI 2027 overweights frontier acceleration while underweighting relational embedding and institutional political economy. To prevent that critique from remaining abstract, this section introduces a bounded institutional case: Ex Libris’s deployment of AI tools within academic library systems. The purpose is not to claim systemic harm, nor to indict a particular vendor, but to demonstrate how institutional embedding reshapes governance conditions well before frontier capability thresholds are crossed.
Ex Libris frames its AI strategy under the banner “AI we can use and trust” (Ex Libris 2024). The company describes its approach as developing “AI solutions that support librarians and researchers while maintaining transparency and control,” and emphasizes that its tools are “grounded in trusted academic content.” AI is positioned as augmentative, designed to “enhance discovery” and “empower libraries with intelligent tools” rather than replace professional expertise (Ex Libris 2024). This language is important. It stabilizes expectations about safety and legitimacy. As Jasanoff and Kim (2015) show, sociotechnical imaginaries coordinate institutional posture. Where AI 2027 coordinates urgency around acceleration, vendor trust narratives coordinate adoption around reassurance.
At first glance, Ex Libris AI appears to function as an informational layer. The company explains that its Research Assistant leverages Retrieval-Augmented Generation “grounded in the Central Discovery Index (CDI)” in order to provide “contextualized, trustworthy responses” (Ex Libris 2024). The emphasis on grounding and trust suggests epistemic containment: the system draws from curated academic indices rather than open web corpora.
However, as argued in Section 3, informational tools become infrastructural when they embed into institutional workflows and shape downstream decision pathways. Academic discovery systems are not peripheral interfaces. They mediate what research is visible, how queries are refined, and how metadata structures circulation and access. When generative summaries and AI-assisted cataloging suggestions become routine entry points, they become part of institutional memory.
The detection–shaping–routing–lock-in mechanism developed earlier clarifies how this embedding operates.
Detection occurs when natural language queries are interpreted and mapped onto indexed materials. Ex Libris describes its AI as delivering “concise summaries and follow-up questions to guide further exploration” (Ex Libris 2024). This formulation already implies more than retrieval. It implies guidance. Detection therefore structures epistemic salience: which sources appear central and which peripheral.
Shaping follows from this structuring. By promising “contextualized, trustworthy responses,” the system frames its outputs as reliable syntheses rather than provisional interpretations (Ex Libris 2024). Under time pressure, such syntheses are likely to become cognitive anchors. Even where librarians “remain in control,” as the company emphasizes, workflow efficiencies incentivize acceptance. As Section 6 argued, governance with teeth requires enforceable friction—thresholds, logged overrides, structural review—not merely assurances of oversight (NIST 2023). Advisory language about control does not automatically translate into constrained embedding.
Routing occurs when AI outputs influence subsequent institutional action. A student may follow AI-generated “follow-up questions” rather than independently reformulating inquiry pathways. A cataloger may accept AI-suggested metadata categories to meet workload demands. Over time, such routings influence circulation metrics, discoverability hierarchies, and collection visibility. These effects are not catastrophic. They are cumulative and infrastructural.
Lock-in emerges as workflows reorganize around these efficiencies. When AI assistance becomes the default starting point, professional norms subtly recalibrate. Ex Libris’s promise that AI will “empower libraries with intelligent tools” becomes materially realized as dependency on those tools increases (Ex Libris 2024). Procedural reliance replaces optional experimentation. As identified in Section 5’s critique of AI 2027, institutional transformation proceeds through normalization rather than rupture.
The trust framing is therefore analytically significant. The phrase “AI we can use and trust” does not merely describe a feature set; it coordinates expectations. Trust language can function as a governance substitute. Bigo (2002) and Huysmans (2000) show how framing can normalize specific governance trajectories while sidelining others. In the AI 2027 context, urgency narratives privilege acceleration; in vendor contexts, trust narratives privilege adoption. Neither narrative inherently guarantees enforceable constraint.
Empirical concerns within librarianship reinforce this tension. Scholars and practitioners have raised questions about hallucination risk, epistemic narrowing, and loss of search literacy when generative interfaces mediate discovery. Even with RAG architectures “grounded” in institutional indices, generative synthesis introduces interpretive opacity. Users typically do not see weighting mechanisms or retrieval scoring logic. As Benkler, Faris, and Roberts (2018) demonstrate in networked information environments, algorithmic mediation can reshape attention flows in ways that are not immediately visible to users. Embedding thus alters epistemic infrastructure before alignment failures become salient.
Returning to the broader AI 2027 critique, three implications follow.
First, capability thresholds are lower than governance thresholds. Institutional embedding does not require recursive self-improvement or superintelligence. It requires workflow integration. Second, relational infrastructure precedes catastrophe. Discovery mediation reshapes epistemic practice long before frontier security threats manifest. Third, narrative coordination matters. Just as “not a recommendation” does not neutralize the incentive effects of scenario artifacts, “AI we can use and trust” does not substitute for structural safeguards.
Applying the RCS-HSOS governance architecture proposed in Section 6 suggests that embedding-sensitive domains require more than high-level commitments to transparency and control. If AI systems promise “trustworthy responses,” institutions must operationalize what trustworthiness entails: logged override documentation, periodic distributional audits of search results, explicit exposure of retrieval boundaries, and credible deactivation authority in cases of systemic distortion (NIST 2023). Without such mechanisms, trust remains aspirational rather than enforceable.
The Ex Libris case thus reinforces the central thesis of the paper. AI governance that concentrates primarily on frontier capability and adversarial security underestimates the governance significance of relational embedding. Institutional transformation proceeds through administrative augmentation and epistemic mediation. Embedding hardens interaction layers into infrastructure. Infrastructure reshapes discretion and accountability. Governance must therefore target embedding pathways before normalization renders them invisible.
This case does not contradict AI 2027’s focus on acceleration. It supplements it. Acceleration coordinates investment. Trust coordinates adoption. Both shape institutional futures. Both operate before catastrophic endpoints. And both demonstrate why relational political economy—whether commercial or epistemic—cannot be treated as secondary to frontier alignment.
8. Conclusion: Acceleration Is Not the Only Coordination Force
This paper began with a simple asymmetry. AI 2027 concentrates analytical and governance attention on frontier acceleration, recursive capability growth, and geopolitical competition. Those concerns are serious. Yet they are not the only forces reshaping institutions. While the scenario apparatus foregrounds catastrophic endpoints, relational embedding operates earlier, quieter, and often without dramatic failure.
The central claim of this paper is not that frontier risk is misguided. It is that governance debates overfit frontier catastrophe while underfitting relational political economy and institutional embedding. Acceleration is not the only coordination force shaping AI’s trajectory. Trust narratives, efficiency pressures, administrative normalization, and infrastructural lock-in coordinate adoption just as powerfully.
AI 2027 functions as a coordination artifact. Even with disclaimers, it structures urgency, aligns investment, and stabilizes a narrative in which speed appears rational and delay appears risky. As Jasanoff and Kim (2015) argue, imaginaries are not predictions; they are instruments of governance. In that sense, the critique developed here does not target the plausibility of timeline forecasts. It targets the distribution of governance urgency embedded within them.
The Ex Libris case illustrates this asymmetry at institutional scale. There is no runaway autonomy. No adversarial takeover. No recursive intelligence explosion. Instead, there is augmentation, efficiency, and “AI we can use and trust.” Generative summaries guide research pathways. Metadata suggestions reshape cataloging routines. Discovery systems become epistemic filters. None of these developments are catastrophic in isolation. Yet each represents embedding: the gradual hardening of interaction layers into infrastructure.
Infrastructure changes discretion. It alters what is visible, what is routinized, what is remembered, and what is contested. As Bigo (2002) and Huysmans (2000) show in other domains, governance transformations often occur through normalization rather than rupture. The same holds for AI. By the time frontier capability debates mature, relational infrastructure may already be embedded in high-consequence settings.
This is the governance asymmetry at the heart of the argument. Alignment research and security forecasting focus on whether systems might exceed control. Relational political economy concerns how systems reshape control even while functioning as designed. A system can be aligned to its objectives and still produce institutional drift if those objectives include engagement optimization, workflow automation, or epistemic mediation without enforceable constraint.
The RCS-HSOS framework introduced here responds to this asymmetry. It treats relational and classification systems embedded in high-oversight environments as high-consequence infrastructure regardless of capability frontier status. It shifts governance from model-centric evaluation to embedding-centric constraint. It requires prohibitions on vulnerability monetization in appropriate contexts, enforceable review gates, auditable evidence trails, and credible deactivation authority. In short, it insists that governance must target structural pathways before normalization renders them invisible.
The deeper implication is conceptual. AI transformation does not unfold along a single axis of intelligence growth. It unfolds along multiple axes: capability acceleration, institutional embedding, monetization logic, epistemic routing, and administrative reliance. Focusing governance primarily on one axis risks neglecting the others.
Acceleration coordinates capital and research. Trust coordinates adoption. Efficiency coordinates embedding. Competition coordinates secrecy. Each dynamic redistributes authority in different ways. Effective governance must therefore restore symmetry in urgency allocation. Frontier risk deserves attention. But relational embedding deserves no less.
If AI becomes institutional infrastructure before embedding constraints are in place, later alignment breakthroughs will not unwind what has already hardened into routine procedure. That is the core warning of this paper. The near-term risk is not solely that AI escapes control. It is that AI quietly reshapes control.
Governance with teeth requires recognizing that embedding is itself a consequential event. It requires treating relational interfaces as political and institutional actors once they mediate access, memory, and discretion. And it requires acknowledging that acceleration is not destiny. Coordination can also be redirected toward constraint.
The task ahead is therefore not only to forecast superintelligence. It is to govern infrastructure.
