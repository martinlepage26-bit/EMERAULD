The Sealed Card Protocol: Mediated Legitimacy, Charging, and Governance at the Seam
Abstract
This paper introduces the Sealed Card Protocol, an instrument for making mediated legitimacy observable under generative conditions. It begins from the glitch as a perceptual threshold event: the moment mediation becomes narratable and evaluation pivots from artifact to pathway. Rather than treating AI debates as capability disputes or moral arguments about authenticity, the protocol reframes them as governance disputes about pathway authorization under consequence pressure. It locks a disciplined vocabulary, distinguishing glitch, seam, pathway, charging, legibility, and legitimacy so evaluators cannot let criteria drift.
Methodologically, the protocol couples three practice-based arms to expose the same conversion mechanism under different proof regimes. The artistic arm examines HEXA and Wheels of Will as controlled variation evidence of how charging adheres or collapses in platform circulation when optimization becomes perceptible. The academic arm uses procedural autoethnography as bounded evidence trail design, retaining drafts, prompts, decision points, and verification steps to test whether pathway inspectability stabilizes trust or becomes punitive exposure. The ritual arm treats witch bottles as an evidentiary edge case that tracks translation across archaeology, museum display, contemporary practice, and platform circulation, revealing proof-regime mismatch and legitimacy redistribution. Across arms, the paper argues for a shift from purity to procedure: accountable, traceable mediation with proportional standards that reduce both epistemic risk and legitimacy harm.
Introduction
The glitch is the tell: the moment a reader senses the machine in a sentence or a viewer senses optimization in an image, and the encounter changes texture. The artifact may still be compelling, even persuasive, yet evaluation migrates. Attention moves away from what the work is doing toward how it was made, who can be held responsible for it, and whether the pathway is authorized. Understanding glitch as a perceptual threshold event, not a technical error, clarifies why evaluation pivots from artifact to pathway. This paper begins from that small perceptual stop and treats it as data rather than embarrassment. It reads the glitch as a threshold event where recognition can either convert into durable legitimacy or be rerouted into suspicion about mediation.
Public arguments about generative AI are often staged as capability disputes. Can these systems write, draw, or speak well enough to count. That framing mislocates the pressure point. The sharper problem is institutional and cultural, not purely technical. The struggle is over accountability and the conversion of recognition into credit once mediation becomes newly visible and newly nameable. Under generative conditions, audiences and institutions ask who did what, what counts as work, what counts as evidence, and where sanction or praise should land. These questions feel moral because they are asked in the language of authenticity, but they function as governance because they decide what is authorized, what is disqualified, and what kinds of people can be recognized as legitimate makers.
This paper refuses a familiar shortcut. It does not ask whether AI corrupts authenticity, as if authenticity were the absence of mediation or the presence of a pure interior voice. It treats authenticity and legitimacy as negotiated readings of mediation (Taylor, 1991), stabilized or destabilized at interfaces: peer review, platform formatting, citation regimes, aesthetic conventions, disclosure rules, and the everyday thresholds through which a public decides whether a work feels handled or feels engineered. As Charles Taylor notes, authenticity has become “a powerful moral ideal” shaping modern self-understanding (Taylor, 1991: 25). The same artifact, the object produced under evaluation, can be praised for clarity while the pathway that produced that clarity is treated as contamination. The same work can be received as convincing while its maker is rendered suspect. The glitch is where enforceability finds an attachment point.
To keep the analysis operational rather than metaphysical, the paper introduces charging as action rather than charge as substance. Charging names the social work by which meaning adheres so that an artifact can survive circulation as real enough to trust, even when proof is incomplete. Charging can accumulate through repetition, narrative framing, marks of use, aesthetic coherence, and procedural traceability. Charging can also glitch. It can spike when an artifact is read as intense without being explainable. It can implode when legitimacy collapses around a detected seam, even if the artifact remains persuasive. It can dissolve when work remains visible but becomes socially weightless, present yet not granted the right to count. The question is not whether AI can produce artifacts. The question is whether artifacts produced through hybrid pipelines can still be charged in ways that publics and institutions will recognize as legitimate, and what happens when legitimacy is withdrawn at the seam.
The paper’s vocabulary is deliberately tight because contemporary debate is repeatedly derailed by vague moral language that lets evaluators shift criteria without admitting they have shifted. Glitch names the perceptual threshold where mediation becomes a plausible story an audience can tell about what they are seeing. Seam names the interface where evaluative attention flips from artifact to pathway. A pathway is the reconstructible route of production, including tool use, human decisions, and verification steps, not “AI use” as a binary. Charging names the allocation of credibility that allows meaning to adhere under circulation. Legibility names the degree to which an artifact fits the reading habits and proof expectations of a regime, including genre expectations, tempo expectations, tone expectations, and acceptable forms of self-presentation. Legitimacy names institutional authorization, the conversion of recognition into durable credit under conditions where consequences can be attached. Consequence pressure refers to contexts where evaluation can allocate credit or impose sanction. None of these terms are synonyms. Their separation is the method. It lets the reader track where the analysis is describing perception, where it is describing evaluation, where it is describing circulation, and where it is describing institutional consequence.
Those consequences are not distributed evenly. The demand to prove provenance, to justify tools, to narrate labor, and to perform coherence is never placed on all bodies in the same way. This matters because AI is frequently framed as an ethics problem that can be solved by disclosure, as if transparency were neutral. In practice, disclosure functions as sorting. It can become a gate, a stigma, a trigger for intensified scrutiny, and the burden of scrutiny tends to land hardest on those already positioned on the losing side of institutional credibility. The paper therefore treats AI anxiety as part of the empirical field rather than as noise around the field. Suspicion is not only a personal reaction. Suspicion is a governance response (Foucault, 1976) that reshapes what counts as responsible practice, what kinds of speech count as scholarly, and what kinds of artifacts can still be trusted without their makers being punished for the route they took to get there. Power, as Foucault reminds us, “produces reality; it produces domains of objects and rituals of truth” (Foucault, 1976: 194).
The method is triangulated and practice-based, not because the paper needs three case studies to look complete, but because legitimacy conversion is enforced through multiple grammars that cannot be reduced to a single analytic lens without flattening the phenomenon. Procedural autoethnography here means documenting the paper’s own production pathway (Bertaux, 2010; Beaud & Weber, 2003) as bounded evidence (drafts, prompts, revision passes, decision points, verification steps) so that claims about legitimacy can be audited without turning method into confession. The evidence trail is intentionally bounded: enough to reconstruct decision ownership and verification, not enough to demand exhaustive autobiography. The protocol couples three arms to observe how legitimacy is manufactured, granted, refused, and weaponized across domains that are often discussed separately. This coupling is the instrument. Each arm forces the same conversion mechanism to appear under a different proof regime, making it possible to see what is stable in the mechanism and what changes when the grammar of evidence changes.
The first arm is artistic and it is placed first on purpose because this is where the glitch is perceived fastest. It treats two practices as the two sides of one instrument. HEXA is a recursive writing pipeline that runs long-duration material through cycles of revision, re-ingestion, and re-circulation, making authorship legible as procedure rather than origin. Wheels of Will is an Instagram-based visual practice that re-photographs the same ritual tools across the year, testing what reads as anchored versus staged when objects circulate under platform conditions. In both, the central question is not whether the maker is sincere. The central question is how sincerity is inferred, how labor is made readable, how optimization becomes detectable, and how that detectability can flip charging into glitch.
The second arm is academic and it tracks legitimacy as infrastructure. If legitimacy is withdrawn at the seam, the relevant data is not only what the paper argues but how evaluators react once the pathway becomes inspectable. It treats knowledge-making as genre-governed labor and asks how institutions reward fluency, tempo, and performable coherence alongside argument. It uses procedural autoethnography constrained by traceability, including retention of drafts, prompts, revision logs, and decision points, so that the pathway can be reconstructed without collapsing accountability into confession. The aim is to test whether making the pathway inspectable stabilizes trust or intensifies suspicion, and to show how that reaction reveals the enforcement logic of the regime doing the judging.
The third arm is ritual and it closes the analysis because it forces the most demanding evidence question. Witch bottles serve as the crystallizing case, moving across archaeology, contemporary practice, institutional display, and platform circulation. This ritual edge case pairs with the academic arm’s traceable procedural autoethnography to test the same governance question from opposite sides: what happens when institutions demand pathway auditability, either because it is newly possible (academic production) or because it is structurally resistant (preventative ritual efficacy). The point is not to prove whether ritual works under institutional proof standards. The point is to show that proof regimes are plural and that translation between regimes redistributes legitimacy. A practice can be socially real and stabilizing for its participants while remaining structurally resistant to audit in the institutional sense, especially when the desired outcome is preventative, protective, or negatively defined as a harm that does not occur. Under translation, the container can survive while the claim is edited, and the practitioner can become the one rendered suspect.
The contribution of the paper is diagnostic and philosophical at once. It maps how legitimacy is assigned, withdrawn, and weaponized as artifacts circulate across art worlds, academic institutions, and ritual practice. It also argues for a shift in ethics conversation from purity to procedure: away from the demand for unmediated origin, and toward accountable, traceable mediation that does not require the performance of suffering as proof of rigor. The core claim is simple and non-negotiable. The glitch is not marginal. It is a governance event. It is the point where an audience decides whether mediation is acceptable technique or disqualifying contamination, and where the possibility of being recognized as a creator of knowledge is either granted or refused.
Literature Review
Contemporary scholarship on generative AI is increasingly converging on a shared center of gravity: the politics of accountability under conditions of distributed mediation. The most consequential work in the field keeps returning to responsibility, credit, and sanction once plausibility becomes easy and once the pathway that produced plausibility becomes newly inferable. This is the hinge that makes the glitch matter. A glitch is not only an aesthetic disturbance. It is a social cue that invites a pathway story, and once a pathway story becomes tellable, evaluation often migrates from content to production. Scholarship across domains keeps circling this migration, even when it uses different vocabularies to name it, and this convergence is what the Sealed Card Protocol treats as the actual problem field.
Work in media studies and platform scholarship has been particularly clear that credibility is negotiated under attention scarcity. When objects circulate through feeds, evaluation is compressed into fast inference. This does not mean audiences have become shallow. It means platforms have turned reception into a constrained cognitive task where meaning must arrive quickly. Under those conditions, surface cues do more than decorate. They operate as condensed narratives of handling, effort, intention, and lineage. Generative systems intensify this by multiplying plausible production stories while also producing outputs with a recognizable smoothness. That smoothness does not prove automation, but it makes automation narratable. The primary cultural effect is not simply that AI “fakes” art. It is that it changes the conditions under which art can be trusted at a glance, and it changes which cues become proxies for pathway legitimacy.
Authorship studies and cultural production research also insist that modern authenticity norms are not simply about truthfulness. They are about narratable labor. Many art worlds anchor legitimacy to socially recognizable process stories: training, technique, apprenticeship, the mark of the hand, evidence of duration. Generative AI disrupts that anchoring by weakening the readability of effort at the surface. Even when conceptual labor and curatorial labor remain heavy, the artifact can circulate as if it were frictionless, inviting an inference that the maker did not earn the effect. This reveals a dependency institutions and publics often deny: legitimacy has long depended on effort legibility. Under uncertainty, evaluators reach for proxies: disclosure demands, tool bans, stylistic suspicion, and informal gatekeeping. These proxies sometimes track risk. They also frequently become moral theater that punishes dispreferred pathways rather than irresponsible practice.
Scholarship in communication, journalism studies, and misinformation research intensifies the governance angle by treating verification as infrastructure rather than virtue. Under conditions where texts and images can be generated at scale, older trust heuristics become unreliable. Institutions and publics either reinvest in traceability practices or retreat into suspicion. The former treats provenance, correction capacity, and source control as legitimacy infrastructure. The latter treats mediation itself as contamination. Contemporary debates oscillate between these responses, often within the same institution, which is why disclosure becomes paradoxical: demanded as a solution, then punished as a stain. The Sealed Card Protocol reads this paradox as a symptom of a deeper issue. Institutions want enforceable accountability, but inherited categories of accountability were built around simplified authorship models. When pipelines become hybrid and distributed, institutions still need a place where consequences can land, so they intensify attachment to the author node even as the author node becomes less descriptive of actual production.
Publication ethics and research integrity guidance becomes crucial here, not only as an anti-cheating discourse but as a liability management regime. Authorship is preserved as the category that anchors accountability, and non-human systems are treated as ineligible for that role because accountability is not only about contribution. It is about responsibility under consequence. A key implication follows: responsibility and mediation are being conflated in practice even when they can be separated conceptually. A responsible hybrid workflow can include robust verification, explicit decision-making, and careful source control. An irresponsible human-only workflow can include fabrication and plagiarism. The governance question is therefore not “was there mediation” but “is the pathway reconstructible and is responsibility assignable.”
Method debates around autoethnography repeatedly return to the distinction between self-writing as expressive narrative and self-writing as an analytic instrument that can support theory-building. Within that lineage, analytic variants of autoethnography defend structured reflexivity and explicit linkage between lived position, interpretive decisions, and claims, rather than treating sincerity as sufficient evidence. In parallel, qualitative rigor scholarship treats audit trails and process documentation as core supports for dependability and confirmability, precisely because they allow an external reader to inspect how interpretations were produced. Taken together, this literature motivates the protocol’s wager: when legitimacy becomes pathway authorization under consequence pressure, the relevant response is not purity rhetoric but evidentiary design that makes decision ownership and verification practices visible while keeping disclosure bounded.
Critical university studies, disability studies, and queer method complicate any simple procedural fix. Audit regimes do not land neutrally. Institutions already distribute legitimacy through norms of fluency, tempo, and performable coherence, and those norms are deeply ableist even when they are not recognized as such. Disfluency, hesitation, unconventional structure, recursive writing, and affective intensity are often treated as evidence of incompetence rather than alternative rigor. In this landscape, transparency can function as sorting rather than remedy. “Be transparent” can become another way of saying “make yourself vulnerable to policing,” especially for scholars already positioned as suspicious.
Digital religion scholarship prevents the debate from collapsing into an academic integrity narrative alone. Mediation does not merely transmit practice. It reformats practice. Ritual translated into posts, tutorials, livestreams, and aestheticized documentation becomes subject to platform-specific credibility cues. Authority can shift from lineage to visibility, from embodied repetition to demonstrable competence, and from relational proof to narratable outcome. Generative systems accelerate this formatting by offering instant scripts, instant interpretive frames, and instant symbolic coherence. The protocol’s interest is in what happens to charging when conditions of circulation change: what anchors can still carry credibility when practices are translated into content and when content is produced through hybrid mediation.
Anthropology and science and technology studies clarify why ritual is not an exotic side-case. Proof regimes are plural. What counts as evidence in one domain can be category error in another. Many legitimacy conflicts emerge from evidentiary mismatch rather than deception. In such cases, insisting on a single proof grammar can manufacture suspicion. The witch bottle matters as an analytic traveler because it moves across archaeology, museum display, platform circulation, and contemporary practice, revealing how legitimacy is reallocated as objects are translated across regimes.
Taken together, the field suggests a reframing the Sealed Card Protocol makes explicit. Recognition is increasingly cheap because plausible artifacts are increasingly easy to produce and circulate. Legitimacy is increasingly conditional because accountability must still be enforced under consequence pressure. Institutions respond by demanding seals: stable attachment points where responsibility can land. Those seals take the form of authorship categories, disclosure regimes, provenance claims, and stylistic expectations that function as proxy evidence of labor and integrity. These proxies sometimes manage real risk, but they also intensify pathway policing and reproduce inequalities in who is expected to explain themselves.
Conceptual Framework
Because debates about AI are frequently derailed by vague moral language, the conceptual framework locks vocabulary early and returns to it with discipline. The point is operational precision. If the paper claims that legitimacy disputes under generative conditions are governance disputes about pathways, then the framework must let the reader track when the analysis is describing perception, when it is describing evaluation, when it is describing circulation, and when it is describing institutional consequence.
The framework begins from the seam. A seam is the interface where evaluative attention flips from artifact to pathway. It is an adjudicative switch. The seam appears when a work can remain compelling while its legitimacy becomes contestable because the pathway that produced it has become narratable. Recognition is the moment an audience says the artifact works. Legitimacy is the institutional and cultural authorization that allows that recognition to convert into durable credit under consequence pressure. The seam is where conversion can fail even when recognition is high.
Glitch names the operational trigger that makes the seam audible. Glitch does not mean a technical malfunction. It refers to a perceptual threshold event: the moment a reader or viewer senses mediation and reorients evaluation toward the pathway. Glitch can be triggered by stylistic regularity, excessive polish, compressed tempo, uncanny phrasing, or a sense that the work has been engineered for readability. Glitch does not prove automation. It marks that automation has become a plausible story the audience can tell, and once that story is tellable, evaluation becomes procedural.
Pathway names what is being judged once the seam appears. A pathway is the reconstructible route of production, including tool use, human decisions, revision passes, verification steps, and source control practices. The framework treats “AI use” as an insufficient category because it collapses heterogeneous configurations into a binary and encourages proxy policing. A pathway can be responsible or irresponsible regardless of the presence of generative tools. The relevant question is whether responsibility is assignable and whether an evidence trail is sufficient for audit under the regime doing the judging.
Charging names what is at stake at the seam. Charging is the social work by which credibility and meaning adhere under circulation. Charging is observed through anchors: cues that permit inference about duration, intention, handling, and responsibility. Anchors can be material (residue, wear, density, containment), procedural (draft histories, logged decision points, explicit uncertainty marks), or relational (lineage claims, community witnessing, institutional endorsement). Charging can also be negative, meaning an artifact can become charged as suspicious and that charge can travel faster than careful audit. The framework keeps charging central because legitimacy disputes often hinge less on what a work says than on how its credibility is inferred, stabilized, or weaponized.
Legibility is regime-specific fit: the degree to which an artifact matches the reading habits and proof expectations of a given environment, including genre conventions, tempo expectations, tone expectations, and acceptable forms of self-presentation. Legibility is not the same as truth or quality. It is compatibility with what a regime is trained to recognize as “serious,” “responsible,” or “professional.” Generative systems intensify legibility politics because they can increase fluency and structure quickly, while also making fluency itself narratable as mediated.
Legitimacy is pathway authorization under consequence pressure. It is enforceable authorization: what a journal accepts as scholarship, what a platform elevates as credible content, what a community recognizes as valid practice, what an institution counts as labor. Consequence pressure refers to contexts where evaluation can allocate credit, impose sanction, trigger exclusion, or determine access to resources. Under AI anxiety, institutions intensify attachment to seals, meaning stable attachment points where consequences can land even when production is distributed.
These terms are linked through a causal grammar applied across all three arms. Artifacts circulate under constrained attention and normative proof expectations. Anchors invite charging. A glitch event makes mediation narratable. Narratability triggers the seam. The receiving regime seeks a seal where consequences can land. If the pathway is authorized, recognition converts into legitimacy. If the pathway is treated as contamination, legitimacy is withheld even if the artifact remains compelling.
Two analytic operators keep this mechanism concrete rather than merely asserted. Sakura Cardcaptor functions as a diagram of governance under asymmetry: a custodian becomes responsible for forces they did not create but must contain under consequence pressure, which clarifies why institutions seek seals even when causality is distributed. ReBoot functions as a diegetic ontology of computation as environment: meaning is repeatedly reclassified by infrastructural events and responsibility must be renegotiated when environment overrides intention, which clarifies why refitting and rebooting are ordinary adaptations to changing enforcement conditions rather than evidence of bad faith. These operators are used as disciplined diagrams, not as decorative metaphors, to name how governance enters reception in real time and how pathway authorization becomes a moving target when the environment of evaluation shifts.
Methods and Materials
The protocol’s method is built to make legitimacy conversion observable rather than asserted. It treats glitch as a data-generating event because the moment mediation becomes perceptible is also the moment evaluation migrates from artifact to pathway and from pathway to enforcement. That is why the design is practice-based and triangulated. Legitimacy is enforced through more than one grammar at once, so the inquiry moves across proof regimes without collapsing them into a single story.
Triangulation here is not three topics assembled for coverage. It is three coupled arms that function as stress tests for the same vocabulary discipline. The artistic arm is used because evaluation is fastest there and platform conditions make seam events frequent. The academic arm is used because seam events become explicit procedure through disclosure rules, peer review expectations, and institutional sanctions. The ritual arm is used because it pushes the argument to the edge of verification itself, where efficacy is often structurally resistant to institutional proof while containers remain legible and collectible. The aim is not to generalize prevalence. The aim is to expose mechanism, and to do so with enough internal documentation that a reader can audit how claims were produced. It is included because the paper’s core claim is that legitimacy disputes are governance disputes about pathways.
Data is deliberately heterogeneous because legitimacy is enforced through multiple grammars. Primary materials in the artistic arm include selected HEXA texts treated longitudinally as controlled variation evidence of reboot under platform constraint, and a sequence of Wheels of Will bottle photographs treated as controlled variation in aesthetic register. Primary materials in the academic arm consist of a bounded procedural trace retained as an accountability object: drafts, prompts, revision passes, decision points, verification steps, and explicit uncertainty markings. Institutional feedback, reviewer expectations, and disclosure norms are treated as data about legitimacy conversion rather than neutral assessment. Primary materials in the ritual arm center on witch bottles as an evidentiary edge case, tracking translation across archaeology, museum display, contemporary practice, and platform circulation.
The academic and ritual arms are methodologically paired because both test audit pressure at the seam from opposite directions. Procedural autoethnography supplies a bounded evidence trail designed to make a scholarly pathway reconstructible without collapsing into confession. Witch bottles invert the situation. They often produce claims whose desired outcome is preventative or negatively defined, making full audit structurally difficult even when the practice is socially real for participants. Following the bottle across regimes shows how legitimacy is redistributed when an object is made collectible and describable (archaeology and museum display) while the practitioner’s pathway is rendered suspect or unsayable under institutional proof demands. In short, the academic arm asks whether pathway inspectability stabilizes legitimacy; the ritual arm shows the limit case where pathway inspectability is precisely what translation cannot deliver, and where institutions compensate by authorizing containers while policing makers.
Across all arms, analysis is conducted in three coordinated moves. First, close reading and close visual analysis identify anchors, cues, and constraint signals that invite inference about handling, duration, intention, and responsibility. Second, the protocol tracks seam events explicitly: moments when an evaluator’s stance shifts from artifact surface to pathway narration, and from pathway narration to authorization or refusal. Third, the protocol maps consequences: which forms of recognition become durable legitimacy, which remain socially weightless, and which trigger policing that injures producers and demands recomposition.
A minimal governance layer is carried inside the workflow as method and object of analysis. The evaluative target is accountability evidence (verification, decision ownership, correction capacity), not mediation presence treated as a disqualifying category. Procedural autoethnography functions as evidence trail design. It operationalizes accountability through reconstructibility and verification, while also measuring the incentive problem the paper diagnoses: whether visibility is rewarded as responsible practice or punished as contamination. The protocol distinguishes mediation from accountability and treats governance as three design problems: control design (verification and source control), evidence trail design (what documentation counts as sufficient for audit), and incentive design (how to prevent responsible disclosure from becoming a trigger for punitive policing). Traceability is treated as double-edged. It can increase accountability and exposure at once. This is why the protocol treats the evidence trail itself as part of what is being tested: whether audit operates as proportional governance or as a disguised morality play about who is allowed to take which pathway into recognition without being punished for the route itself.
Analysis and results
The protocol’s central empirical claim is that legitimacy conversion is decided at the seam, and the seam is most audible where evaluation is fastest. The artistic arm therefore begins at the surface. Images and short texts are judged under time pressure. Viewers and readers infer. They do not audit. Under these conditions, credibility stabilizes or collapses through cues that permit pathway inference without requiring explicit claims. Wheels of Will and HEXA operate as the two sides of the same instrument. One runs the test through vision and platform reception. The other runs it through language and longitudinal refitting.
Wheels of Will makes charging visible as action performed through anchors rather than through declarative insistence. The photographic setup is deliberately spare, compressing attention onto seals, bindings, residues, sediment, density of contents, and containment architecture. These elements function as condensed pathways. They allow inference about duration, handling, repetition, decision, and risk management. A wire-wrapped neck reads as constraint and care. Sediment reads as time. A sealed mouth reads as containment and consequence. For viewers who recognize these cues as signs of practice, credibility can stabilize quickly. For viewers who read the same cues as aesthetic staging, suspicion arrives just as quickly. The surface does not prove efficacy. It makes a pathway inferable enough for a viewer to allocate credibility in a fast evaluation environment.
Platform conditions intensify a double bind. Feeds reward legibility and fast recognition, but credibility can depend on appearing insufficiently optimized. The object must look intentional without looking over-calculated. Mediation must be present without being foregrounded as strategy. The work must be readable at a glance while still suggesting remainder, a sense that something persists beyond immediate consumption. Under these conditions, the seam appears when optimization becomes perceptible. The glitch is not a technical error in the image. The glitch is the viewer’s sense that the artifact has been engineered for readability. That sense can reroute appreciation into suspicion because the inferred pathway flips from handled practice to performance for the feed.
The bottle family is designed to stage this negotiation between immediacy and remainder. It offers rapid cues while withholding full access to contents, forcing the viewer to complete the pathway through inference rather than inspection. Seals imply closure with intention and restrict casual access. Bindings imply constraint and a decision that something must be held together. Residues imply use, repetition, and contact rather than pristine display. Sediment implies settling and duration, a record that is difficult to display convincingly without also simulating time. Density of contents implies weight and consequence in a practical sense: something has been gathered, inserted, sealed, and maintained. Containment architecture implies what is allowed to leak and what must be prevented from leaking. Together, these cues invite a viewer to infer a pathway whether that pathway is named as ritual, craft, or obsessive care.
This produces a stable finding for the artistic arm. Charging is more reliably performed through anchors than through explicit claims of authenticity. Explicit claims can trigger skepticism because they read as marketing or self-authorizing speech. An object that insists on its own power can be dismissed as performance. An object that quietly carries marks of use can be read as anchored. Under platform circulation, anchors are the practical mechanism by which charge adheres long enough to travel.
Platforms complicate that mechanism because they reward consistent aesthetics, high-contrast clarity, clean composition, and novelty. The artist is pressured to optimize visibility while avoiding the appearance of optimization. Too much polish can trigger the glitch because viewers infer branding rather than practice. Too little polish can trigger dismissal because viewers infer incompetence rather than depth. The seam appears when platform literacy becomes suspicion and the inferred pathway flips from handled object to optimized content.
HEXA runs the same test through language, but it does so longitudinally. The 2008 Ovid and Pygmalion piece charges itself through drift, reverence, pastiche, and an unarmored intimacy with lineage. Its intensity comes from attachment expressed as form, from sentences allowed to wander until they become rooms. Lineage appears as intimacy rather than citation. Position is carried by cadence rather than explicit argument. This architecture can read as charged for readers willing to inhabit its tempo. It can be dismissed as undisciplined by readers trained to treat visible structure as the primary evidence of seriousness and responsibility.
The later refitting functions as infrastructural adaptation rather than purely stylistic change. Segmentation, hinge lines, and visible scaffolds prioritize scan readability and audit-trained navigation. The refitting shows how legibility can be engineered without necessarily changing what the work is attached to. It also shows how legitimacy can still be withheld because visible structure can be read as strategic packaging rather than lived attachment. In one proof regime, structure reads as discipline. In another, structure reads as marketing. The pair isolates a variable that matters across the protocol: legibility engineering can increase access and circulation while simultaneously increasing the risk of being read as optimized, and therefore suspect.
The comparison with Wheels of Will is structural. In HEXA, reboot takes the form of segmentation and hinge clarity. In Wheels of Will, reboot takes the form of framing, composition, and the calibrated visibility of anchors. In both cases, the same adaptation that makes an artifact travel can also make optimization narratable. Once optimization becomes narratable, evaluation migrates from artifact to pathway.
The academic arm translates this volatility into formal rule. Academic legitimacy is built from genre signals that function as compliance markers: signposting, citation density, stable tone, orderly sequencing, and explicit methodological signaling. These are not only communication techniques. They are legitimacy cues that permit audit and allocate credit. Under generative conditions, these cues can be produced more quickly and more uniformly, which increases institutional pressure to police pathways rather than artifacts. If the artifact is no longer treated as reliable evidence of the labor narrative institutions expect, the pathway becomes the evaluative object.
Procedural traceability appears here as an accountability instrument rather than confession. A structured trace of drafting phases, decision points, source checks, and revision moves makes distributed labor inspectable so responsibility can be evidenced rather than inferred. The trace can show verification, correction, and deliberate choice. Yet the same trace can be read as self-incrimination under punitive norms. In a regime that treats mediation as contaminant, traceability does not convert into legitimacy. It converts into proof of dependency. The same document can be interpreted as transparency and as disqualification. This is the institutional form of the artifact versus pathway shift: the artifact meets genre expectation while the route is treated as illegitimate.
This arm makes a second finding visible. Rigor operates as infrastructure. It is not only cognitive quality. It is a legible performance of pathway that must be readable to evaluators. Standard article structures, citation patterns, and methodological signals provide pathways readers can audit and institutions can reward. Fluency in that performance is treated as competence and responsibility. Failure to perform the pathway is treated as deficiency, regardless of insight. A disability-oriented analysis clarifies what is often naturalized: rigor frequently encodes an assumed body, an assumed tempo, and an assumed endurance capacity.
Generative tools can function as legibility prostheses by supporting drafting, restructuring, and stylistic smoothing. They can allow scholars to meet genre requirements without requiring a normative body. Institutions often moralize that support as shortcut. The artifact can satisfy the genre while the pathway is disallowed. The governance problem is not merely ethical mood. It is an enforcement logic about who is authorized to reach recognition without being punished for the route.
Traceability tests whether this dynamic can be shifted by changing what is rewarded. In a governance regime oriented toward accountability, documentation of verification and decision ownership increases trust because it makes responsibility visible. In a punitive regime, the same visibility becomes a trap. This produces a practical implication: disclosure demands without standards create incentives for concealment. If institutions want mediation disclosed, they must define responsible configurations and specify what evidence counts as sufficient. Otherwise, responsible actors are punished for visibility while irresponsible actors are rewarded for hiding mediation.
The ritual arm functions as a stress test for evidentiary norms by exposing proof-regime mismatch. Ritual credibility is often produced through recurrence, embodied uptake, relational proof, and narrative stabilization rather than institutional certification. Witch bottles crystallize the evidence problem because efficacy is often defined as harm prevented. The desired outcome can be the absence of a negative event. That absence cannot be archived as a positive trace in the formats institutional proof regimes prefer. Credibility nonetheless persists through repetition, community testimony, and felt stabilization. These are not irrational forms of evidence. They are fitted to the kinds of effects the practice claims.
Archaeological recognition operates through patterned form and recurring placement, allowing description without verifying efficacy. Contemporary practice often relies on lived narratives of protection and felt reduction of volatility. Digital circulation translates objects into images and practices into scripts that can be adopted, branded, and judged at speed. Under translation, the container can survive while the claim is edited. In a gallery, the bottle can become an art object that carries aura while losing practice. In a museum, it can become folklore. On a platform, it can become content. Each frame can stabilize recognition while withdrawing legitimacy from the pathway, especially when the pathway asserts cosmological authority the receiving regime cannot certify.
This arm does not ask institutions to endorse ritual efficacy. It asks analysts to track what happens to credibility when objects cross proof regimes. The seam appears when a receiving regime demands proof in a format the practice neither can provide nor aims to provide. The resulting suspicion is frequently produced by mismatch rather than mediation itself.
Across arms, the same mechanism emerges. Translation produces recognition. Recognition triggers seam events. Seam events trigger pathway audits. Pathway audits allocate legitimacy or withdraw it. Charging accumulates when anchors allow audiences to infer duration, handling, and responsibility. Charging collapses when optimization is interpreted as unauthorized mediation. Generative AI accelerates translation and increases seam detectability, making this mechanism more frequent and more consequential. The analysis keeps the mechanism visible and refuses moral shortcuts. Suspicion is treated as a governance response that can be studied, not as evidence. Purity is treated as an unstable demand, not as a solvable baseline. Legitimacy is treated as infrastructure, and the practical question becomes how infrastructures could be redesigned to reward accountable procedure rather than punishing visible mediation.
Discussion
The protocol’s distinctiveness lies in its refusal of curative fantasies. Disclosure alone does not restore trust. Purity cannot be recovered as a stable baseline. Hybrid work is not automatically equivalent across configurations. The analysis begins instead with a disciplined claim about where legitimacy is decided. The seam is the point where evaluation flips from artifact to pathway. Glitching is the trigger that makes the flip audible by making mediation narratable. Charging names what then becomes vulnerable: the adhesive credibility that allows meaning to survive circulation long enough to count.
Across the three arms, suspicion functions less as a detector of falsity than as a governance response to enforceability problems. When plausibility becomes easy to produce, institutions and publics need attachment points where consequences can land. They reach for proxies that are fast, portable, and socially legible: polish versus roughness, fluency versus hesitation, structure versus drift, disclosure versus silence, and provenance stories that feel coherent versus ones that feel strategic. These proxies sometimes track real risk. They also routinely misfire because they target mediation presence rather than accountability practice. The result is a familiar distortion. A persuasive artifact can be treated as contaminant because its pathway is narratable in the wrong way, while a careless artifact can be tolerated because its pathway fits inherited expectations of authorship and effort.
The artistic arm shows how this distortion operates under platform conditions. Wheels of Will reveals that credibility can stabilize without explicit authenticity claims when anchors allow a viewer to infer handling, duration, and decision. It also reveals the double bind created by feed economies. The same aesthetic optimizations that increase legibility can make strategy perceptible, and once strategy is perceptible, evaluation migrates to the pathway. The work may remain compelling while becoming socially suspect because the inferred route flips from practice to performance. HEXA shows the same mechanism in language. A refitting that increases hinge clarity and scan readability can expand access and circulation. That same refitting can also invite accusations of packaging, because legibility engineering is itself judged as a pathway signal. Together, these cases demonstrate that the problem is not that audiences are irrational. The problem is that evaluation under scarcity relies on pathway stories, and generative conditions multiply plausible pathway stories while making some cues of mediation easier to narrate.
The academic arm translates this into formal rule. Academic legitimacy is built from genre cues that permit audit and allocate credit. Under generative conditions, those cues are easier to produce, which increases the temptation to treat the pathway itself as the object of judgment. Procedural traceability appears as a plausible accountability instrument because it can show decision ownership, verification, and correction. Yet the same trace can become self-incriminating in punitive regimes that treat mediation as contaminant. This ambivalence matters. It shows that transparency is not a neutral solution. Visibility can convert into legitimacy only when institutions specify what counts as responsible use and when disclosure is not structurally punished. Without standards and protections, disclosure demands function as sorting mechanisms that reward concealment and punish responsibility.
The ritual arm forces a further caution. Some practices produce effects that are real for participants but not recoverable in institutional proof formats, especially when outcomes are preventative or negatively defined as harm averted. When institutions demand a single evidentiary grammar, mismatch is treated as fraud and suspicion is manufactured by format rather than by error. Witch bottles show how translation across regimes can preserve containers while editing claims, reallocating legitimacy to objects and away from practitioners. This is not an argument that institutions must endorse ritual efficacy. It is an argument that governance must recognize proof-regime plurality and must resist using alien proof demands as a shortcut to disqualification.
These results sharpen the governance implications. The relevant distinction is not between AI and non-AI production. The relevant distinction is between pathways that bear responsibility and pathways that evade it. Governance with teeth targets failure modes such as fabrication, citation instability, and unverifiable synthesis through concrete controls: source checking, stable citation practice, explicit uncertainty marking, and human review of high-risk claims. Governance must also target legitimacy harm created by proxy policing. When polish is treated as fraud and disfluency is treated as rigor, evaluation becomes an ableist moral economy of effort. That moral economy intensifies under AI anxiety because smoothness is newly narratable as mediated. A disability-oriented analysis makes the stakes explicit. If tools function as legibility prostheses, blanket bans operate as exclusion. A proportional accountability regime can permit assistive use while requiring verification and decision ownership, shifting evaluation away from bodily tempo and toward responsible practice.
The protocol also clarifies a methodological dilemma about form. Unconventional architecture can be method rather than failure, especially when the phenomenon involves recursion, contradiction, and the policing of voice. Readers trained to audit argument still require joints where claims and evidence can be checked. The response is method-legibility rather than voice laundering: section-level functions, locked definitions, and explicit hinge points that preserve cadence while making the pathway of the argument inspectable.
The paper’s diagnostic claim can now be stated without moral shortcuts. Legitimacy is pathway authorization under consequence pressure. Generative systems intensify seam events by increasing translation speed and making mediation easier to narrate. Institutions respond by tightening seals, often through purity-inflected proxy rules that collapse diverse configurations into binary verdicts. The practical task is not to restore innocence. The task is to redesign evaluation infrastructures so that responsibility can be assigned proportionally, evidence trails are meaningful and bounded, and disclosure is paired with standards that reward accountable visibility rather than punishing it.
Conclusion
The Sealed Card Protocol treats the glitch as a governance event. A reader senses mediation. A viewer senses optimization. Evaluation migrates from artifact to pathway because consequences need somewhere to land. Across three arms, the same conversion mechanism reappears. Recognition is often fast. Legitimacy is conditional. The condition is whether the pathway is narratable and authorized within the proof regime doing the judging.
The artistic arm shows that charging is not secured by declarative authenticity claims. It is secured by anchors that allow duration, handling, and decision to be inferred under time pressure. Wheels of Will demonstrates how anchors can stabilize credibility while also becoming staging cues once optimization is perceived. HEXA demonstrates how refitting for legibility can expand circulation while also increasing the risk of being read as packaged, and therefore suspect. In both cases, the seam is crossed when strategy becomes narratable, not when an artifact becomes false. The work can remain persuasive while legitimacy is withdrawn because the inferred route is treated as contamination.
The academic arm shows how this volatility hardens into institutional procedure. Genre signals operate as audit infrastructure and as credit allocation mechanisms. Under generative conditions, those signals are easier to produce, which increases pressure to judge pathways rather than artifacts. Traceability appears as an accountability instrument because it can make verification and decision ownership visible. It becomes a trap when disclosure is punished and when institutions treat mediation itself as disqualifying. This yields a concrete governance implication. Disclosure demands without standards reward concealment. If institutions want visibility, they must define responsible configurations, specify what evidence counts as sufficient, and constrain how disclosure can be used so that audit does not become punitive policing.
The ritual arm shows why proof-regime plurality must be part of the analysis. Some practices are socially real and stabilizing while being structurally resistant to institutional proof formats, especially when effects are preventative or negatively defined. Witch bottles demonstrate how objects can retain recognition under translation while practitioners lose legitimacy when claims are forced into alien evidentiary grammars. Suspicion in such cases is often produced by mismatch rather than by deception. Governance that ignores proof plurality will treat mismatch as fraud and will weaponize legitimacy as disqualification.
Taken together, the protocol supports a shift from purity to procedure. The question is not whether mediation occurred. Mediation is ubiquitous. The question is whether accountability can be evidenced: whether claims are checkable, sources stable, decisions owned, and verification practices proportionate to risk. A proportional regime differentiates configurations rather than treating “AI” as a contaminant category. It designs controls that prevent epistemic harm while reducing legitimacy harm produced by proxy policing.
The political stakes are unevenly distributed. Disclosure, audit, and coherence demands do not land on all bodies equally. Norms of fluency, tempo, and performable structure encode an assumed body and an assumed endurance capacity. Generative tools can function as legibility prostheses, enabling scholars excluded by normative performance standards to meet genre requirements. If institutions moralize assistance as shortcut, they will intensify exclusion while failing to manage real epistemic risk. A workable governance response therefore requires both accountability standards and access commitments.
The Sealed Card Protocol is not a universal theory of AI. It is an instrument for making legitimacy conversion observable. It treats the seam as the site where power enters evaluation and where recognition is converted into credit or blocked as contamination. Under generative conditions, seam events will not disappear. The practical demand is that institutions and platforms make responsible practice possible: clear standards, bounded evidence trails, proportional consequence assignment, and incentives that reward accountable visibility rather than punishing it.
