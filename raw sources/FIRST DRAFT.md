FIRST DRAFT

Compress without opacity
Introduction
Before the headline, the regulatory finding, the court order, there is a quieter breakdown. A hiring system trained on a decade of historical decisions downgrades applications bearing the word "women's" and no one in the governance chain had built the infrastructure to notice. Not when the model was procured. Not when it was deployed. Not when outputs began coupling to consequential hiring decisions at scale. The governance failure precedes the scandal; the scandal merely makes the failure reconstructible. That sequence — invisible breakdown, visible consequence — is the primary object of this paper. Its governing thesis follows directly: AI governance is not a technical program of performance benchmarks and safety evaluations. It is a politics of visibility that produces the conditions under which organizations become legible, decisions become enforceable, and affected populations either retain or lose the capacity to contest outcomes that classify, score, and allocate on their behalf. Consequence domain: regulatory, legal, reputational, and epistemic, distributed unevenly across deploying organizations and the populations their systems act upon.
The politics of the gaze has a long theoretical genealogy, and naming it is a condition of using its tools correctly. Institutions discipline subjects through regimes of perception, classification, and exposure while sheltering their own mechanisms behind procedural opacity: that asymmetry is foundational, not incidental. Following Browne (2015) — who positions the history of surveillance in Western societies as inseparable from the racialized management of populations — this paper treats contemporary algorithmic systems as genealogically continuous with earlier surveillance regimes, inheriting their asymmetries even when they encode no explicit racial logic. The guard tower of the panopticon is not a historical curiosity; it is an institutional architecture in which the capacity to see without being seen concentrates power at a specific organizational node. What has changed is the infrastructure. The gaze has been automated, made programmable, and deployed at commercial scale across domains — labor recruitment, welfare administration, criminal justice risk scoring, customer service, biometric identification — that have no single originating surveillance logic but now share a common technical substrate. Following Mirzoeff (2011) — who operationalizes countervisuality as the demand for the right to look back at the institutions that organize perception — this paper takes that demand as a governance requirement: if systems see the world, the world must be able to see systems sufficiently to inspect, contest, and force justification. The turn on both is institutional: the asymmetry is treated not only as a political condition to diagnose but as an organizational liability to reconstruct.
Two structural failures organize the empirical terrain, and defining both precisely before pairing them is necessary to the analysis. By asymmetric visibility, this paper means the condition in which individuals become hypervisible through data traces — their behaviors, transactions, attributes, and associations rendered legible and actionable — while the decision mechanisms that act on those traces remain structurally opaque and difficult to inspect, challenge, or prove. The asymmetry is not incidental to AI deployment; it is, as Browne (2015) establishes, its default inheritance. By invisibilization, this paper means the complement: groups rendered statistically absent or distorted by training and measurement gaps become systemically misrecognized or excluded, their erasure automated, repeatable, and difficult to litigate because bias encodes in the absence of data rather than in the presence of discriminatory intent. Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) anchor this empirically: commercial gender classification systems perform worst on darker-skinned women, a population underrepresented in benchmark training sets, producing accuracy disparities that follow the contours of governance gaps in procurement and evaluation scope rather than the logic of random technical error. Barocas and Selbst (2016) supply the structural account: data-driven systems produce disparate impact through facially neutral proxies without any intent to discriminate, which means invisibilization requires neither animus nor awareness to constitute harm. The two failures are distinct yet linked at the level of cause: both follow from the same missing machinery — the absent inventory, the unenforced gate, the unmonitored deployment — that allows a system to operate without the evidence trails that would make its operations contestable.
A second analytic register shapes the paper throughout, operating at a different level of abstraction from the governance critique. By the peer-from-our-doubts frame, this paper means the following: an AI system is not a neutral instrument that processes inputs and returns outputs; it is the material residue of what an institution chose to measure, what it chose to discount, and which questions it became too comfortable to keep asking. The Amazon recruiting tool did not introduce bias. It inherited the institutional doubt — encoded in a decade of hiring decisions that systematically favored men — that certain application features correlated with reduced suitability. What the system did to candidates, it simultaneously disclosed about the institution that built it. That is the frame's core claim: governance failures are not only accountability failures in the external sense. They are epistemic failures — moments at which an organization's foreclosed questions, unexamined assumptions, and structurally discounted perspectives become auditable through the damage they produce. Importantly, the frame does not reduce governance to psychology or organizational culture alone. It operationalizes as a concrete requirement: governance machinery must force the questions back into visibility that deployment pressure tends to foreclose, and it must do so before consequences are imposed on the populations the system was always already classifying.
These two frames are not parallel but entangled, and their entanglement produces the paper's central theoretical contribution. The politics-of-visibility frame asks who is made legible by AI systems and who is erased: it is a claim about power distribution in institutional space. The peer-from-our-doubts frame asks what those patterns of legibility and erasure disclose about the epistemic culture of the organizations that produced them: it is a claim about institutional self-knowledge. Read together, AI governance is simultaneously an external accountability problem — who can contest automated outcomes, through what pathways, with what evidence — and an internal reckoning — what automated outcomes make visible about the assumptions, silences, and power distributions that governance had not previously been asked to inspect. The mechanism chain connecting both is governance machinery: the repeatable operational sequence of inventory, controls, assessments, evidence, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting that turns values into inspectable decisions. Each link in that sequence is, simultaneously, a condition of external contestability and a site at which the organization is forced to ask the questions the model cannot ask for itself. A gate that requires equity metrics before deployment is a legal control; it is also an institutional insistence that the question of whose accuracy matters must be answered before consequences are imposed.
The framework extends to voice, and that extension requires a claim of its own. Most AI governance scholarship privileges vision as its organizing metaphor even when the actual interface is conversational: the automated phone screen, the clinical documentation system, the customer-facing chatbot. Voice forces a tighter and more uncomfortable claim: AI governance also structures audibility, determining who gets accurately heard, who gets misrecognized, and whose speech becomes grounds for suspicion or exclusion. If classification can render groups invisible, algorithmic listening can render them inaudible, with equally consequential effects on access, eligibility, and institutional standing. Koenecke and colleagues (2020) establish the empirical anchor: racial disparities in commercial automated speech recognition systems are large enough to convert transcription error into allocation mechanism in hiring workflows, clinical documentation, and education platforms. A misheard sentence in an automated phone screen is not a quality nuisance; it is a gate that closes without logging. The system does not know it did not hear. That failure — silent, unlogged, structurally invisible — is precisely what reciprocal audibility is designed to surface: the voice-specific sibling of reciprocal visibility, requiring that if institutions deploy algorithmic listening, affected people must have enforceable routes to verify what the system heard, how it converted speech into decision-relevant features, and how errors are corrected. The governance response is not merely engineering improvement. It is subgroup evaluation, production monitoring, and verifiable equity of the kind that Gebru and colleagues (2018) and Mitchell and colleagues (2019) argue must be documented and defended across the system lifecycle.
The extension to voice introduces a further mechanism claim, one that the visibility literature has not named with precision. By interface laundering, this paper means the process through which conversational interfaces shift consequential institutional decisions — policy communication, eligibility assessment, risk scoring — into informal interaction registers that appear transactional and low-stakes, thereby evading the evidentiary infrastructure that accountability requires. The mechanism is testable in two directions. Outward-facing interface laundering: Air Canada deploys a customer-facing chatbot without source control, versioning, or correction timelines, and the system's outputs function as institutional speech in the eyes of customers and courts. Screenshots become evidence. The system's informality is its legal exposure. Inward-facing interface laundering: Samsung employees route sensitive work through an external conversational tool because policy has not caught up with practice, creating audit voids in which inputs, prompts, and outputs are never operationalized as governance artifacts. In both cases, the mechanism is identical: governance opacity converts a technical convenience into an accountability liability by ensuring that what was said, by which version, under what prompts, and with what correction pathway, cannot be reconstructed — which means it cannot be contested. Interface laundering is not a description of risk; it is a finding whose verification requires specific artifacts (logs, versioning records, escalation documentation, correction timelines) and whose refutation is the absence of those artifacts.
The paper positions itself at the intersection of three research streams whose integration is its primary contribution. Critical surveillance scholarship — following Browne (2015) and Mirzoeff (2011) — supplies the framework for reading AI systems as regimes of perception organized by power rather than neutrality; this paper turns that framework toward institutional reconstruction rather than critical diagnosis alone. Algorithmic accountability research — following Diakopoulos (2015), Kroll and colleagues (2017), and Raji and colleagues (2020) — establishes that accountability is not a normative aspiration but an evidential infrastructure with specifiable components; this paper operationalizes that insight at the organizational level, treating governance machinery completeness as the primary unit of cross-case analysis. Documentation and fairness research — following Gebru and colleagues (2018), Mitchell and colleagues (2019), Buolamwini and Gebru (2018), Selbst and colleagues (2019), Barocas and Selbst (2016), Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell (2018), Ustun, Spangher, and Liu (2018), and Kaminski and Malgieri (2021) — supplies the concrete tooling for what governance machinery must produce; this paper uses that tooling to identify, across seven cases, which specific links in the machinery chain were absent and how their absence propagated into harm. The integration gap the paper addresses is this: most research treats visibility, fairness, contestability, documentation, and voice as separate problems requiring separate solutions. A visibility-plus-audibility framework, grounded in both positionality frames, gives a unifying analytic capable of holding them together without collapsing their differences.
The empirical analysis proceeds through a comparative, qualitative multiple-case study organized as governance reconstruction. Seven cases are examined: Amazon's recruiting tool (labor gatekeeping, visibility-dominant), Clearview AI (biometric data provenance and multi-jurisdiction enforcement), Zillow Offers (capital allocation at scale), the Dutch Toeslagenaffaire (administrative due process failure at population scale), COMPAS (contested fairness under vendor opacity), Samsung and ChatGPT (inward-facing interface laundering), and the Air Canada chatbot (outward-facing interface laundering and institutional speech liability). The corpus spans visibility-dominant systems, voice and conversational systems, and hybrid high-impact scoring regimes across labor markets, welfare administration, criminal justice, capital allocation, and customer service — institutional contexts that apply different pressures to governance machinery and generate different harm pathways when it fails. The paper proceeds as follows: the literature review maps the three research streams and names the integration gap; the conceptual framework specifies the four analytic terms — reciprocal visibility, reciprocal audibility, governance machinery, interface laundering — and the positionality frames that orient their use; the methods section operationalizes the coding scheme and evaluative indices; the case analyses reconstruct governance failure across the seven dossiers; and the discussion and conclusion hold the governance standard and the peer-from-our-doubts frame together as a single argument about what it means for an organization to remain open to the questions its systems cannot ask about themselves. The stakes are institutional and epistemic in equal measure: governance machinery is the condition under which legitimacy becomes an evidence-backed asset rather than a performance claim, and under which AI systems function as accountable peers rather than automated repositories of the doubts an organization preferred not to examine.
Positionality
This paper does not proceed from a view from nowhere. Its normative anchor is the manifesto that inaugurates the project, which states its governing claim without ambiguity: AI governance is not a technical program but a politics of visibility, and a mature organization must govern that politics with the same rigor it applies to any other enterprise liability. The manifesto issues two explicit refusals. It refuses the asymmetric model in which the citizen becomes hypervisible while decision mechanisms remain opaque, naming that asymmetry not as an implementation detail but as an architecture of power. And it refuses the quieter violence of effacement, where training gaps convert statistical absence into practical discrimination. In their place, it demands a standard that is simple, auditable, and actionable: traceability, useful transparency, verifiable equity, contestability, supplier governance, and incident readiness.
A second positionality claim, operating at a different register, must also be named. This paper is written in a moment when AI systems are being introduced as cognitive infrastructure inside organizations, mediating analysis, drafting, deliberation, and decision. The META framing that orients this research characterizes AI not as a neutral instrument but as the peer that emerges from our doubts, our biases, and the devil's advocate we were too comfortable to retain in the room. On this account, deploying an AI system is not only a technical act but an epistemic one: the system inherits the questions an organization stopped asking, the perspectives it systematically discounted, and the edge cases it preferred not to surface. What the system does to others, it also reveals about the institution that built and deployed it.
These two positionality claims are not parallel but entangled. The politics-of-visibility frame asks who is made legible by AI systems and who is erased. The peer-from-our-doubts frame asks what those patterns of legibility and erasure say about the epistemic culture of the organizations that produced them. Together, they frame AI governance as simultaneously an external accountability problem - who can contest automated outcomes - and an internal reckoning - what automated outcomes disclose about institutional assumptions, silences, and power distributions that governance had not previously been asked to inspect.
Literature Review: From the Politics of Visibility to the Politics of Voice in AI Governance
1. Visibility as Governance, Not as a Side Effect
AI governance scholarship increasingly treats visibility as a primary site of power rather than a neutral byproduct of measurement. The governing claim is direct: AI governance constitutes a politics of visibility that decides who becomes legible, who disappears, who gains time, who pays the price, and who can contest. That framing aligns with a long arc of critical theory in which institutions discipline subjects through regimes of perception, classification, and exposure, while sheltering their own mechanisms behind procedural opacity. Surveillance studies sharpen this asymmetry: systems that see rarely become equally seeable, and the harms of being rendered measurable distribute unevenly across racialized, gendered, and classed populations. Browne (2015) demonstrates how the history of surveillance in Western societies is inseparable from the management and control of Black life, establishing the genealogy of racializing surveillance as foundational to any account of who bears the costs of algorithmic legibility.
A useful bridge to AI governance is Mirzoeff's (2011) account of visuality as authority-making and countervisuality as a demand for the right to look. In governance terms, the right to look becomes a right to inspect, to contest, and to force institutional justification. This operationalizes as reciprocal visibility: if systems see the world, then affected people, regulators, and auditors must be able to see enough of those systems to support inspection and remedy. The literature supports this as a shift from transparency as virtue to visibility as capacity - not a posture of openness but an active evidential practice.
2. Algorithmic Accountability as an Evidentiary Project
A second stream frames accountability as a social practice that makes computational power investigable. Diakopoulos (2015) defines algorithmic accountability in terms of rendering algorithmic influence legible through institutional mechanisms, including investigative methods and procedural demands on organizations. The key consequence is epistemic and legal: without reliable routes to observation, organizations can neither verify claims about fairness, safety, or compliance, nor credibly contest allegations of harm. Accountability, on this account, is not primarily a normative aspiration but an evidential infrastructure.
Kroll and colleagues (2017) extend this insight into constitutional terms, arguing that algorithmic decision-making implicates rule-of-law values such as due process, equal protection, and transparency, and that technical approaches alone cannot substitute for accountability structures grounded in legal and organizational practice. Their argument converges with a risk-management framing: trustworthiness is not a property of models but a property of organizational practices that surround, constrain, and document model use. The implication for governance design is direct - legitimacy behaves like an enterprise resource because it conditions regulatory stability, reputational resilience, and operational continuity, and it cannot be claimed through assertion alone.
Raji and colleagues (2020) translate these imperatives into operational form, proposing an end-to-end framework for internal algorithmic auditing that spans problem formulation, artifact testing, and deployment monitoring. Their framework supports what this paper calls a proof orientation: governance must produce evidence trails that let a third party reconstruct what was done, why it was done, and what changed after failures. Without that chain, accountability claims remain aspirational rather than auditable.
3. Documentation and Traceability as the Substrate of Reciprocal Visibility
A third research stream builds the concrete tooling required for reciprocal visibility: documentation artifacts and traceability practices that travel with systems. Gebru and colleagues (2018) argue that machine learning lacks standardized dataset documentation, and propose structured records of motivation, composition, collection, recommended use, and limitations as a prerequisite for accountability. Mitchell and colleagues (2019) extend the same logic to trained models through model cards, emphasizing evaluation context, intended use, and subgroup performance reporting. Both contributions treat documentation not as overhead but as the substrate on which accountability claims can be grounded, tested, and contested.
The demand for a lifecycle record - a life sheet - for each high-impact system, covering purpose, data, model, suppliers, metrics, limits, named owners, and retirement conditions, maps directly onto this documentation literature. The mechanism is straightforward: documentation turns trust-me governance into inspectable governance. Without documentation, contestation collapses into narrative conflict and remediation becomes guesswork. This is where the transparency literature often fails in practice: organizations may publish policy statements or high-level principles but omit the operational records that make claims testable. Kroll and colleagues (2017) identify this gap explicitly, arguing that the social costs of algorithmic decision-making cannot be addressed through technical fixes that leave institutional opacity intact.
4. Fairness as a Visibility Problem and the Limits of Abstraction
Empirical work on algorithmic bias repeatedly shows that systems can fail to see some groups in ways that translate into material exclusion. Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) demonstrate that commercial gender classification systems exhibit significant accuracy disparities across skin tone and gender, with the worst performance concentrated among darker-skinned women. Their findings establish that classification error is not randomly distributed but follows the contours of underrepresentation in training data - making what appears to be a technical failure into a governance failure rooted in procurement, data curation, and evaluation scope. The implication is that invisibilization is not an accidental byproduct of model training but a predictable consequence of governance gaps in how systems are built, evaluated, and deployed.
In the broader field of algorithmic fairness, Selbst and colleagues (2019) warn that fairness interventions become dangerously misguided when they abstract models away from the social systems they reorganize - what they call the portability trap, the formalism trap, and related failure modes. That critique supports an institutional reading of error: disparity is not merely a model property but is produced by pipelines, incentives, procurement choices, and governance gaps. Barocas and Selbst (2016) deepen this structural analysis by showing how data-driven systems can produce disparate impact through facially neutral proxies, even without any intent to discriminate. The implication is that equity cannot be declared. It must be measured, monitored, and defended in production - what this framework calls verifiable equity sustained through iterative improvement.
5. Contestability and Recourse as Governance Requirements
Accountability becomes real when affected people can contest outcomes and obtain correction. The literature supports this by shifting explanation-as-curiosity to explanation-as-agency. Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell (2018) propose counterfactual explanations as a way to provide actionable reasons for outcomes without disclosing full model internals, explicitly linking explanation to rights under data protection regimes. A counterfactual explanation tells a person not why a decision was made in the abstract but what would need to change for the decision to be different - a form of knowledge that enables meaningful response rather than passive receipt of outcomes.
Ustun, Spangher, and Liu (2018) formalize actionable recourse, arguing that people should have feasible pathways to change a model's decision in high-stakes domains. The argument is normative and technical together: a system that renders decisions without offering realistic routes to reversal converts inconvenience into domination. These strands align closely with this paper's contestability commitment: absent a real appeal channel, classification becomes coercive.
Kaminski and Malgieri (2021) situate these concerns within the legal architecture of the GDPR, arguing that algorithmic impact assessments should produce multi-layered explanations directed at different audiences - individual data subjects, regulators, and auditors - with each layer calibrated to the information needs and rights of that audience. Their framework reinforces the point that contestability is not a single mechanism but a system of interlocking procedural rights and organizational capacities. The governance implication is methodological: recourse must be designed, not assumed, and its adequacy must be assessed across affected populations rather than for an idealized average user.
6. Adding the Lens of Voice: Audibility, Identity, and Algorithmic Listening
The literature above largely privileges vision metaphors, even when the actual interface is conversational. Voice forces a tighter claim: AI governance now also structures audibility - who gets accurately heard, who gets misrecognized, and whose speech becomes grounds for suspicion or exclusion. If classification can render groups invisible, algorithmic listening can render them inaudible, with equally consequential effects on access, eligibility, and institutional standing.
Empirical speech technology research makes the governance stakes concrete. Koenecke and colleagues (2020) document large racial disparities in commercial speech recognition error rates, demonstrating that listening gaps can systematically disadvantage Black speakers in voice-driven applications. The consequence domain is immediately institutional: speech-to-text mediates hiring workflows, clinical documentation, education tooling, customer service, and accessibility. In these settings, transcription error does not remain a quality nuisance - it becomes an allocation mechanism. That finding parallels Buolamwini and Gebru's (2018) results in computer vision, suggesting a cross-modal pattern: systems trained on underrepresentative data reproduce the exclusions of that data at scale, regardless of the sensory modality involved. The governance response in both cases is the same - verifiable equity requires evaluation across demographic subgroups, sustained through monitoring in deployment, not claimed at release.
Voice also functions as biometric infrastructure. Speaker recognition and voice biometrics introduce bias and privacy risks that mirror, but are not identical to, facial recognition debates. In governance terms, voice systems intensify the core asymmetry of this framework: people become more measurable while systems remain procedurally shielded. The gap in the peer-reviewed landscape noted by Browne (2015) for visual surveillance applies here: the politics of who is heard, and at what cost, remains undertheorized in AI governance discourse despite the rapid expansion of voice-facing deployments.
7. Toward Reciprocal Visibility and Reciprocal Audibility
The literature supports the core analytic move of this paper, then extends it. Reciprocal visibility names the principle that systems must become inspectable in proportion to their power to classify (Mirzoeff, 2011; Browne, 2015; Diakopoulos, 2015). Voice requires a sibling principle: reciprocal audibility. If institutions deploy algorithmic listening, then affected people must have enforceable routes to verify what the system heard, how it converted speech into decision-relevant features, and how errors are corrected. This is not a call for full openness but for defensible pathways: logs, evaluation evidence across speech communities, contestation procedures, and provenance controls.
The governance framework articulated in this paper translates these principles into an auditable standard. Traceability practices (Gebru et al., 2018; Mitchell et al., 2019), actionable transparency (Kroll et al., 2017; Diakopoulos, 2015), verifiable equity (Barocas & Selbst, 2016; Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018; Selbst et al., 2019), and operational contestability (Wachter et al., 2018; Ustun et al., 2018; Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021) translate cleanly into both visibility and voice governance requirements. Raji and colleagues (2020) provide the organizational chassis: an end-to-end auditing framework that makes each of these commitments institutionally legible as a process requirement rather than a value statement. The gap in the peer-reviewed landscape is integration: most research treats speech recognition disparity, documentation practices, and contestability as separate conversations. A visibility-plus-audibility framework gives a unifying analytic that stays board-relevant without sacrificing scholarly rigor.
Across these literatures, a consistent pattern emerges: governance failures are rarely failures of the model alone. They are failures of the machinery - the missing inventory, the absent controls, the undocumented assessments, the unenforced gates, the unmonitored deployment. Legitimacy, on this account, is not a residue of good intentions. It is a durable asset produced by evidence (Browne, 2015; Mirzoeff, 2011; Diakopoulos, 2015; Raji et al., 2020). And like any asset, it can be depleted by the absence of the practices that maintain it.

Conceptual Framework: AI Governance as a Politics of Algorithmic Visibility
This paper reframes AI governance as governance of algorithmic perception and institutional voice. The starting claim is direct: AI governance does not sit beside operations as commentary or compliance overhead. It produces the conditions under which people become legible, decisions become enforceable, and power becomes defensible or contestable. Rather than treating governance as a technical program of performance, safety, and compliance metrics, this framework reads it as a politics of visibility that organizes who becomes readable, who is erased, and who retains the capacity to challenge automated outcomes.
1. Core Problem Configuration: Two Recurrent Governance Failures
Two structural failures anchor the theory and recur across empirical cases, regardless of domain or modality.
The first is asymmetric visibility. Individuals become hypervisible through data traces - their behaviors, attributes, transactions, and associations are rendered legible and actionable - while the decision mechanisms that act on those traces remain opaque, difficult to inspect, and harder still to challenge or prove. This asymmetry is not incidental to AI deployment; it is its default condition. Browne (2015) establishes the genealogical weight of this asymmetry: the history of surveillance in Western societies is inseparable from the racialized management of populations, and contemporary algorithmic systems inherit that asymmetry even when they encode no explicit racial logic. Mirzoeff (2011) frames the political stakes: authority is constituted through the power to organize visuality - to decide what is seen, by whom, and under what frame - while countervisuality names the demand for the right to look back.
The second failure is invisibilization. Where asymmetric visibility describes the condition of being seen too much and too unevenly, invisibilization describes its complement: groups rendered statistically absent or distorted by training and measurement gaps become systemically misrecognized or excluded. Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) demonstrate this empirically in commercial gender classification, finding that error rates are highest for darker-skinned women - a population underrepresented in benchmark training sets. The governance implication that Barocas and Selbst (2016) draw from the broader disparate impact literature is structural: data-driven systems can produce discriminatory effects through facially neutral proxies without any intent to discriminate, and without any explict encoding of protected characteristics. Invisibilization is therefore not a byproduct of model training; it is a predictable consequence of governance gaps in procurement, data curation, evaluation scope, and production monitoring.
2. Normative-Analytic Hinge: Reciprocal Visibility and Reciprocal Audibility
The framework proposes reciprocal visibility as a proportionality principle for addressing asymmetric visibility: if systems see the world, affected publics must be able to see systems enough to secure accountability. This is not a demand for full transparency or unrestricted access to model internals. It is a demand for sufficient inspectability - enough to support notice, contestation, and correction. Reciprocal visibility is specified through three rights held by affected persons and three organizational capacities that institutions must maintain.
The three rights are: (1) notice of AI intervention in consequential decisions; (2) meaningful contestation - not merely the formal existence of an appeal channel but a pathway that is actionable and substantively accessible; and (3) correction when error is demonstrated. Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell (2018) ground the second right in data protection law through counterfactual explanation - telling a person not why a decision was made in the abstract but what would need to change for the outcome to differ. Ustun, Spangher, and Liu (2018) formalize the third as actionable recourse, requiring feasible pathways to change a model's decision in high-stakes domains. Kaminski and Malgieri (2021) situate all three within the legal architecture of the GDPR, arguing that algorithmic impact assessments must produce multi-layered explanations calibrated to the information needs and legal standing of individuals, regulators, and auditors alike.
The three organizational capacities are: (1) proof - the ability to reconstruct what happened and why through inspectable evidence trails; (2) explanation - the ability to account for decisions in ways that are actionable rather than merely technical; and (3) rapid remediation - the operational capacity to correct errors, update systems, and document what changed. Raji and colleagues (2020) translate these into an end-to-end auditing framework that spans problem formulation, artifact testing, and deployment monitoring, making each capacity institutionally legible as a process requirement rather than a value statement.
The framework then extends the same proportionality logic to voice, understood in two coupled senses. Voice as listening describes how institutions capture speech, convert it into features, and operationalize it as risk, trust, or eligibility. Voice as institutional speech act describes how AI systems speak on behalf of organizations, turning generated outputs into communications that can bind the institution in disputes, regulatory proceedings, and courts. These two senses are analytically distinct but operationally entangled: the same conversational interface can simultaneously misrecognize input (a listening failure) and misrepresent policy (a speech failure).
This dual analysis yields reciprocal audibility as the voice-specific sibling of reciprocal visibility: if institutions deploy algorithmic listening, affected people must have enforceable routes to verify what the system heard, how it converted speech into decision-relevant features, and how errors are corrected. Koenecke and colleagues (2020) establish the empirical basis: racial disparities in commercial speech recognition are large enough to convert transcription error into allocation mechanism in institutional settings such as hiring, clinical documentation, and education. That finding places ASR performance squarely within the fairness literature surveyed in Section 4 of the literature review - the governance response is not merely engineering improvement but the kind of subgroup evaluation, production monitoring, and verifiable equity that Gebru and colleagues (2018) and Mitchell and colleagues (2019) argue must be documented and defended across the system lifecycle.
3. Governance Machinery as Sociotechnical Theory of Accountability
The framework treats governance machinery as the sociotechnical form through which legitimacy is produced. The concept resists both the checklist reading - governance as a set of boxes to tick - and the principles reading - governance as a set of values to endorse. Instead, governance machinery designates a repeatable operational sequence whose links are empirically inspectable: inventory, controls, assessments, evidence, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting. Each link is a site where institutional power either remains asymmetrical or becomes contestable.
This sequence yields a concrete and evaluable definition of defensibility: a system is governed when an organization can produce evidence of what occurred, justify why it occurred, and remediate rapidly, in ways that preserve enforceable rights to notice, contestation, and correction. Kroll and colleagues (2017) supply the constitutional framing: algorithmic decision-making implicates rule-of-law values that technical approaches cannot substitute for, and the organizational practices that surround model use are as legally and politically significant as the models themselves. Diakopoulos (2015) supplies the investigative framing: accountability requires reliable routes to observation, because without inspectability, organizations can neither verify their own claims nor credibly contest allegations of harm.
4. Interface Laundering as a Mechanism Claim
The framework advances a specific mechanism claim for conversational and voice-facing AI: interface laundering. Conversational interfaces tend to launder consequential institutional decisions into informal interaction - shifting policy communication, allocation, and risk assessment into a register that feels transactional and low-stakes - unless governance forces outputs back into the evidentiary domain through policy-fidelity controls, escalation routes, logging, ownership, and documented correction processes.
The mechanism operates in two directions. Outward-facing interface laundering occurs when organizations deploy customer-facing conversational AI without adequate controls for policy fidelity, escalation, and version management. Policy communication then becomes institutional speech in the eyes of customers, regulators, and courts, and even mundane artifacts - screenshots of chatbot exchanges - become legally and reputationally binding evidence. Inward-facing interface laundering occurs when employees route sensitive work through external conversational tools because organizational policy lags practice and governance fails to provide sanctioned pathways and discovery signals. In both cases, the mechanism functions identically: governance opacity converts a technical convenience into an accountability liability by ensuring that what was said, by which system version, under what prompts, and with what escalation, cannot be reconstructed.
Interface laundering is not merely a description of risk. It is a testable claim that directs the analysis toward specific governance artifacts: source control, ownership and versioning records, monitoring logs, escalation documentation, and correction timelines. Their absence is not a nuisance but a finding.
Methods and Materials
The analysis applies a comparative, qualitative multiple-case design organized as governance reconstruction. Each incident is treated as a trace of three analytically distinct but empirically entangled elements: algorithmic perception (the visibility, voice, or hybrid modality through which the system apprehends its subjects), the coupling between model output and institutional action, and governance capacity under stress. The design draws on the case study research tradition in which multiple articles of evidence are brought together to illuminate an empirical case as a whole, and in which an explicit theoretical framework, established prior to data collection, disciplines inference and prevents post-hoc rationalization.
1. Research Design and Logic of Inference
Inference is built through two complementary strategies. Process tracing within cases reconstructs the sequence of events that connects model outputs to institutional actions and downstream harms, with the explicit goal of identifying the first break in governance machinery and how it propagated into public failure or harm. The question for process tracing is not whether harm occurred but when the machinery that could have prevented or contained it was absent, fragile, or bypassed. Pattern matching across cases tests whether observed failures cluster around recurring missing components - absent gates, weak evidence integrity, limited vendor audit rights, fragile recourse design - and whether voice-mediated systems exhibit higher rates of interface laundering when evidence controls are not enforced. Pattern matching is used in the construct-validity sense: theoretical patterns function as hypotheses about what should be observable in the data, and confidence increases when cases match more complex, uniquely identifying patterns rather than generic ones.
2. Case Selection
Case selection follows purposeful, maximum-variation sampling across consequence domains and modalities. The goal is not representativeness in a statistical sense but analytic breadth: the corpus must include visibility-dominant systems, voice or conversational systems, and hybrid high-impact scoring regimes, across institutional contexts that apply different pressures to governance machinery. Seven cases are included.
The Amazon recruiting tool case (labor gatekeeping, visibility-dominant) illustrates how a system trained on historical hiring patterns learns to downgrade applications associated with women, and how the governance failure is best characterized not as bias-exists but as absent thresholds, missing proxy review, undefined decision rights, and unmonitored output-to-action coupling.
The Clearview AI case (biometric data provenance and multi-jurisdiction enforcement, visibility-dominant) illustrates how the absence of governance over data collection and supplier relationships produces regulatory and reputational consequences that extend well beyond any single deployment context.
The Zillow Offers case (capital allocation at scale, visibility-dominant) illustrates how forecasting error, when coupled tightly to high-stakes action at portfolio scale, produces balance-sheet exposure that governance stress-testing and exposure controls could have bounded.
The Dutch Toeslagenaffaire case (administrative due process failure, hybrid) illustrates how risk scoring combined with a fragile appeal architecture can produce systemic harm at population scale, with the governance failure concentrated in the weakness of contestation, correction, and oversight pathways.
The COMPAS case (contested fairness under vendor opacity, hybrid) illustrates how competing fairness definitions and proprietary opacity combine to make institutional justification of authority structurally impossible, regardless of which fairness criterion is applied.
The Samsung and ChatGPT case (internal shadow AI leakage, voice-conversational) illustrates inward-facing interface laundering: when policy lags practice and governance fails to provide sanctioned pathways and discovery signals, employees route sensitive work through external conversational tools, creating predictable leak vectors that evade auditability.
The Air Canada chatbot case (institutional speech and liability, voice-conversational) illustrates outward-facing interface laundering: customer-facing AI deployed without adequate policy-fidelity controls, versioning, logging, and remediation processes concentrates accountability because the chatbot's outputs function as official statements in the eyes of customers, regulators, and courts.
3. Data Sources: The Evidence Dossier Approach
For each case, an evidence dossier is assembled from publicly available materials. The dossier structure prioritizes source type in the following order: public investigative reporting and reconstructed timelines; regulatory findings and enforcement notices where applicable; court decisions and legally relevant outcomes where present; organizational statements and post-incident policy changes; and publicly available technical artifacts including model documentation, audit disclosures, and monitoring notes. No single source type is treated as authoritative; triangulation across types is the primary trustworthiness mechanism.
A methodologically central commitment is that the absence of documentation is treated as data, not as missing data. When dossiers lack audit artifacts, monitoring evidence, ownership records, or contestation documentation, that absence is interpreted as an empirically meaningful signal: accountability claims cannot be supported with inspectable evidence, which is itself a governance failure. This treatment is consistent with the paper's theoretical insistence that governance is an evidentiary project - if an organization cannot produce the chain of evidence, the system was deployed rather than governed.
4. Analytic Coding Scheme
Each case is coded along five structured dimensions that operationalize the paper's theoretical commitments while remaining auditable across the corpus.
Modality codes whether the system operates primarily through visual/classificatory perception, through conversational or voice-facing interfaces, or through a hybrid combination. Modality is analytically important because it shapes the evidentiary surface: visibility-dominant systems primarily produce classification records, while voice-mediated systems must additionally produce speech capture logs, versioning records, and escalation documentation.
Coupling codes how tightly model output binds institutional action, ranging from advisory outputs that inform human judgment to determinative outputs that directly trigger consequences, and from low-scale individual decisions to high-scale deployments where errors compound across populations. The Zillow case is paradigmatic for high-stakes coupling; COMPAS for determinative outputs in liberty-affecting domains.
Asymmetry codes the extent to which people become measurable while mechanisms remain opaque - the core power relation named by Browne (2015) and Mirzoeff (2011) and given empirical form in the audit literature (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018; Raji et al., 2020). Asymmetry is coded by assessing whether affected parties and oversight bodies have access to sufficient information about the system to form a contestable account of how it operates.
Recourse architecture codes the presence and strength of the three rights: notice of AI intervention, meaningful contestation, and correction. This dimension draws directly on the contestability literature (Wachter et al., 2018; Ustun et al., 2018; Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021) and is coded by asking whether a person subject to a consequential decision could, in practice, obtain an actionable explanation and a realistic path to reversal.
Governance machinery completeness codes whether the organization can evidence the full operational chain: inventory, controls, assessments, evidence, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting. This dimension is the most directly diagnostic: missing links are the primary findings of the governance reconstruction process, and their identification is the basis for cross-case comparison.
5. Evaluative Indices
To keep normative commitments testable and to prevent the analysis from drifting into principles talk disconnected from evidence trails, the coding scheme is anchored to two evaluative indices. These are not psychometric measures; they are audit heuristics designed to discipline interpretation and ensure that theoretical claims map onto observable artifacts or observable absences.
The Reciprocal Visibility Index (RVI) assesses whether affected parties and oversight bodies can inspect decision pathways sufficiently to contest and correct outcomes. It scores the three rights and three organizational capacities specified in the framework, and is sensitive to whether rights and capacities are formally declared or operationally evidenced - a policy statement is not a proof.
The Reciprocal Audibility Index (RAI) assesses whether conversational and voice-mediated systems generate durable evidence and enforceable accountability through logging, versioning, escalation, and remediation timelines - particularly when system outputs function as institutional speech. The RAI is specifically designed to detect interface laundering: systems that appear conversationally accessible but structurally prevent the reconstruction of what was said, by which version, under what prompts, and with what correction pathway.
Both indices use a three-point rubric: 0 = absent (no evidence trail; no meaningful recourse); 1 = partial (some documentation or recourse, but non-actionable or structurally fragile); 2 = robust (actionable notice, contestation, and correction backed by strong proof, explanation, and remediation capacity). The rubric is applied to dossier evidence, not to organizational self-reporting, and scores are treated as interpretive anchors for the case narratives rather than as stand-alone quantitative outputs.
6. Cross-Case Synthesis
Synthesis proceeds in two connected stages. Within-case process tracing, described above, produces a governance reconstruction for each incident: a sequenced account of where machinery broke or never existed and how that gap propagated into harm or institutional failure. Each reconstruction explicitly identifies the first missing link in the operational chain and the downstream consequences that followed from it.
Across-case pattern matching then tests whether failures cluster around recurring missing components - a result that would strengthen the claim that governance failures are structural rather than idiosyncratic - and whether voice-mediated systems exhibit higher rates of interface laundering when evidence controls are absent. The Samsung and Air Canada cases serve as the primary voice-facing contrasts; Amazon, Clearview, Zillow, and COMPAS provide the visibility-dominant baseline; the Dutch case anchors the analysis of recourse architecture failure at population scale.
7. Trustworthiness
Trustworthiness is treated as endogenous to the paper's theory of evidence: claims about governance are only as strong as the traceable record that supports them, and the analysis holds itself to the same standard it applies to the cases it examines. Three mechanisms address the primary threats to validity.
Triangulation across source types - press narratives, regulatory materials, court-relevant outcomes, and technical artifacts - reduces dependence on any single account and surfaces contradictions that single-source analysis would conceal. Where sources conflict, the conflict is noted and treated as analytically informative rather than resolved by editorial preference.
Negative-case logic actively seeks examples of incidents that did not escalate because governance machinery contained them, counteracting the scandal-selection bias that afflicts research built exclusively on high-profile failures. Where containment cases can be identified and documented, they provide a comparative baseline against which governance machinery in the primary corpus can be assessed.
Documentary absence as data, already discussed under the dossier approach, functions simultaneously as a validity mechanism: it prevents the analysis from treating the absence of counter-evidence as evidence of governance adequacy, and ensures that the evidentiary bar applied to institutional claims mirrors the bar applied to the paper's own conclusions.
In line with the compress-without-opacity orientation established in the methodology blueprint, the write-up prioritizes warrant-preserving synthesis: narrative is compressed where it can be without erasing the evidentiary links that permit an external reader to reconstruct how conclusions follow from dossiers. Consequence modeling is applied to bound claims by stakes, scope conditions, and foreseeable misuse, ensuring that the paper's governance recommendations are calibrated to the institutional contexts in which they will be applied rather than stated as universal prescriptions.

Analysis
The analysis applies a comparative, qualitative multiple–case design organized as “governance reconstruction,” treating each incident as a trace of (i) algorithmic perception (visibility, voice, or hybrid), (ii) the coupling between model output and institutional action, and (iii) governance capacity under stress. Evidence is assembled through a dossier approach that prioritizes public investigative reporting and timelines, regulatory findings where applicable, court decisions or legally relevant outcomes where present, organizational statements and post-incident changes, and any publicly available technical artifacts; critically, the absence of documentation is treated as empirically meaningful rather than as mere missing data, because it signals that accountability claims cannot be supported with inspectable evidence. Case selection is purposeful and maximum-variation across consequence domains and modalities, spanning labor gatekeeping (Amazon recruiting), biometric data provenance/enforcement (Clearview AI), capital allocation at scale (Zillow Offers), administrative due process failure (Dutch Toeslagenaffaire), vendor-mediated risk scoring and contested fairness (COMPAS), internal “shadow AI” leakage (Samsung + ChatGPT), and customer-facing institutional speech (Air Canada chatbot).
Within each dossier, coding operationalizes the paper’s core theoretical hinge—AI governance as a politics of algorithmic visibility and voice—by scoring how power, legibility, and contestability are distributed. Each case is coded by modality (visibility, voice, hybrid), coupling (how tightly outputs bind decisions), asymmetry (the extent to which people become measurable while mechanisms remain opaque), recourse architecture (strength of notice, contestation, and correction pathways), and governance machinery completeness (whether the organization can evidence a full chain from inventory through audit-ready reporting). Rather than treating governance as a checklist, the analysis treats this “machinery” as a sociotechnical sequence whose links can be empirically inspected: inventory, controls, assessments, evidence, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting. This enables a consistent comparative logic: the same abstract principles are forced to “show up” as concrete artifacts (or absences) across heterogeneous institutional contexts.
To keep normative commitments testable, the analysis uses two evaluative indices aligned with reciprocal visibility and reciprocal audibility. A reciprocal visibility index assesses whether affected parties and oversight bodies can inspect decision pathways sufficiently to contest and correct outcomes; a reciprocal audibility index assesses whether conversational/voice systems generate durable evidence and enforceable accountability through logging, versioning, escalation, and remediation timelines, particularly when system outputs function as institutional speech. The indices are not offered as psychometric measures; they are audit heuristics designed to discipline interpretation and prevent the analysis from drifting into “principles talk” disconnected from evidence trails.
Inference proceeds through a two-stage synthesis that combines process tracing and cross-case pattern matching. First, within-case process tracing identifies the earliest break in governance machinery and reconstructs how it propagates into downstream harms or public failure. Second, across-case pattern matching tests whether observed failures cluster around recurring missing components—especially gates, evidence integrity, vendor audit rights, and recourse design—and whether voice-mediated systems exhibit higher risk of “interface laundering” unless evidence controls are enforced. Pattern matching is used explicitly as a construct-validity strategy: theoretical patterns operate as hypotheses about what should be observed, and confidence increases when cases match more complex, uniquely identifying patterns rather than generic ones.
Trustworthiness is treated as endogenous to the paper’s theory of evidence: governance claims are only as strong as the traceable record that supports them. The analysis therefore relies on triangulation across source types (press narratives, regulatory materials, court-relevant outcomes), negative-case logic to reduce “scandal selection bias,” and the systematic interpretation of documentary absence (missing audit artifacts, missing monitoring evidence, unclear decision rights) as data about non-governance rather than as an analytic inconvenience. In line with the paper’s “compress without opacity” stance, the write-up prioritizes warrant-preserving synthesis: it compresses narrative while retaining the evidentiary links that permit an external reader to reconstruct how conclusions follow from dossiers ().
Results
Across the seven-case corpus, the primary cross-case result is that high-profile AI failures are rarely best explained as “model failure” alone; they typically follow earlier, quieter breakdowns in governance structure that remain invisible until scrutiny forces reconstruction. The dossiers converge on governance failure as missing machinery: organizations treat governance as commentary beside operations, rather than as operational equipment capable of producing proof under stress. When incidents escalate, what collapses is not only technical performance but institutional defensibility—the capacity to show what happened, justify why it happened, and change what happens next in a way that preserves enforceable rights to notice, contestation, and correction.
A second result is that failures cluster around predictable “break points” in the machinery chain, most notably the absence of gates, weak evidence integrity, limited supplier audit rights, and fragile recourse design. The synthesis explicitly frames this as a pattern-matching result: case variation in domain (labor, welfare, criminal justice, consumer service, internal operations) does not eliminate the recurrence of these missing links; instead, variation changes the harm pathway and who bears costs. The Amazon recruiting case, for example, is interpreted not merely as “bias exists,” but as a governance failure involving missing thresholds, proxy review, decision rights, and monitoring—i.e., absent gating and weak feedback control over how outputs couple to action. In this reading, “bias incidents” are symptoms of institutional incapacity to instrument and correct sociotechnical systems, not isolated technical surprises.
Third, the cases support the paper’s central power diagnosis: AI governance operates as a politics of legibility in which asymmetric visibility is structurally common—individuals become hypervisible through data traces while the decision mechanisms that act on those traces remain hard to inspect, challenge, or prove. This asymmetry is compounded by invisibilization, where training and measurement gaps render some groups effectively “unseen,” translating statistical underrepresentation into practical exclusion or misrecognition. Importantly, the analysis treats asymmetry and invisibilization not as ethical labels but as empirically detectable conditions: they appear when dossiers lack inspectable documentation, subgroup evaluation evidence, and production monitoring that would allow an affected party—or regulator—to contest outcomes in a bounded and actionable way.
Fourth, voice and conversational interfaces introduce a distinct mechanism—interface laundering—that reshapes how accountability is produced and evaded. The analysis finds that conversational systems tend to launder consequential institutional decisions into informal interaction unless governance forces them back into the evidentiary domain through policy-fidelity controls, escalation routes, logging, ownership, and documented correction processes. Air Canada functions as the outward-facing archetype: customer-facing AI concentrates accountability because policy communication becomes institutional speech “in the eyes of customers, regulators, and courts,” and even seemingly mundane artifacts (screenshots) become legally and reputationally binding evidence. Correspondingly, the synthesis identifies concrete governance requirements for such systems: source control, ownership/versioning, monitoring, and documented correction timelines. Samsung functions as the inward-facing archetype: employees routed sensitive work through an external conversational tool because policy lagged practice and governance failed to provide a sanctioned pathway plus discovery signals; conversational use evaded auditability because inputs, boundaries, and reporting channels were not operationalized as controls. The comparative finding is not that “voice is riskier,” but that voice alters the evidentiary surface: without durable logs and versioning, organizations cannot reliably reconstruct what was “said,” by which system version, under what prompts, and with what escalation, making contestation and correction structurally harder.
Finally, the results synthesize into a governance standard oriented toward legitimacy as an evidence-backed asset rather than performance as a standalone metric. The paper’s abstract-level contribution is operationalized through reciprocal visibility (rights to notice, contestation, correction; organizational capacities for proof, explanation, rapid remediation) and translated into auditable controls such as lifecycle traceability (“system life sheet”), inspectable transparency (documentation, logs, tests, evidence), monitored equity, actionable contestability for high-impact decisions, supplier governance that prevents black-box risk transfer, and incident readiness (detection, stop thresholds, playbooks, remediation). Read through the cross-case patterns, these controls are not aspirational; they are precisely the missing artifacts repeatedly exposed by governance reconstruction when incidents force institutions to account for their systems.

Discussion
The seven cases examined in this paper converge on a finding that the positionality statement anticipates but that the data must independently support: high-profile AI failures are not primarily failures of technical performance. They are failures of governance machinery - the missing inventory, the absent controls, the unenforced gates, the undocumented assessments, the unmonitored deployment. That pattern matters because it relocates the problem and therefore the remedy. If AI harms were random technical failures, engineering improvement would suffice. Because they follow predictable breaks in institutional infrastructure, governance reconstruction is required.
1. Asymmetric Visibility as an Architecture, Not an Accident
The manifesto names asymmetric visibility as an architecture of power. The case analysis confirms this diagnosis in institutional terms. Across the corpus, affected persons are rendered measurable - their behaviors, attributes, and associations captured, scored, and acted upon - while the decision mechanisms that act on those traces remain difficult to inspect, challenge, or prove. What the manifesto calls an architecture of power, the coding scheme operationalizes as a measurable gap between inspection capacity and deployment scale: the higher the stakes and the larger the population affected, the more pronounced the asymmetry, because high-stakes systems tend to operate at exactly the institutional junctures where opacity is most financially and legally convenient.
Browne's (2015) genealogical account of racializing surveillance grounds this finding historically: the conditions that make asymmetric visibility possible in contemporary algorithmic systems are not novel. They inherit the logic of surveillance regimes that have always concentrated the costs of being seen on populations least positioned to contest that exposure. Mirzoeff's (2011) countervisuality framework names what reciprocal visibility demands in return: not merely access to information, but the institutional recognition that affected people hold a right to look back at the systems that classify them. The governance standard the manifesto demands - traceability, useful transparency, contestability - is the operational translation of that right into auditable practice.
The peer-from-our-doubts frame adds a dimension to this finding that accountability theory has not yet fully integrated. If an AI system inherits the questions an organization stopped asking, then asymmetric visibility is not only a governance failure in the external sense - a failure to disclose to those classified. It is also an internal epistemic failure: the organization that deploys a classification system without adequate inspection infrastructure is also a system that has stopped asking what would happen if its assumptions were wrong, if its training data was incomplete, if its proxies were discriminatory. Governance machinery, on this reading, is not only a mechanism for external accountability. It is a practice of institutional self-examination. The inventory, the controls, the assessments, the gates - each is also a moment at which the organization is forced to ask the questions the model cannot ask for itself.
2. Invisibilization, Institutional Silence, and the Limits of Declared Equity
The manifesto's second refusal - of the quiet violence of effacement - is given empirical texture by Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) and the structural analysis in Barocas and Selbst (2016). Both establish that invisibilization is not accidental but the predictable consequence of governance gaps in procurement, evaluation scope, and production monitoring. Wherever algorithmic scoring systems operate without adequate subgroup evaluation and contestation pathways, the groups most likely to bear the costs of classification error are precisely those whose underrepresentation in training and testing rendered them invisible to the system in the first place. The Dutch Toeslagenaffaire and COMPAS cases extend this logic beyond computer vision: in administrative welfare systems and criminal justice risk scoring alike, the populations most harmed are consistently those whose complexity was not represented in the training regime and whose contestation rights were most structurally attenuated.
The peer-from-our-doubts framing sharpens this finding considerably. A model trained on historical data does not merely reflect what existed; it reflects what was measured, what was recorded, what was considered worth knowing. The populations effaced by underrepresentation in training data are precisely the populations about whom the institution had the fewest doubts - not because those populations posed the fewest governance challenges, but because the institution was not asking the right questions about them. Invisibilization in AI systems is therefore a disclosure: it makes visible the prior epistemic exclusions of the institutions that produced the systems. Buolamwini and Gebru's (2018) benchmark disparities do not only reveal a training data problem. They reveal whose accuracy was treated as worth caring about, whose face was worth including in a test set, whose error rate was assumed to be someone else's problem.
The governance implication, which the manifesto states directly and Selbst and colleagues (2019) ground theoretically, is that equity cannot be declared. It must be measured, monitored, and defended in production. The portability trap warns that fairness properties asserted in development do not automatically hold in deployment; the formalism trap warns that mathematical constraints substituted for institutional accountability produce the appearance of equity without its substance. Verifiable equity - the manifesto's third governance demand - is the organizational form of those warnings: a commitment that is evidenced, not asserted, and that surfaces the questions the model cannot ask about its own blind spots.
3. Interface Laundering, Institutional Speech, and the Evidentiary Surface of Voice
The voice analysis opens a dimension that the manifesto gestures toward and the peer-from-our-doubts frame renders fully legible. The manifesto frames AI governance as structuring audibility as well as visibility: who gets accurately heard, who gets misrecognized, and whose speech becomes grounds for suspicion or exclusion. Interface laundering names the mechanism through which conversational systems evade accountability - consequential institutional decisions are shifted into informal interaction registers, appearing transactional and low-stakes, unless governance forces them back into the evidentiary domain through logging, versioning, policy-fidelity controls, and documented correction processes.
The Air Canada and Samsung cases are complementary archetypes. Air Canada's outward-facing laundering: a customer-facing chatbot deployed without source control, versioning, or correction timelines concentrated accountability because the system's outputs functioned as institutional speech in the eyes of customers and courts. Screenshots became evidence. The system's informality was its legal exposure. Samsung's inward-facing laundering: employees routing sensitive work through external tools because governance failed to provide sanctioned pathways created predictable leak vectors. In both cases, the mechanism is identical: the absence of logging, versioning, and escalation documentation ensures that what was said, by which version, under what prompts, and with what correction pathway, cannot be reconstructed - which means it cannot be contested.
The peer-from-our-doubts frame transforms this finding from a risk description into a diagnostic claim. When a conversational AI system is deployed without governance infrastructure, the outputs it produces are not only uncontestable; they are unattributable. The system speaks with institutional authority while sheltering the institution from the questions that authority should require it to answer: What policy was this output based on? Who authorized that policy? Has it been updated? Was it tested for consistency? Interface laundering, on this reading, is not only a technical failure of logging. It is the deployment of institutional voice without institutional accountability - the automation of speech acts that the institution would not have permitted a human employee to make without documentation, oversight, and correction pathways.
Koenecke and colleagues (2020) establish the demographic dimension: when speech recognition systems perform unevenly across demographic groups, transcription error becomes allocation mechanism. The populations most disadvantaged by ASR disparities are those whose speech patterns were least represented in training - which is to say, the populations about whom the institution had the fewest questions during system development. The peer-from-our-doubts frame makes explicit what the technical literature tends to leave implicit: the gaps in ASR performance are not neutral measurement errors. They are the material residue of whose voice was imagined as the default input.
4. Governance Machinery as Institutional Self-Knowledge
The paper's most consequential analytical move is treating governance machinery as the primary unit of analysis and reform. The manifesto grounds that move normatively: because governance is a politics of visibility, the relevant question is not whether a system performed well in aggregate but whether the institutional infrastructure required to make performance contestable existed, was documented, and held under stress. Raji and colleagues (2020) provide the organizational chassis; Gebru and colleagues (2018) and Mitchell and colleagues (2019) provide the documentation substrate; Kaminski and Malgieri (2021) provide the legal architecture. Together, these contributions establish that governance is an evidentiary project: a system is governed when an organization can produce a chain of evidence - from inventory through audit-ready reporting - that allows a third party to reconstruct what was done, why it was done, and what changed after failures.
The peer-from-our-doubts frame extends this claim in a direction the governance literature has not yet fully occupied. If AI systems are peers constituted by institutional doubts, biases, and silenced devil's advocates, then governance machinery is not only a mechanism for external accountability. It is the institutional practice of making the organization's own epistemic commitments inspectable - forcing the questions back into visibility that the model had quietly foreclosed. An inventory that documents what data was used is also a record of what data was not used, and why. An assessment that tests subgroup performance is also an acknowledgment that the institution's default assumptions were not universal. A gate that prevents deployment until equity metrics are demonstrated is also an institutional insistence that the question of whose accuracy matters be answered before consequences are imposed.
On this reading, governance machinery is a form of institutional devil's advocacy: a set of structured practices designed to surface the doubts the organization is most tempted to foreclose, the perspectives it is most likely to exclude, and the populations it is most likely to harm by assuming their absence from its models is someone else's problem. The manifesto's demand for a governance standard that is simple, auditable, and actionable is therefore not only a compliance agenda. It is an invitation to institutional self-examination - to ask, at each link in the machinery chain, not only whether the system works, but whose doubts it carries and whose it silenced.

Conclusion
This paper opened with a claim the manifesto states as political rather than technical: AI governance decides who becomes legible, who disappears, who gains time, who pays the price, and who can contest. The analysis has sought to give that claim empirical and institutional content - to show, across seven cases, what governance failure looks like when it is treated not as an engineering problem but as a breakdown in the machinery through which organizations produce accountability, evidence, and legitimacy.
Three findings stand as the paper's primary contributions. First, governance failures in AI are structurally predictable rather than idiosyncratic. They cluster around recurring missing components - absent gating, weak evidence integrity, fragile recourse design, vendor opacity - that persist across domains as varied as labor markets, welfare systems, criminal justice, capital allocation, and customer service. That recurrence is the paper's strongest argument for treating governance machinery as a durable object of organizational investment rather than a compliance overhead that scales with deployment.
Second, the extension of the visibility framework to voice is analytically productive and practically necessary. Interface laundering names a real mechanism that alters the evidentiary surface of AI governance in ways that documentation-oriented governance frameworks have not yet adequately addressed. Conversational systems require not only the governance infrastructure that visibility-dominant systems require - traceability, equity monitoring, contestability, supplier governance - but the additional infrastructure of durable speech: logging that persists, versioning that is enforced, escalation that is operationalized, and correction timelines that are documented and auditable.
Third, the peer-from-our-doubts frame produces a governance implication that exceeds the accountability literature's current horizon. If AI systems are constituted by institutional doubts, biases, and foreclosed devil's advocates, then governance machinery is the practice of making those epistemic commitments inspectable. The inventory is not only a compliance record; it is a disclosure of what the institution chose to measure and what it chose to ignore. The subgroup evaluation is not only a fairness test; it is an acknowledgment that the institution's default subject was not universal. The contestation pathway is not only a legal safeguard; it is a structural commitment to the possibility that the institution was wrong. Governance, on this account, is not the management of AI risk. It is the institutional practice of remaining open to the questions the model cannot ask about itself.
The manifesto closes with a sentence that frames the normative ambition in terms the peer-from-our-doubts frame makes fully legible: we want powerful systems, but we demand accountable ones. Legitimacy is the condition of durable value. That demand is not a constraint on innovation. It is the epistemic condition under which innovation remains oriented toward the populations it classifies, the communities it affects, and the futures it forecloses or enables. A system that cannot be contested is a system that has stopped asking whether it should. Governance is the institutional practice of not stopping.
The limitations of this analysis are real. The case corpus, while purposefully selected for maximum variation, remains anchored in contexts where public documentation was available - which means it is itself subject to a visibility bias: the cases that can be reconstructed are the cases that became visible enough to generate press coverage, regulatory attention, or litigation. The governance failures that never escalated, or that were contained by machinery that left no public trace, remain outside the frame. Future research should develop methods for accessing non-public governance records, for working with organizations willing to open internal dossiers, and for identifying the negative cases - the deployments that were governed well - that this corpus systematically underrepresents.
What the present analysis can offer is a framework for asking the question that the peer-from-our-doubts frame keeps returning to: not what the system does, but what the system's existence discloses about the organization that built it, deployed it, and declined to govern it. The answer, across seven cases, is consistent. It discloses an organization that was more comfortable seeing than being seen, more comfortable classifying than being classified, and more comfortable deploying power than accounting for it. Governance is the practice of refusing that comfort - of insisting, at every link in the machinery chain, that the organization can prove what happened, explain why it happened, and change what happens next in ways that preserve the rights of those its systems were always already looking at.

Introduction
Before the headline, the regulator’s notice, or the court’s written reasons, there is usually a quieter breakdown: a system begins to act on people at scale while the organization lacks the evidentiary infrastructure to notice, contest, or correct what it is doing. Consider the now-canonical hiring example in which a résumé screener learned to penalize the word “women’s” (as in “women’s chess club captain”), reproducing the gendered structure of historical hiring data long before any public scandal forced the failure into view. (The Guardian) The governance failure in such cases is temporally prior to the public failure, and that sequencing matters because it locates the primary object of inquiry upstream of “model performance” and downstream of institutional choice. In this sense, AI governance is best understood as a politics of visibility: a set of organizational practices that decide which decision pathways become legible, which harms remain structurally unobservable, and which affected parties retain meaningful capacity to challenge outcomes that classify, score, allocate, and deny. The stakes are already institutionalized in the present regulatory moment, where risk-based governance frameworks are consolidating alongside intensified enforcement, including voluntary but operationally detailed guidance such as NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework and binding regimes such as the EU AI Act. (NIST) This paper argues that legitimacy under these conditions behaves less like a reputational claim and more like an evidence-backed asset: it is produced, or depleted, by whether an organization can show what happened, why it happened, and what changed after harm.
A politics-of-visibility frame has a long theoretical genealogy, and naming it precisely is a condition of using its tools correctly. Surveillance studies insists that institutions do not merely observe; they govern through regimes of perception, classification, and exposure, while simultaneously shielding their own mechanisms behind procedural opacity. Browne’s account of racializing surveillance makes the historical point with methodological force: contemporary systems inherit older asymmetries of who is made knowable and who bears the costs of being rendered measurable, even when the system’s surface vocabulary is formally neutral. (Smithsonian Institution) Mirzoeff’s counterhistory of visuality then supplies the hinge from critique to governance requirement by framing “the right to look” as a demand that authority be made answerable to those it organizes. (Duke University Press) Put differently, the political problem is not that institutions see; it is that they see without being equally seeable, producing an asymmetry that converts classification into a one-way exercise of power. Algorithmic systems intensify this condition by making perception programmable and scalable across domains that now share a common technical substrate, from labor recruitment to welfare administration to customer service and biometrics. The implication for governance is specific: visibility cannot remain a metaphor for openness; it must become a capacity to inspect, contest, and force justification, proportionate to the system’s power to impose consequences.
Two recurring governance failures structure the empirical terrain, and defining both before pairing them is necessary to avoid collapsing distinct harm mechanisms. By asymmetric visibility, this paper means the condition in which individuals become hypervisible through data traces while the decision mechanisms acting on those traces remain structurally opaque and hard to challenge with evidence. By invisibilization, this paper means the complement: groups become statistically absent or distorted through training and measurement gaps, and that absence is converted into repeatable misrecognition that is difficult to litigate precisely because the harm can be attributed to “error” rather than intent. The empirical logic of invisibilization is visible in computer vision audits such as Buolamwini and Gebru’s work, which documents intersectional performance disparities in commercial gender classification and shows how benchmark composition and evaluation scope can harden into uneven error burdens. (Proceedings of Machine Learning Research) The legal-structural point is that disparate impact can emerge through ostensibly neutral proxies and institutional pipelines, which means harm can be produced even when no actor can credibly be described as harboring discriminatory intent. (Lawcat) Consequently, “fairness” cannot be treated as a property of a model abstracted from its deployment context. The fairness-and-abstraction critique shows how modular thinking and abstraction boundaries can render technical interventions ineffective, or even dangerously misguided, once they enter the sociotechnical systems that allocate opportunity and punishment. (Microsoft) Read together, these literatures reframe bias incidents as governance diagnostics: they disclose where institutional evaluation practices, procurement incentives, and monitoring thresholds failed to make exclusion legible before it became consequential.
Voice and conversational interfaces tighten the claim and expand the domain of governance from visibility to audibility. If algorithmic classification can render groups invisible, algorithmic listening can render them inaudible: misheard speech becomes an allocation mechanism when transcripts and derived features gate access to jobs, services, healthcare, or education. Koenecke and colleagues substantiate the disparity claim by documenting large racial gaps in word error rates across major commercial speech recognition systems, converting a “quality” metric into a predictable inequality channel when deployed at scale. (PubMed) Yet voice systems also introduce a second, distinct vulnerability: they speak on behalf of institutions. The Air Canada chatbot decision makes this concrete as an accountability problem rather than a UX anecdote, holding the airline liable for inaccurate policy advice delivered through a website chatbot and treating the system’s output as institutional speech that customers can reasonably rely upon. (Deeth Williams Wall) In this paper’s terms, conversational AI creates conditions for interface laundering: consequential decisions and communications are shifted into an informal interaction register that feels low-stakes, thereby evading the evidentiary practices that accountability requires unless governance forces outputs back into the domain of logs, versioning, escalation, and correction timelines. The inward-facing sibling of this mechanism is visible in organizational “shadow AI” dynamics, where employees route sensitive work through external tools and create audit voids that are difficult to reconstruct after the fact. (TechCrunch)
These failures are not best addressed by treating governance as a checklist adjacent to technical work; they require treating accountability as an evidentiary project that must be operationalized end-to-end. Diakopoulos frames algorithmic accountability in terms of making computational power investigable, emphasizing that without reliable routes to observation, both public oversight and internal self-audit collapse into narrative conflict. (Northwestern Scholars) Kroll and colleagues sharpen the institutional stakes by arguing that automated decision systems implicate rule-of-law values and require technical and procedural tools that allow compliance with fairness and due process constraints to be verified rather than asserted. (Intellectual Life @ PCL) Raji and colleagues translate this orientation into organizational practice through an end-to-end framework for internal algorithmic auditing that treats documentation, evaluation, and monitoring as durable governance artifacts rather than optional add-ons. (AI Now Institute) The documentation substrate is specified by proposals such as datasheets for datasets and model cards, which formalize what must be recorded about training data, intended use, limitations, and subgroup performance for systems to be contestable across time and organizational turnover. (Astrophysics Data System) Finally, contestability becomes real when affected persons can obtain actionable explanation and feasible recourse, a requirement supported by work on counterfactual explanations and multi-layered governance-facing explanations in data protection law. (openresearch.surrey.ac.uk) The integration gap this paper targets is that these streams are often treated as parallel conversations: visibility as critique, fairness as metrics, documentation as best practice, and voice as a separate technical domain. A visibility-plus-audibility framework positions them as interlocking governance requirements that rise and fall together because they are produced by the same machinery.
Against this backdrop, this paper advances two linked contributions, one conceptual and one operational. Conceptually, it reframes AI governance as governance of algorithmic perception and institutional voice, specifying reciprocal visibility and reciprocal audibility as proportionality principles: if systems see and listen in ways that impose consequences, then affected parties and oversight bodies must have enforceable capacity to inspect what the system did, contest how it did it, and trigger correction when it fails. Operationally, it treats governance machinery as the primary unit of analysis: a repeatable sequence of inventory, controls, assessments, evidence integrity, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting that converts values into inspectable decisions. Methodologically, the paper implements this framework through comparative qualitative “governance reconstruction” across seven heterogeneous cases spanning labor gatekeeping, biometric data provenance, capital allocation, administrative due process, vendor-mediated risk scoring, internal conversational leakage, and customer-facing institutional speech. The cases include the Amazon recruiting tool, Clearview AI, Zillow Offers, the Dutch childcare benefits scandal (Toeslagenaffaire), COMPAS, Samsung’s ChatGPT-related leakage episode, and the Air Canada chatbot dispute, enabling cross-case pattern matching of where governance chains broke and how those breaks propagated into harm. (The Guardian) The paper proceeds by mapping the literature streams and their integration gap, specifying the conceptual terms and evaluative indices, reconstructing each case dossier to identify the earliest missing link in governance machinery, and then synthesizing design requirements for governance that preserve legitimacy as an evidence-backed asset rather than a performance claim.
Literature Review: From the Politics of Visibility to the Politics of Voice in AI Governance
The introduction frames AI governance as a politics of visibility in which legitimacy depends on whether institutions can render their decision-making systems inspectable, contestable, and correctable. The literature across surveillance studies, algorithmic accountability, fairness research, and speech technology substantiates this claim while also revealing a fragmentation: visibility, documentation, equity, and voice are often treated as adjacent but separate problems. Read together, however, these streams support a unified account in which governance is an evidentiary project oriented toward reciprocal visibility and reciprocal audibility. This review synthesizes these literatures to establish the conceptual and operational foundations for that integration.
Surveillance scholarship establishes that visibility is not a neutral condition of observation but a regime of power that structures who becomes legible and who remains shielded. Simone Browne, in Dark Matters: On the Surveillance of Blackness, argues that modern surveillance practices are genealogically continuous with racialized regimes of control that rendered Black bodies hypervisible while concealing institutional authority (Browne, 2015). Surveillance, in this account, is not merely data collection but a political arrangement in which exposure is unevenly distributed and historically patterned.
Nicholas Mirzoeff complements this analysis by framing visuality as authority-making and countervisuality as the demand for “the right to look” (Mirzoeff, 2011). Visuality organizes what can be seen, by whom, and under what interpretive frame; countervisuality insists that those subjected to regimes of perception retain a claim to inspect and contest them. When translated into AI governance, this right to look becomes a demand for inspectability proportionate to institutional power.
These theoretical contributions anchor the paper’s concept of asymmetric visibility: individuals are rendered measurable through data traces while decision mechanisms remain opaque. Algorithmic systems intensify this asymmetry by automating and scaling perception across domains such as labor recruitment, welfare administration, and criminal justice. Visibility becomes programmable, but institutional opacity often persists. The governance implication is direct: transparency cannot remain a rhetorical commitment; it must materialize as operational capacity for inspection, contestation, and remedy.
Building on these theoretical foundations, algorithmic accountability scholarship reframes governance as an evidentiary problem. Nicholas Diakopoulos defines algorithmic accountability as the capacity to make computational power investigable through structured observation and institutional oversight (Diakopoulos, 2015). Without reliable routes to observation, both public scrutiny and internal audit devolve into narrative conflict rather than evidence-based evaluation.
Similarly, Joshua A. Kroll and colleagues argue that automated decision systems implicate rule-of-law values, including due process and equal protection (Kroll et al., 2017). Technical performance metrics alone cannot secure these values. Institutions must establish procedural mechanisms that allow fairness and compliance claims to be verified rather than asserted. Accountability, therefore, is not a post hoc justification; it is a structural precondition for legitimate deployment.
Operationalizing this insight, Inioluwa Deborah Raji and colleagues propose an end-to-end internal auditing framework that treats documentation, evaluation, and monitoring as durable governance artifacts (Raji et al., 2020). Their framework spans problem formulation, data collection, model testing, and post-deployment oversight, emphasizing that governance must persist across the system lifecycle. In combination, these works support the paper’s claim that legitimacy behaves like an evidence-backed asset: it is accumulated through traceable decisions and depleted when documentation, controls, and monitoring are absent.
Documentation research provides the technical substrate for reciprocal visibility. Timnit Gebru and colleagues introduce datasheets for datasets, structured records detailing data composition, collection processes, intended uses, and limitations (Gebru et al., 2018). By formalizing metadata and context, datasheets transform datasets from opaque inputs into inspectable artifacts.
Margaret Mitchell and colleagues extend this logic to trained models through model cards, which report evaluation metrics, subgroup performance, and appropriate use contexts (Mitchell et al., 2019). These documentation practices convert high-level commitments to fairness into concrete disclosures that can travel across organizational turnover and regulatory review.
The significance of these artifacts lies not merely in their informational content but in their role as governance machinery. Documentation anchors accountability by creating a durable evidentiary trail. Without such records, contestation collapses into speculation. With them, institutions can reconstruct decisions, justify design choices, and demonstrate remediation efforts. Documentation thus becomes the operational form of the right to look back at institutional perception.
Empirical fairness research reveals how invisibilization emerges through training and measurement gaps. Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru demonstrate that commercial gender classification systems exhibit substantial error disparities across skin tone and gender, with the highest error rates for darker-skinned women (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018). These disparities trace directly to benchmark composition and evaluation scope, illustrating how statistical absence becomes repeatable misrecognition.
Legal scholarship deepens this structural account. Solon Barocas and Andrew D. Selbst show that data-driven systems can produce disparate impact through ostensibly neutral proxies, even absent discriminatory intent (Barocas & Selbst, 2016). Harm can emerge from correlations embedded in institutional pipelines rather than from explicit bias.
Further, Selbst and colleagues (2019) warn against abstraction traps that isolate models from the sociotechnical systems they reorganize. Fairness metrics applied at the model level may fail once systems are embedded in institutional contexts with complex feedback loops. Together, these works challenge the notion that fairness is a static property of a model. Instead, fairness is a governance outcome contingent on procurement choices, evaluation practices, and deployment monitoring.
This literature substantiates the paper’s concept of invisibilization as a governance failure rather than a technical anomaly. Groups become statistically absent when institutions neglect representation in data collection and testing; the resulting harms are difficult to litigate because they appear as error rather than intent. Governance responses must therefore include subgroup evaluation, continuous monitoring, and documented thresholds for acceptable disparities.
Accountability acquires practical meaning when affected individuals can contest and correct decisions. Sandra Wachter, Brent Mittelstadt, and Chris Russell propose counterfactual explanations that inform individuals what would need to change for a different outcome (Wachter et al., 2018). Such explanations shift transparency from abstract disclosure to actionable knowledge.
Berk Ustun and colleagues formalize actionable recourse, emphasizing feasibility: individuals must have realistic pathways to alter model decisions in high-stakes domains (Ustun et al., 2018). Explanation without feasible recourse risks converting procedural formality into substantive domination.
Within European data protection law, Margot E. Kaminski and Gianclaudio Malgieri argue for multi-layered algorithmic impact assessments that produce explanations tailored to individuals, regulators, and auditors (Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021). Contestability thus becomes an interlocking set of procedural rights and organizational capacities.
These contributions align with the reciprocal visibility principle advanced in the introduction. If systems classify and allocate, affected persons must receive notice, meaningful explanation, and correction pathways supported by institutional proof and remediation capacity.
While much governance literature privileges visual metaphors, speech technologies introduce a parallel domain of audibility. Allison Koenecke and colleagues document substantial racial disparities in word error rates across commercial automatic speech recognition systems (Koenecke et al., 2020). When deployed in hiring platforms, clinical documentation tools, or educational systems, transcription error can gate access to opportunity.
These findings extend invisibilization into the auditory domain. Speech communities underrepresented in training data experience higher misrecognition rates, transforming quality disparities into allocation mechanisms. The governance implication mirrors computer vision: subgroup evaluation, documentation, and monitoring must accompany deployment.
Conversational systems further complicate accountability because they generate institutional speech. When a chatbot communicates policy or eligibility criteria, its output may be treated as authoritative. The literature on institutional liability for automated communications underscores that conversational AI can bind organizations in disputes when outputs function as official statements. Voice systems thus implicate both listening (input capture) and speaking (output authority).
This duality supports the paper’s proposal of reciprocal audibility: if institutions deploy algorithmic listening and speaking at scale, affected parties must have enforceable routes to verify what was heard, how it was processed, and how outputs were generated and corrected. Logging, versioning, escalation protocols, and documented remediation timelines become necessary governance artifacts.
Across these literatures, a consistent pattern emerges. Surveillance theory identifies asymmetric regimes of perception. Accountability scholarship insists that investigability requires structured observation and documentation. Fairness research exposes invisibilization through measurement gaps. Contestability scholarship demands actionable explanation and recourse. Speech technology research reveals audibility disparities and institutional speech risks.
Yet these contributions are often treated as discrete domains: visibility as critique, fairness as metrics, documentation as best practice, and voice as technical performance. The integration gap lies in recognizing that these are interdependent components of a single governance machinery. Documentation enables inspection; inspection supports contestation; contestation disciplines fairness; fairness monitoring exposes invisibilization; voice systems require the same evidentiary infrastructure to prevent interface laundering.
A visibility-plus-audibility framework therefore positions governance as the operationalization of reciprocal inspection. Systems that see and listen at scale must be rendered inspectable in proportion to their power. Legitimacy, under this view, is not secured by compliance declarations or aggregate accuracy metrics. It is produced through durable evidence trails that allow third parties to reconstruct what happened, why it happened, and what changed after harm.
The literature reviewed here converges on a central claim: AI governance is best understood as an evidentiary practice that structures visibility and audibility. Surveillance studies reveal the historical asymmetries of perception; accountability scholarship operationalizes investigability; documentation research provides the artifacts that sustain inspection; fairness research demonstrates how invisibilization emerges from governance gaps; contestability scholarship establishes the rights necessary for meaningful recourse; and speech technology research extends these concerns into the auditory domain.
Together, these streams support the conceptual shift advanced in the introduction. Governance must move upstream of model performance and downstream of institutional choice. Reciprocal visibility and reciprocal audibility are not metaphors for openness; they are proportionality principles that translate power into inspectable obligation. Institutions that cannot evidence what their systems did, why they did it, and how they corrected errors risk depleting legitimacy as an operational asset. Conversely, organizations that embed documentation, monitoring, and recourse into their deployment pipelines transform governance from reputational posture into durable infrastructure.
Conceptual Framework
This paper reframes AI governance as governance of algorithmic perception and institutional voice, meaning that governance does not sit beside operations as commentary or compliance overhead, but instead produces the conditions under which people become legible, decisions become enforceable, and power becomes defensible or contestable. Read this way, the core object is neither “the model” nor “the policy” in isolation, but the institutional capacity to convert high-level commitments (fairness, safety, due process, accountability) into evidence-bearing practices that can survive pressure, scrutiny, and contestation. The framework therefore treats governance as a politics of visibility and audibility: it asks who becomes readable to institutions through data and automated perception, who becomes misrecognized or erased through measurement gaps, and who retains the capacity to inspect, challenge, and force justification when automated systems allocate opportunities and harms.
Two recurrent failures organize this politics. The first is asymmetric visibility: individuals become hypervisible through data traces while decision mechanisms remain opaque, difficult to inspect, and harder still to challenge or prove. The asymmetry is consequential because it stabilizes power at the organizational node that can see without being seen: the institution gains the ability to classify, score, and allocate at scale, while those classified struggle to reconstruct how outcomes were produced, which variables mattered, which thresholds were used, and where responsibility sits. The second is invisibilization: groups rendered statistically absent or distorted by training and measurement gaps become systemically misrecognized or excluded, with harms that are repeatable and difficult to litigate because they can arise without discriminatory intent. Together, these failures describe a structural double movement: some people are made intensely legible as governable objects, while others are made functionally unreadable as full participants in the institution’s category system.
The conceptual hinge that follows is a proportionality claim. Reciprocal visibility names the requirement that systems become inspectable in proportion to their power to classify and allocate: if systems see the world, affected publics must be able to see systems enough to secure accountability. The key point is operational rather than rhetorical. Reciprocal visibility is not a demand for full transparency or unrestricted access to model internals; it is a demand for sufficient inspectability to support notice, contestation, and correction. In the draft, reciprocal visibility is specified as three rights held by affected persons and three organizational capacities that institutions must maintain. The rights are (1) notice that AI meaningfully intervened in a consequential decision, (2) meaningful contestation via an accessible pathway that is actionable rather than merely formal, and (3) correction when error is demonstrated. The capacities are (1) proof, understood as the ability to reconstruct what happened and why through durable evidence trails, (2) explanation that is usable for action rather than only technical narration, and (3) rapid remediation, meaning the operational ability to fix systems and document what changed after failure. Reciprocal visibility therefore functions as a governance standard: it translates legitimacy from a claim into an evidence-backed asset.
The framework then extends the same proportionality logic to voice. In the draft, voice is treated in two coupled senses: voice as listening (how institutions capture speech, convert it into features, and operationalize it as risk, trust, or eligibility), and voice as institutional speech act (how AI systems speak on behalf of organizations in ways that can bind them in disputes, regulatory proceedings, and courts). These senses are analytically distinct yet operationally entangled because a single conversational interface can misrecognize inputs while also misrepresenting policy. That entanglement motivates reciprocal audibility as the sibling concept to reciprocal visibility: if institutions deploy algorithmic listening, affected people must have enforceable routes to verify what the system heard, how it converted speech into decision-relevant features, and how errors are corrected. Reciprocal audibility matters because conversational interfaces can convert error into silent allocation: a misheard sentence in a screening call, a clinical dictation error, or a customer-service transcript that never faithfully registers the complaint is not merely “quality” drift; it is governance drift, particularly when no durable evidentiary trace exists to make the drift contestable.
This is where governance machinery becomes the framework’s central sociotechnical concept. The draft defines governance machinery as a repeatable operational sequence whose links are empirically inspectable: inventory, controls, assessments, evidence, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting. The concept refuses two common reductions. It refuses a checklist reading where governance becomes box-ticking without causal leverage, and it refuses a principles-only reading where governance becomes value statements without enforceable infrastructure. Instead, governance machinery names the organizational equipment that turns rights (notice, contestation, correction) into capacities (proof, explanation, remediation) at the speed and scale of automated decisions. When that machinery is missing, the institution may still deploy systems, but it cannot credibly claim they are governed, because it cannot show what happened, why it happened, and what changed afterward in a way a third party could audit.
Interface laundering is the framework’s mechanism claim for conversational and voice-facing AI. The draft defines it as the process through which conversational interfaces shift consequential institutional decisions into informal interaction registers that appear transactional and low-stakes, thereby evading the evidentiary infrastructure accountability requires. The mechanism operates outward-facing when customer chatbots communicate policy without adequate policy-fidelity controls, escalation routes, version management, logging, and remediation timelines; it operates inward-facing when employees route sensitive work through external conversational tools because policy lags practice and sanctioned pathways are absent. In both directions, the mechanism is identical: governance opacity converts technical convenience into accountability liability by preventing reconstruction of what was said, by which version, under what prompts, and with what correction pathway. Importantly, the draft insists that interface laundering is testable, because it points to specific artifacts whose presence or absence can be verified: logs, versioning records, escalation documentation, and correction timelines.
Taken together, the framework supplies the paper’s comparative leverage. Asymmetric visibility and invisibilization describe the power problem; reciprocal visibility and reciprocal audibility specify enforceable counter-demands; governance machinery names the institutional form those demands must take; interface laundering isolates a voice-specific pathway through which governance can fail even when systems appear “friendly” and accessible. This architecture then disciplines the analysis across the seven cases by forcing every normative claim to “show up” as an artifact, a missing artifact, or an operational link that held or snapped under stress.
Methods and Materials
The study uses a comparative, qualitative multiple-case design organized as governance reconstruction, treating each incident as a trace of (i) algorithmic perception (visibility, voice, or hybrid), (ii) the coupling between model output and institutional action, and (iii) governance capacity under stress. The methodological wager is that public failures are rarely “first failures.” They are late-stage visibility events that render earlier governance breakdowns reconstructible. Governance reconstruction therefore treats incidents as diagnostic windows into the machinery that should have constrained deployment before harm, contained harm during drift, and produced proof after escalation.
Inference proceeds through two complementary strategies already specified in the draft: within-case process tracing and across-case pattern matching. Process tracing reconstructs the sequence connecting model outputs to institutional actions and downstream consequences, with the explicit goal of identifying the earliest break in governance machinery and tracking how that break propagated into public failure or harm. The aim is not to decide whether harm occurred, but to locate when preventability became structurally unavailable because the institution lacked inventory discipline, gatekeeping thresholds, monitoring, or a contestation pathway that could convert error into correction.
Pattern matching tests whether observed failures cluster around recurring missing components (for example absent gates, weak evidence integrity, limited supplier audit rights, fragile recourse design) and whether voice-mediated systems exhibit higher rates of interface laundering when evidence controls are not enforced. In construct-validity terms, the conceptual framework supplies expected patterns: if governance functions as evidentiary infrastructure, then breakdowns should be visible as missing artifacts and broken operational links rather than as isolated technical surprises. Confidence increases when cases match more uniquely identifying patterns (for example, “lack of logging plus lack of versioning plus institutional speech exposure”) rather than generic ones (“bias happened”).
The design is also theory-disciplined in the sense emphasized in research methods guidance: conceptual constructs and their propositions capture the “how” and “why” of relationships, while boundary conditions specify the “who, when, and where” under which those relationships should hold. Here, the key boundary condition is institutional consequence: the framework is applied to systems coupled to material allocation (jobs, welfare eligibility, liberty, capital deployment, consumer redress), where contestability and evidentiary durability are not optional goods but rule-of-law conditions.
Case selection follows purposeful, maximum-variation sampling across consequence domains and modalities. The goal is analytic breadth rather than statistical representativeness: the corpus is designed to include visibility-dominant systems, voice or conversational systems, and hybrid high-impact scoring regimes across contexts that apply different pressures to governance machinery. Seven cases are examined, and their selection is anchored to the framework’s interest in modality, coupling, and contestability.
The Amazon recruiting tool case (labor gatekeeping, visibility-dominant) is used to examine how a system trained on historical hiring patterns can reproduce gendered proxies and how the governance failure concentrates in absent thresholds, missing proxy review, undefined decision rights, and unmonitored output-to-action coupling. The Clearview AI case (biometric data provenance and multi-jurisdiction enforcement, visibility-dominant) examines supplier governance, data provenance, and the regulatory consequences of deploying biometric infrastructures without robust institutional accountability controls. The Zillow Offers case (capital allocation at scale, visibility-dominant) tests coupling: forecasting error becomes balance-sheet exposure when outputs bind high-stakes action at portfolio scale, raising the governance question of stress-testing, exposure controls, and stop thresholds.
The Dutch Toeslagenaffaire case (administrative due process failure, hybrid) anchors the analysis of recourse architecture at population scale, where risk scoring combines with fragile appeal and correction pathways to produce systemic harm. The COMPAS case (hybrid) foregrounds vendor opacity and contested fairness definitions, examining the conditions under which institutional justification becomes structurally impossible even when stakeholders agree that consequences are high and rights should be enforceable. Two voice-conversational cases operationalize interface laundering in both directions: Samsung and ChatGPT as inward-facing laundering, where policy lag plus absent sanctioned pathways produce audit voids and leakage risk; Air Canada chatbot as outward-facing laundering, where conversational outputs function as institutional speech and liability concentrates when versioning, logging, and remediation processes are absent or weak.
For each case, an evidence dossier is assembled from publicly available materials, organized to maximize reconstructibility rather than narrative coherence. The draft specifies dossier source prioritization as follows: investigative reporting and reconstructed timelines; regulatory findings and enforcement notices where applicable; court decisions and legally relevant outcomes where present; organizational statements and post-incident policy changes; and publicly available technical artifacts including model documentation, audit disclosures, and monitoring notes. No single source type is treated as authoritative; triangulation across types functions as the primary trustworthiness mechanism, particularly in domains where institutions have incentives to minimize disclosure after public failure.
A methodologically central commitment is that documentary absence is treated as data rather than as mere missingness. When dossiers lack audit artifacts, monitoring evidence, ownership records, contestation documentation, or versioning logs, that absence is coded as an empirically meaningful signal: accountability claims cannot be supported with inspectable evidence, which is itself a governance failure under the paper’s framework. This commitment aligns the study’s evidentiary standard with its normative thesis: if governance is an evidentiary project, then the inability to produce evidence becomes part of the finding rather than an inconvenience to be politely ignored.
The primary unit of analysis is the “governance break,” defined as the earliest identifiable point in the machinery chain where a missing link (inventory, controls, assessments, evidence integrity, gates, monitoring, audit-ready reporting) plausibly enabled downstream harm or prevented meaningful correction. Each case narrative therefore aims to produce a sequenced account that starts before the public incident, locating the quieter infrastructure failure that made the later scandal possible to reconstruct.
Operationalization proceeds via a structured coding scheme specified in the draft. Each case is coded along five dimensions: modality, coupling, asymmetry, recourse architecture, and governance machinery completeness. Modality codes whether the system operates primarily through visual or classificatory perception, through conversational or voice-facing interfaces, or through a hybrid combination, because modality shapes the evidentiary surface and the kinds of artifacts governance must reliably produce (for example classification records versus speech capture logs plus versioning plus escalation documentation). Coupling codes how tightly model output binds institutional action, ranging from advisory systems to determinative systems, and from low-scale individual decisions to high-scale deployments where errors compound across populations.
Asymmetry codes the extent to which people become measurable while mechanisms remain opaque, operationalized by assessing whether affected parties and oversight bodies have access to sufficient information to form a contestable account of system operation. Recourse architecture codes the presence and strength of the three rights emphasized in the conceptual framework: notice of AI intervention, meaningful contestation, and correction, assessed in practice rather than by policy declaration. Governance machinery completeness codes whether the organization can evidence the full operational chain from inventory through audit-ready reporting, and missing links are treated as primary findings enabling cross-case comparison.
To prevent analysis from drifting into abstract “principles talk” detached from observable evidence trails, the draft specifies two evaluative indices that function as audit heuristics rather than psychometric measures. The Reciprocal Visibility Index (RVI) assesses whether affected parties and oversight bodies can inspect decision pathways sufficiently to contest and correct outcomes, scoring both rights and organizational capacities while distinguishing between formal declarations and operational evidence. The Reciprocal Audibility Index (RAI) assesses whether conversational and voice-mediated systems generate durable evidence and enforceable accountability through logging, versioning, escalation, and remediation timelines, with explicit sensitivity to whether system outputs function as institutional speech. The RAI is designed to detect interface laundering as a patterned failure mode: systems that appear accessible in interaction while structurally preventing reconstruction of what was said, by which version, under what prompts, and with what correction pathway.
Both indices use a three-point rubric (0 = absent, 1 = partial, 2 = robust), applied to dossier evidence rather than organizational self-reporting, and scores are used as interpretive anchors for narrative reconstruction rather than as stand-alone quantitative outputs. In this sense, the indices discipline description by forcing a repeated question: what would a third party need to see to contest this decision pathway, and does the dossier show that the organization could provide it?
Analysis proceeds in two stages. First, within-case process tracing produces a governance reconstruction: a timeline of deployment, decision coupling, detection (or non-detection), escalation, and response, with explicit identification of the first missing machinery link and the downstream consequences that followed. Second, cross-case synthesis uses pattern matching to test whether failures cluster around recurring missing components (especially gates, evidence integrity, vendor audit rights, recourse design) and whether voice-mediated systems exhibit higher interface laundering risk when evidence controls are absent. The synthesis is comparative in a constrained sense: it does not claim that cases share identical technical architectures, but it claims that governance machinery can be compared across heterogeneous settings because it is a sequence of organizational functions that should be present whenever automated systems bind consequential decisions.
Trustworthiness is treated as endogenous to the theory of evidence: governance claims are only as strong as the traceable record supporting them, so the analysis uses triangulation across source types and treats conflicts among sources as analytically informative rather than as noise to be edited away. The draft also specifies negative-case logic as a corrective to scandal-selection bias, seeking incidents that did not escalate because governance machinery contained them, when such containment cases can be documented with sufficient detail to function as comparisons. Documentary absence as data further functions as a validity mechanism by preventing the analysis from mistaking the absence of counter-evidence for evidence of governance adequacy.
Two limitations follow from this design. First, reliance on public dossiers means visibility is itself uneven: some organizations disclose more by necessity or regulation, while others can suppress reconstruction through settlement, secrecy, or proprietary claims. This limitation is not merely methodological; it is part of the phenomenon the paper studies, because asymmetric visibility is one of the framework’s core diagnoses. Second, cross-case comparison risks flattening domain specificity. The design mitigates this by keeping coupling and consequence domain explicit, treating governance machinery as comparable only at the level of operational links (inventory, gating, monitoring, recourse) rather than at the level of technical implementation details.
Analysis
This study treats AI governance as a politics of visibility, meaning that organizational practices determine which decision pathways become legible, which harms remain structurally unobservable, and which affected parties retain practical capacity to challenge consequential outcomes. Rather than locating failure primarily in model error rates, the analytic focus is upstream of public scandal and downstream of institutional choice, where evidentiary infrastructures either enable or prevent noticing, contesting, and correcting harm before it escalates. The design therefore operationalizes governance as an evidentiary project. Legitimacy is treated as an evidence-backed asset that is produced or depleted by whether an organization can reconstruct what happened, why it happened, and what changed after harm.
The analysis proceeds from public case dossiers assembled through heterogeneous document types. The approach follows a core premise from field research traditions that documentary materials are neither neutral nor self-interpreting, and that their status as primary or secondary sources and as public or private artifacts matters for inference and credibility. Accordingly, dossier building treats court records, regulator findings, corporate statements, investigative reporting, and technical audits as perspectival traces, each carrying incentives and omissions that must be interpreted in context rather than taken as transparent accounts.
A central methodological commitment is that no single source type is treated as authoritative; trustworthiness is pursued through triangulation across types, especially because many institutions face incentives to limit disclosure after failure. This triangulation logic aligns with classic field research guidance that validity is strengthened when accounts of the same situation can be cross-checked across interviews, observations, and documents, not to locate a singular “truth,” but to stabilize what can be responsibly inferred and where disagreements are analytically meaningful.
Within this evidentiary stance, conflicts among sources are treated as data. Divergent narratives are read as signals of contested accountability, organizational boundary work, or missing governance artifacts, rather than as noise to be smoothed away. In parallel, documentary absence is explicitly treated as data rather than mere missingness. When dossiers lack audit artifacts, monitoring evidence, ownership records, contestation documentation, or versioning logs, that absence is coded as an empirically meaningful signal that accountability claims cannot be supported with inspectable evidence. This decision is consistent with documentary method cautions that the presence or absence of particular materials may itself be significant, raising questions about availability, access, revisions, and representativeness.
The study implements comparative qualitative “governance reconstruction” across seven heterogeneous cases spanning labor gatekeeping, biometric data provenance, capital allocation, administrative due process, vendor-mediated risk scoring, internal conversational leakage, and customer-facing institutional speech. The cases are the Amazon recruiting tool, Clearview AI, Zillow Offers, the Dutch childcare benefits scandal (Toeslagenaffaire), COMPAS, Samsung’s ChatGPT-related leakage episode, and the Air Canada chatbot dispute.
The comparative claim is deliberately constrained. The analysis does not assume shared technical architectures. Instead, it assumes that governance machinery can be compared across heterogeneous settings because it is composed of a sequence of organizational functions that should be present whenever automated systems bind consequential decisions. This mirrors a field-research logic in which comparison does not require identical sites, but does require comparable analytic objects and consistent criteria for tracing processes across cases.
The primary unit of analysis is the governance break, defined as the earliest identifiable point in the machinery chain where a missing link plausibly enabled downstream harm or prevented meaningful correction. Governance machinery is specified as a repeatable operational sequence: inventory, controls, assessments, evidence integrity, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting. Each case narrative is reconstructed as a sequenced account that starts before the public incident, locating the quieter infrastructure failure that made later escalation possible to reconstruct.
Operationally, this approach resembles process tracing adapted to documentary environments. It treats deployments and organizational responses as events that can be placed in temporal order and linked through plausible mechanisms. The reconstruction asks a repeated question: at what point did the organization lose the capacity to produce an inspectable account of decision pathways, and what downstream consequences followed from that evidentiary loss. This emphasis draws on qualitative analysis traditions that build propositions from systematic comparison across incidents, while recognizing that causal inference is provisional and must remain faithful to available evidence.
Each case is coded along five dimensions: modality, coupling, asymmetry, recourse architecture, and governance machinery completeness. These dimensions translate the paper’s conceptual framework into observable dossier features.
Modality codes whether the system operates primarily through visual or classificatory perception, through conversational or voice-facing interfaces, or through a hybrid combination, because modality shapes what evidence must exist for accountability to be possible (for example, classification records versus durable speech capture logs with versioning and escalation documentation).
Coupling codes how tightly model output binds institutional action, spanning advisory systems to determinative systems, and low-scale to high-scale deployments where errors compound across populations. Coupling matters because tightly bound systems require stronger gates, monitoring, and recourse pathways to prevent automated inference from hardening into irreversible institutional action.
Asymmetry codes the extent to which people become measurable while mechanisms remain opaque, operationalized by whether affected parties and oversight bodies have sufficient access to form a contestable account of system operation. This coding implements the politics-of-visibility thesis at the level of dossier evidence: asymmetry is not a philosophical claim but a measurable deficit in contestable artifacts.
Recourse architecture codes the presence and strength of three rights emphasized by the conceptual framework: notice of AI intervention, meaningful contestation, and correction, assessed in practice rather than by policy declaration.
Governance machinery completeness codes whether the organization can evidence the full operational chain from inventory through audit-ready reporting; missing links are treated as primary findings enabling cross-case comparison.
To discipline interpretation and reduce drift into abstract “principles talk,” the analysis applies two evaluative indices as audit heuristics rather than psychometric measures. The Reciprocal Visibility Index (RVI) assesses whether affected parties and oversight bodies can inspect decision pathways sufficiently to contest and correct outcomes, distinguishing between formal declarations and operational evidence. The Reciprocal Audibility Index (RAI) assesses whether conversational and voice-mediated systems generate durable evidence and enforceable accountability through logging, versioning, escalation, and remediation timelines, with sensitivity to the fact that system outputs may function as institutional speech.
Both indices use a three-point rubric (0 absent, 1 partial, 2 robust), applied to dossier evidence rather than organizational self-reporting, and are used as interpretive anchors rather than stand-alone quantitative outputs. The indices enforce a repeated evidentiary demand: what would a third party need to see to contest this decision pathway, and does the dossier show the organization could provide it.
The RAI is specifically designed to detect interface laundering: systems that appear accessible in interaction while structurally preventing reconstruction of what was said, by which version, under what prompts, and with what correction pathway. This aligns with the paper’s claim that conversational AI can shift consequential communications into an informal register that evades accountability unless governance forces outputs back into durable logs, versioning, escalation, and correction timelines.
Analysis proceeds in two stages. First, within-case process tracing produces a governance reconstruction: a timeline of deployment, decision coupling, detection (or non-detection), escalation, and response, with explicit identification of the first missing machinery link and its downstream consequences. Second, cross-case synthesis uses pattern matching to test whether failures cluster around recurring missing components, especially gates, evidence integrity, vendor audit rights, and recourse design, and whether voice-mediated systems show higher interface laundering risk when evidence controls are absent. The synthesis is comparative in a constrained sense: it compares governance functions across heterogeneous domains without collapsing technical differences into false equivalences.
Trustworthiness is treated as endogenous to the evidentiary theory. Governance claims are only as strong as the traceable record supporting them, so triangulation is used and documentary absence is treated as a validity mechanism that prevents mistaking the absence of counter-evidence for evidence of adequacy. This approach also draws support from field research discussions of category construction and constant comparison, where coding and iterative comparison across incidents generate properties of categories and delimit theory through saturation rather than exhaustive enumeration.
Two limitations follow directly from the design. First, reliance on public dossiers means visibility is uneven: some organizations disclose more due to regulation or litigation, while others suppress reconstruction through settlement, secrecy, or proprietary claims. Second, cross-case comparison risks flattening domain specificity; the study mitigates this by keeping coupling and consequence domain explicit and treating machinery as comparable only at the level of operational links, not implementation details.
Results
Across the seven cases, governance breaks concentrate at points where organizations fail to convert consequential automation into durable, inspectable artifacts. The cross-case synthesis indicates recurring deficits in (1) gating, where systems move from experimentation to consequential deployment without enforceable sign-off; (2) evidence integrity, where records are incomplete, non-versioned, or non-reconstructable; (3) vendor boundary controls, where audit rights and documentation access are constrained; and (4) recourse architecture, where notice, contestation, and correction remain aspirational rather than operational.
This clustering supports the paper’s central claim that governance machinery is the primary analytic object. When machinery links are missing, downstream harms become difficult to detect early and difficult to remedy later because accountability collapses into narrative conflict rather than evidence-backed reconstruction.
Cases involving voice-facing or conversational interfaces show a distinct failure mode: interface laundering. Even when end users experience an interaction as an institutional commitment, the evidentiary substrate may be too thin to reconstruct what was said, by which system version, under what prompts, and with what escalation path. The RAI is sensitive to this mechanism by scoring the presence of durable logging, versioning, escalation procedures, and remediation timelines.
In the Air Canada chatbot dispute and Samsung’s internal conversational leakage episode, dossier evidence plausibly points to audibility gaps as governance breaks. In the customer-facing case, the interface operates as institutional speech while accountability depends on whether organizations can preserve the speech event as an auditable record. In the internal case, “shadow AI” dynamics create audit voids when employees route sensitive work through external tools absent enforceable controls, logging, and escalation pathways.
High coupling appears as an amplifier. Where model outputs bind institutional action tightly and at scale, even moderate documentation gaps become structurally consequential. In the reconstructed cases spanning welfare administration and vendor-mediated criminal justice scoring, the combination of high coupling and weak recourse architecture yields low reciprocal visibility, meaning that affected persons cannot assemble contestable accounts of decisions and oversight bodies cannot reliably inspect decision pathways.

When governance machinery can produce an auditable chain from inventory to monitoring and audit-ready reporting, organizations retain the capacity to explain, contest internally, correct, and demonstrate remediation. When machinery links are missing, the organization’s ability to account for consequential automation collapses, and the resulting deficit is itself a governance failure that precedes public scandal.
Discussion
The seven-case synthesis reframes what counts as an “AI incident” by relocating failure upstream of public scandal and into the institutional infrastructure that makes systems contestable, correctable, and therefore governable. Read this way, the core finding is less that particular models erred, and more that organizations repeatedly lacked the evidentiary machinery required to notice and contain error before it hardened into consequential allocation. The framework terms this problem a politics of visibility and audibility: institutions scale perception and speech through automated systems while failing to scale reciprocal inspection, thereby concentrating power at the organizational node that can see and speak without being equally seeable or answerable.
A first interpretive payoff of the analysis is that it makes “governance” empirically legible as a sequence of operational links rather than a bundle of aspirations. The draft’s definition of governance machinery is explicit: inventory, controls, assessments, evidence, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting, treated as inspectable links rather than policy language or box-ticking. That definition matters because it refuses two reductions that repeatedly surface in responsible technology discourse: governance as checklist compliance and governance as principles-only rhetoric. The cases show why both reductions fail. When inventory is missing, the organization cannot even name what is deployed; when gates are unenforced, experimentation slides into high-coupling deployment; when evidence integrity is absent, accountability collapses into narrative conflict; when monitoring is thin, drift becomes harm before it becomes measurable.
This is also where the paper’s comparative method becomes more than rhetorical. The analysis claims that heterogeneous cases can be compared because what repeats is not architecture but organizational function: consequential systems require the same governance sequence even when their technical substrates differ. Put differently, “governance reconstruction” works because it treats each incident as a trace of which link broke first, how the break propagated, and what downstream consequences followed. The comparative convergence around gating, evidence integrity, vendor boundary limits, and recourse design therefore supports a stronger claim than “organizations should do better.” It supports the narrower, operational claim that incidents reliably cluster at evidentiary choke points.
The political implication is that legitimacy becomes infrastructural. The draft names legitimacy as an evidence-backed asset: it is produced or depleted by whether an organization can reconstruct what happened, why it happened, and what changed after harm in a form that a third party could audit. This moves the discussion away from reputational “trust” and toward institutional capacity under scrutiny, which is precisely the condition responsible technology journals increasingly require research to interrogate.
A second interpretive contribution is that reciprocal visibility translates a normative demand into a proportionality standard. The draft defines reciprocal visibility as the requirement that systems become inspectable in proportion to their power to classify and allocate, with a deliberately operational emphasis: the demand is not total transparency, but sufficient inspectability to support notice, meaningful contestation, and correction. Crucially, the concept binds those rights to organizational capacities: proof (reconstructability through durable evidence trails), usable explanation (actionable rather than merely technical narration), and rapid remediation (operational ability to fix systems and document what changed). The result is a governance standard that can be inspected for artifacts rather than inferred from institutional tone.
The cross-case results align with that proportionality logic through the coupling dimension. In high-coupling domains, small evidentiary deficits become structurally consequential because outputs bind action at scale. The summary anchors reported in the analysis make this visible: reciprocal visibility is most fragile where coupling is high and recourse is thin, because affected parties lack notice and contestation pathways that are backed by inspectable artifacts. The point is not that low-coupling systems are “safe,” but that the harm gradient steepens when the organization both (i) binds model outputs to institutional action and (ii) fails to provide the documentary substrate through which affected parties can contest and obtain correction.
This proportionality framing also clarifies why invisibilization is not merely a fairness metric problem but a governance problem. The draft defines invisibilization as the condition in which groups rendered statistically absent or distorted by training and measurement gaps become systematically misrecognized or excluded, with harms that are repeatable and difficult to litigate precisely because they can arise without discriminatory intent. In that sense, invisibilization is a diagnostic of missing machinery: if subgroup evaluation, documentation, and monitoring are not forced into the deployment pipeline as gates, then disparity is not “discovered,” it is produced and stabilized.
A third contribution is the extension of governance from visibility to voice. The draft treats voice in two coupled senses: voice as listening (how institutions capture speech and convert it into decision-relevant features) and voice as institutional speech act (how systems speak on behalf of organizations in ways that can bind them in disputes and courts). These senses are analytically distinct yet operationally entangled, because a single conversational interface can misrecognize inputs while also misrepresenting policy. That entanglement motivates reciprocal audibility as the sibling to reciprocal visibility: if institutions deploy algorithmic listening, affected people must have enforceable routes to verify what the system heard, how it converted speech into decision-relevant features, and how errors are corrected.
The results suggest that conversational systems invite a distinctive governance failure: interface laundering. The draft defines interface laundering as the process through which conversational interfaces shift consequential institutional decisions into informal interaction registers that appear transactional and low-stakes, thereby evading the evidentiary infrastructure accountability requires. The definition is deliberately testable. It points to specific artifacts whose presence or absence can be verified: logs, versioning records, escalation documentation, and correction timelines. In the outward-facing case, the governance risk is that institutional speech is produced without institutional recordkeeping. In the inward-facing case, the risk is that employees route sensitive work through tools that create audit voids because sanctioned pathways, controls, and escalation protocols are absent or misaligned with practice.
This mechanism claim helps interpret why the Reciprocal Audibility Index scores were lowest in the two conversational cases in the summary table. The anchors underscore a simple point with uncomfortable consequences: the evidence required for accountability is not automatically produced by conversational interfaces unless logging, versioning, and escalation are treated as governance requirements rather than engineering conveniences. In this sense, “voice governance” is not a niche extension to an otherwise visual paradigm. It is an exposure of a broader governance habit: institutions often treat interface output as low-stakes communication until a court, regulator, or internal incident forces recognition that the output functioned as consequential institutional action.
The methodological stance of treating governance as an evidentiary project also sharpens what counts as trustworthiness in responsible technology research. The dossier approach assumes that documentary materials are perspectival traces rather than transparent accounts, and it treats conflicts among sources as analytically informative rather than editorial noise. The first draft formalizes this as a validity strategy: triangulation across source types reduces dependence on any single narrative, while negative-case logic seeks incidents that did not escalate because governance machinery contained them, countering scandal-selection bias. It also treats documentary absence as data, preventing the analysis from mistaking the absence of counter-evidence for evidence of governance adequacy.
This stance is consistent with classic cautions in field research about documentary evidence and presentation. Burgess emphasizes that documentary evidence must be interpreted in context and that presentation choices force the reader to trust the author unless methods and illustrative strategies are combined, which is precisely why the analysis foregrounds its coding scheme and interpretive indices rather than relying on case narration alone. The same caution also structures the paper’s limitations. The draft names two limits that are also part of the phenomenon: reliance on public dossiers means visibility is uneven (some organizations disclose under pressure while others suppress reconstruction through secrecy or proprietary claims), and cross-case comparison risks flattening domain specificity. These are not merely methodological inconveniences. They instantiate the politics-of-visibility diagnosis: when institutions can choose what becomes reconstructible, they can also choose what becomes governable.
A further implication concerns category construction. Although this study does not conduct ethnographic interviews, its analytic logic parallels ethnographic analysis insofar as it builds meaning through relationships among categories, mechanisms, and artifacts. Spradley characterizes analysis as dividing a phenomenon into constituent parts and identifying relationships among parts and their relationship to the whole, while warning against imposing external categories that “create order” rather than discover it. The present framework responds to that warning by forcing concepts to “show up” as artifacts: reciprocal visibility and audibility are treated as capacities evidenced through logs, gates, contestation pathways, and remediation traces, not as labels retrofitted to cases.
The practical contribution follows directly from the machinery framing: responsible AI governance is best understood as building and maintaining the conditions of reconstructability. The paper’s first draft makes this translation explicit by listing auditable controls that operationalize reciprocal visibility: lifecycle traceability (“system life sheet”), inspectable transparency (documentation, logs, tests, evidence), monitored equity, actionable contestability for high-impact decisions, supplier governance that prevents black-box risk transfer, and incident readiness (detection thresholds, playbooks, remediation). Read through the cross-case patterns, these are not generic recommendations. They are the repeatedly missing artifacts that incidents force into view once harm becomes public.
Voice-facing systems require an even stricter interpretation of “inspectable transparency,” because the interaction itself becomes the decision trace. Here, reciprocal audibility implies that conversational deployments should be governed as institutional communications systems, with enforceable logging, version control, escalation, and correction timelines as standard artifacts rather than post-incident improvisations. The audibility claim therefore expands the scope of responsible AI governance beyond models and datasets: it reaches interface design, customer service policy, legal risk management, and internal information governance, because these domains determine whether what was said can be proven, contested, and corrected.
The study also opens a clear agenda for future research that would deepen, rather than replace, dossier-based governance reconstruction. First, ethnographic and interview-based work inside deploying organizations could map how governance machinery is negotiated in practice: where gates are bypassed under deadline pressure, how ownership is diffused across teams, and which artifacts are produced for auditors versus for operational learning. This would align with Spradley’s iterative view that analysis and hypothesis formation recur throughout the research process, with writing itself functioning as refined analysis that identifies gaps for further data collection. Second, systematic comparison between scandal cases and containment cases could specify which machinery links are most predictive of early detection and rapid remediation, extending the draft’s negative-case logic into a stronger comparative baseline. Third, a focused empirical program on interface laundering could operationalize audibility requirements as measurable governance controls across sectors, enabling more robust assessment of whether conversational systems are being treated as institutional speech infrastructures.
Conclusion
This paper’s analysis and results converge on a single argument with operational teeth: AI governance is a politics of visibility and audibility, and legitimacy under consequential automation behaves like an evidence-backed asset. Reciprocal visibility specifies the proportionality demand that follows from that politics: if systems classify and allocate, affected parties must receive notice, meaningful contestation, and correction, while institutions must maintain the capacities for proof, usable explanation, and rapid remediation through durable evidence trails. Reciprocal audibility extends the same proportionality logic to voice, treating conversational AI as both listening infrastructure and institutional speech act, and requiring enforceable routes to verify what was heard, how it was processed, and how errors were corrected.
The comparative contribution is that governance machinery becomes the unit of analysis that makes these demands inspectable. By treating inventory, gates, evidence integrity, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting as empirically checkable links, governance reconstruction shows that high-profile failures cluster at predictable evidentiary choke points rather than emerging as random technical surprises. The distinctive warning for responsible technology practice is that conversational interfaces intensify risk through interface laundering unless logs, versioning, escalation protocols, and correction timelines are governed as mandatory accountability artifacts.
The paper therefore closes with a disciplined claim: organizations do not become responsible by declaring principles or reporting aggregate performance. They become responsible when they can reconstruct what happened and why, when affected parties can contest with evidence rather than pleading, and when remediation is documented as a change in machinery rather than promised as future intent.

Abstract (≤250 words)
AI governance is often treated as a technical program of performance, safety, and compliance. We argue instead that it is a politics of algorithmic visibility that determines who becomes legible, who is erased, and who can contest automated outcomes. Drawing on critical accounts of the gaze (hooks; Fanon) and panopticism (Foucault), we conceptualize AI deployment as a scalable regime of perception that classifies, prioritizes, recommends, and excludes. Two governance failures recur: (1) asymmetric visibility, where individuals become hypervisible through data traces while decision mechanisms remain opaque; and (2) invisibilization, where non-diverse training data makes some groups “unseen,” turning statistical gaps into practical discrimination (e.g., facial analysis).
We propose a framework of reciprocal visibility: if systems “see” the world, affected publics must be able to see systems sufficiently to secure accountability. Reciprocal visibility is specified as three rights (notice of AI intervention, meaningful contestation, and correction) and three organizational capacities (proof, explanation, rapid remediation). We translate these principles into an auditable standard with six controls: lifecycle traceability (“system life sheet”); inspectable transparency (documentation, logs, tests, evidence); verifiable, monitored equity; actionable contestability for high-impact decisions; supplier governance that prevents black-box risk transfer; and incident readiness (detection, stop thresholds, playbooks, remediation). We conclude that legitimacy—not performance alone—must be treated as a durable asset grounded in evidence.

Conceptual contribution
This paper reframes AI governance as governance of algorithmic perception plus governance of institutional voice. The starting claim is simple: AI governance does not “sit beside” operations. AI governance produces the conditions under which people become legible, decisions become enforceable, and power becomes defensible. That logic appears explicitly in the manifesto framing: “AI governance is not a technical project… it is a politics of visibility,” and it allocates who becomes readable, who disappears, and who can contest.
1) From reciprocal visibility to reciprocal audibility
The paper’s first contribution is a proportionality principle. If systems “see” the world, “the world must be able to see systems,” not fully and not naively, but enough to secure rights and produce proof. That is formalized as reciprocal visibility, specified through three rights and three organizational capacities.
The second move extends that proportionality logic to voice, understood in two coupled senses:
Voice as listening: how institutions capture speech, convert it into features, and operationalize it as risk, trust, or eligibility.
Voice as speech act: how AI systems speak on behalf of organizations, turning generated outputs into “institutional speech” that can bind the institution in disputes.
The Air Canada chatbot case is the clearest “institutional speech” example. The text states that customer-facing AI concentrates accountability because policy communication becomes institutional speech “in the eyes of customers, regulators, and courts,” and it highlights the evidentiary reality of screenshots. This supports the paper’s claim that governance must treat voice interfaces as a legal and reputational surface, not merely a UX surface.
2) Governance failure is rarely “the model”; it is the missing machinery
Across your corpus, a consistent analytic pattern appears: high-profile AI failures typically follow earlier, quieter failures in governance structure. The seven-case synthesis makes that explicit by arguing that organizations treat governance as commentary rather than as operating machinery, then collapse when scrutiny demands reconstruction.
This yields a paper-level conceptual object: governance machinery as a repeatable operational sequence that turns values into inspectable decisions. Your text already names the sequence:
Inventory → controls → assessments → evidence → gates → monitoring → audit-ready reporting.
The paper contributes by treating that sequence as a sociotechnical theory of accountability, not just a consulting checklist. Each step becomes an empirical site where power either stays asymmetrical or becomes contestable.
3) “Interface laundering” as a voice-adjacent mechanism
A third contribution is a mechanism claim: conversational interfaces tend to launder consequential institutional decisions into informal interaction unless governance forces them back into the evidentiary domain.
Air Canada illustrates the outward-facing version: the organization deployed a policy channel without adequate controls for policy fidelity, escalation, and correction, then became accountable because the bot’s outputs functioned as official statements.
Samsung illustrates the inward-facing version: employees routed sensitive work through an external tool because policy lagged behavior, and governance failed to provide a sanctioned pathway plus discovery signals. This case shows how “voice” (here: conversational use of GenAI) can evade auditability unless inputs, boundaries, and reporting channels become operational controls.
4) A unifying definition of “defensibility” grounded in evidence
The manifesto’s refusal of asymmetry anchors the paper’s normative spine: the citizen becomes hypervisible while systems remain opaque, and that asymmetry functions as an “architecture of power.” The paper’s contribution is to convert that into evaluable criteria: a system is governed if the organization can prove what happened, explain why, and remediate fast, in ways that preserve rights to notice, contestation, and correction.

1) Theoretical framework: AI governance as power over legibility
This project treats AI governance as a politics of algorithmic visibility—a regime that organizes who becomes legible, who is erased, and who can contest automated outcomes. Building from critical theories of the gaze and institutional perception (panopticism; racialized/colonial visibility), the framework reads AI systems as scalable infrastructures of perception that classify, prioritize, recommend, and exclude, while often sheltering their own mechanisms behind organizational opacity.
Core problem configuration
Two recurrent governance failures anchor the theory:
Asymmetric visibility: people become hypervisible through data traces, while decision mechanisms remain opaque (hard to inspect, challenge, or prove).
Invisibilization: groups made “unseen” by training/measurement gaps become systematically misrecognized or excluded—turning statistical absence into practical discrimination.
Normative-analytic hinge: reciprocal visibility (and audibility)
The framework proposes reciprocal visibility as a proportionality principle: if systems “see” the world, affected publics must be able to “see” systems enough to secure accountability. This is specified as:
Rights: (1) notice of AI intervention, (2) meaningful contestation, (3) correction.
Organizational capacities: (1) proof, (2) explanation, (3) rapid remediation.
It then extends the same logic to reciprocal audibility for conversational/voice-facing AI: when institutions deploy algorithmic “listening” or AI “speaking” as institutional speech, governance must ensure durable evidence (logs/versioning), escalation, and correction pathways—so the organization can be held to account for what the system “said” or “heard.”

A key mechanism claim is that conversational interfaces can launder consequential decisions into informal interaction unless governance forces them back into the evidentiary domain (policy fidelity, escalation, version control, documented remediation).

this paper’s theoretical framework and methodology are integrated around a single question: how do AI systems reorganize what can be seen, said, and proven in institutional decision-making, and what would it take for those same decisions to become contestable again through evidence, procedure, and enforceable remedy? The theoretical starting point treats visibility as a form of power rather than a neutral property of information systems. Work on visuality and the “right to look” frames visibility as a political relation that structures who is authorized to observe and who is rendered observable (Mirzoeff, 2011), while scholarship on surveillance and racialized monitoring shows how “being seen” is historically uneven, often operating as a mechanism of control rather than protection (Browne, 2015). Read alongside critiques of contemporary data capture and extraction (Zuboff, 2019) and attention economies (Odell, 2019), these accounts situate AI systems as infrastructures that scale perception—classifying, ranking, and filtering—while simultaneously making the institutional grounds of those classifications difficult to interrogate.
From that point, the paper aligns visibility with accountability. Algorithmic accountability research argues that the public relevance of algorithmic systems stems not only from accuracy but from their role in consequential social ordering, which requires mechanisms for scrutiny, explanation, and justification (Diakopoulos, 2015). Yet the paper also takes seriously the warning that governance can fail by “abstracting away” the social context in which a model operates, producing formal fairness claims that do not track lived harms (Selbst et al., 2019). Disparate impact scholarship clarifies why this risk is structural: even when sensitive attributes are not explicitly used, data-driven decision systems can reproduce inequality through proxies and institutional pathways (Barocas & Selbst, 2016). Empirically, the same logic appears in documented performance disparities in commercial classification systems (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018), which this paper treats not as isolated “bias incidents” but as signals of governance failure in documentation, evaluation scope, and accountability pathways. For that reason, technical transparency is defined here as actionable transparency, anchored in documentation practices that make system intent, training context, evaluation limits, and appropriate use legible to oversight. Datasheets for datasets (Gebru et al., 2018) and model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) provide the paper’s baseline vocabulary for what documentation must accomplish if claims about model suitability are to be auditable rather than aspirational.
This paper then extends the visibility problem to voice. Two literatures motivate the move. First, scholarship on timbre and vocality emphasizes that voice is not a neutral channel but a culturally mediated field of perception, where listening practices and identity cues are socially organized (Eidsheim, 2019). Second, speech technology research shows that automated speech recognition can perform unevenly across demographic groups, which is consequential precisely because ASR converts speech into decision-relevant features and institutional records (Koenecke et al., 2020). A further layer is introduced by synthetic audio: when voice can be convincingly manufactured, voice-based workflows face an evidentiary threat, because historically stable cues for authorization and intent become less reliable. The paper treats this as a governance problem of provenance and disclosure, informed by work on audio deepfake detection as an evolving security and trust challenge (Zhang et al., 2025), and by transparency-oriented regulatory approaches such as the EU AI Act’s Article 50 obligations concerning disclosure in relevant AI interactions and contexts involving synthetic or manipulated content (EU AI Act, Art. 50). UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021) and AI Now’s emphasis on AI’s institutional and democratic implications (AI Now Institute, 2023) are used here as normative anchors for why contestability, documentation, and accountability should be treated as governance requirements rather than optional best practices.
With these foundations in place, the paper’s central theoretical move is to treat “governance machinery” as the sociotechnical form through which legitimacy is produced. Rather than presenting governance as a checklist, the draft specifies a repeatable chain—inventory, controls, assessments, evidence, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting—and treats each link as an empirical site where institutional power either remains asymmetrical or becomes contestable. This yields a concrete definition of defensibility: a system is governed when an organization can produce evidence of what occurred, justify why it occurred, and remediate rapidly, in ways that preserve enforceable rights to notice, contestation, and correction. The same framework motivates a mechanism claim tailored to conversational systems: “interface laundering” describes how conversational interfaces can shift consequential institutional decisions into informal interaction unless governance forces outputs back into the evidentiary domain through policy fidelity controls, escalation routes, logging, ownership, and documented correction processes.
Methodologically, these theoretical commitments imply a comparative qualitative case study design, because the object of explanation is not a single model but a sequence of coupled sociotechnical events that connect model outputs to institutional actions and downstream harms. Case study research is well-suited to this task insofar as it brings together multiple articles of evidence to illuminate an empirical case as a whole, and it benefits from an explicit theoretical framework established prior to data collection. Accordingly, the paper adopts a multiple-case design organized around structured “governance reconstruction,” treating each incident as a trace of algorithmic perception, institutional decision coupling, and governance capacity under stress. Inference is built through process tracing within cases to locate where governance machinery broke or never existed, paired with pattern matching across cases against the paper’s propositions, including asymmetry, voice disparity, interface laundering, provenance instability, and recourse effectiveness. Pattern matching is used in the construct-validity sense articulated in the research methods text: theoretical patterns function as hypotheses about what should be observed in the data, and validity strengthens when more complex, uniquely identifying patterns are matched rather than simplistic ones.
Case selection follows purposeful sampling across consequence domains and modalities, enabling comparison between visibility-dominant systems, voice or conversational systems, and hybrid high-impact scoring regimes. The draft’s corpus includes cases framed as labor gatekeeping (Amazon recruiting), data provenance and enforcement (Clearview AI), capital allocation at scale (Zillow Offers), administrative due process failures (the Dutch Toeslagenaffaire), contested fairness under vendor opacity (COMPAS), internal “shadow AI” leakage (Samsung and ChatGPT), and institutional speech and liability in a customer-facing conversational system (Air Canada’s chatbot). Importantly, these cases are not treated as self-evident “scandals”; they function as empirical settings in which the governance chain can be reconstructed and compared under different institutional pressures.
Evidence collection is organized through a dossier approach that prioritizes public investigative reporting and timelines, regulatory findings where applicable, court decisions or legally relevant outcomes where present, organizational statements and post-incident policy changes, and any technical artifacts that are publicly available such as model documentation, audit disclosures, or monitoring notes. The methodological rationale is explicit: the absence of artifacts is itself a governance finding, because it indicates that accountability claims cannot be supported with inspectable evidence. Standards and governance frameworks are treated as interpretive resources for what “good evidence” plausibly looks like in organizational settings, drawing particularly on the AI risk management framing in NIST’s AI RMF 1.0 (NIST, 2023) and the management-system approach in ISO/IEC 42001 (ISO/IEC, 2023). Legal and procedural approaches to explanation and contestability—such as counterfactual explanation proposals (Wachter, Mittelstadt, & Russell, 2018) and actionable recourse concepts (Ustun, Spangher, & Liu, 2018), as well as algorithmic impact assessment discussions (Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021)—are used to discipline how “contestability” is operationalized, without assuming that any single explanation technique is sufficient on its own.
Analysis is structured through a coding scheme that keeps theoretical meaning intact while remaining auditable. Each case is coded by modality (visibility, voice, or hybrid), coupling (how tightly outputs couple to action), asymmetry (the extent to which people become measurable while mechanisms remain opaque), recourse architecture (the strength of contestation, appeals, and correction pathways), and governance machinery completeness (whether the inventory-to-audit-ready chain can be evidenced). The paper then operationalizes its normative commitments through two indices. A reciprocal visibility index scores whether affected parties and oversight bodies could inspect decision pathways sufficiently to contest and correct outcomes, while a reciprocal audibility index scores whether conversational or voice channels produce durable evidence through logging, versioning, escalation, and remediation timelines—especially when system outputs function as institutional speech. Cross-case synthesis proceeds in two connected steps: within-case tracing identifies the first governance break and how it propagated into public failure, and across-case pattern matching assesses whether failures cluster around recurring missing components such as gates, evidence integrity, vendor audit rights, or recourse design.
Finally, trustworthiness is treated as consistent with the paper’s theory of evidence: claims about governance are only as strong as the traceability of the record supporting them. The draft therefore commits to triangulation across press accounts and regulatory or court-relevant documents where available, to negative-case analysis that actively searches for incidents contained by governance rather than only those that escalated, and to treating missing audit artifacts, missing monitoring evidence, and unclear decision rights as empirically meaningful signals of non-governance.
