Governance by Denial: How Legibility, Capacity, and Classification Rewire Accountability
Abstract
Democratic hollowing often proceeds through legality and tempo rather than rupture. Systems can retain elections, courts, oversight bodies, and constitutional language while power over what counts as credible, governable, and sanctionable is reorganized upstream. This article conceptualizes hollowing as a transformation in the governance of public reality and specifies a coupled mechanism family: narrative control, impunity signaling, and weak accountability loops. Narrative control governs intelligibility and adjudication by reshaping inference rules. Impunity signaling recalibrates expectations of enforcement through patterned nonconsequence and strategic silence. Weak accountability loops routinize procedural response while leaving incentive structures intact. The framework integrates queer, disability, and postcolonial analytics to treat intelligibility, capacity, and classification as measurable sites of governance. A theory-driven, process-tracing comparison of the United States, Canada, and Europe, with Israel–Gaza as a bounded stress test, shows how democratic form can persist while plausibility, sanction, and denial are repurposed. The article contributes a portable indicator scheme for tracing hollowing across arenas and regime types.
Introduction
Democratic hollowing often proceeds through legality and tempo rather than rupture. Contemporary political systems may retain elections, courts, oversight bodies, and constitutional language while nevertheless undergoing a profound reconfiguration of how power binds. This article conceptualizes hollowing as a transformation in the governance of public reality, meaning the patterned capacity to shape what becomes credible, governable, and sanctionable under conditions where democratic form remains formally intact. Rather than treating democratic decline as institutional breakdown, the analysis shifts attention to the infrastructures that decide whether institutions can act at all.
The argument begins from a relational understanding of political power. Power is not treated as a possession of offices, actors, or regimes, but as an effect produced through social relations that shape capacities, expectations, and constraints (Barnett and Duvall, 2005: 39–42). From this perspective, democracy’s durability cannot be inferred from institutional design alone. It must be traced through the conditions under which evidence stabilizes, oversight attaches consequence, and harms reliably trigger enforceable response. Hollowing names a situation in which these conditions are reorganized upstream of decision making, allowing legality and procedure to persist while their constraining force weakens.
The paper’s title terms designate the governance levers through which the mechanism family operates. Legibility refers to the institutional conditions under which claims and harms become actionable, including recognized forms of proof, standing, and evidentiary thresholds; it is primarily governed through narrative control. Capacity refers to what institutions can operationally do in time, including enforcement bandwidth, administrative burden, and the practical costs of converting visibility into remedy; it is central to how weak accountability loops become durable. Classification refers to the categories of risk, threat, belonging, and credibility through which subjects and claims are pre-sorted, shaping both whose testimony is treated as presumptively credible and which harms are routed toward or away from sanction; it conditions narrative control and impunity signaling by structuring enforceability upstream.
The paper specifies this upstream reorganization through a coupled mechanism family: narrative control, impunity signaling, and weak accountability loops. Narrative control governs intelligibility by shaping who counts as credible and what counts as evidence, not only through messaging but through the management of inference rules that determine what is treated as reasonable, actionable, or dismissible (Benford and Snow, 2000: 614). Impunity signaling recalibrates expectations of enforcement by teaching publics and institutions which violations will not reliably trigger consequence, often through patterned nonconsequence and strategic silence (Maor, 2016: 2). Weak accountability loops stabilize these lessons by routing harm through procedural repetition that preserves legitimacy while leaving incentive structures intact. Within this model, hollowing can advance even in information-rich environments, because power does not only suppress facts. It can reorganize the conditions under which facts acquire institutional weight.
Three contemporary developments intensify the relevance of this framework. First, conspiratorial interpretation has normalized as an interpretive style that collapses complexity into intention and uncertainty into moral urgency, shifting disputes away from shared adjudication and toward identity-aligned plausibility. This matters for governance because conspiracy frames are not merely claims about hidden actors; they function as inference shortcuts that can satisfy epistemic motives (certainty), existential motives (control), and social motives (positive self or group image), making them especially attractive under stress and polarization (Douglas, Sutton and Cichocka, 2017: 539).
Second, platform tempo and generative AI have altered the ratio between production and verification. Information disorder research emphasizes that contemporary breakdowns are not reducible to false content, but involve the phases of creation, production, and distribution, and the incentives that shape how material circulates (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 12–13). It also stresses that interpretive uptake is shaped by identity and group affiliation, and that accepting information that challenges one’s sense of self can be jarring, which makes resistance easier even when messages are persuasive to a neutral observer (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 28–29). Generative AI intensifies these dynamics by lowering the cost of producing persuasive artifacts and by inflating verification burdens. Generative systems are widely accessible and can produce highly convincing outputs, giving ordinary users unprecedented capacity to generate content for benign or malicious purposes, while governance should not assume reliable detection at scale (Fisher, Howard and Kira, 2024: 2–3). Under such conditions, contested authenticity becomes a routine feature of the credibility environment rather than an exceptional event.
Third, contemporary politics is increasingly organized around “files,” meaning leak cycles, document dumps, dossier-like releases, and scandal archives that circulate as if they should settle disputes. The paper treats file circulation not as an epistemic endpoint but as a contested political object whose meaning is fought over through classification, timing, interpretive authorization, and procedural gating. This stance is consistent with scholarship showing that secrecy frequently fails and disclosures recur, yet disclosure does not reliably translate into democratic accountability or enforceable consequence (Fenster, 2014: 309). It is also consistent with work showing how transparency discourse can be repurposed into a strategy of institutional delegitimation and trust erosion, rather than a straightforward accountability gain (Paquin, 2025: 2).
These developments are integrated here not as separate topics but as force multipliers within the same mechanism family. A further micro-form of narrative control, coded in this paper as apocalyptic compliance framing, becomes salient in this context because it converts uncertainty into urgency and urgency into deference. This frame is analytically tractable through recurrent features (false precision, binary compression, personification of systems, immunization against doubt) that govern agency by narrowing the perceived option set and delegitimating verification as delay. It is treated as a portable technique for governing inference norms upstream of factual dispute, which becomes more effective when overload, identity sorting, and provenance fragility intensify (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 28–29; Roberts, 2014: 13).
This framework does not claim that all controversy is narrative control, that all delay is hollowing, or that institutional disagreement is itself diagnostic. The mechanism family is asserted only where the observable indicators converge and where counterindicators (shared evidentiary settlement, deterrent sanctions, or reforms that measurably reduce recurrence) are absent. The contribution is therefore conditional and diagnostic: it specifies when democratic form persists while the binding conversion of evidence into consequence weakens.
The article proceeds in five moves. It first defines democratic hollowing as a transformation in the governance of public reality and specifies the mechanism family. It then operationalizes these mechanisms through observable signatures, including conspiratorial inference governance, apocalyptic compliance framing, file-politics dynamics, and AI-related provenance stress. Next, it outlines a theory-driven comparative design using process tracing across the United States, Canada, and Europe, with Israel–Gaza included as a bounded stress test for tempo, classification, and evidentiary non-convergence under conflict conditions. The results section reports cross-case patterns showing how democratic form persists while plausibility, sanction, and denial are repurposed. The discussion then draws out the paper’s implications for how democratic constraint is being rewired and what kinds of institutional repair would need to be imagined if evidence is to reliably attach to consequence again.
Materials and Methods
This study adopts a theory-driven comparative design oriented toward causal process tracing rather than endpoint correlation. The methodological choice follows from the paper’s central claim: if democratic hollowing proceeds through upstream reconfiguration of plausibility, sanction, and consequence under intact legality, then it will often remain invisible to approaches that treat the formal presence of institutions, the existence of transparency, or regime-level indices as sufficient evidence of constraint. Process tracing is therefore used to reconstruct how the proposed mechanism family operates over time, with attention to sequencing, institutional learning, and the conditions under which harms do or do not acquire enforceable weight (Beach and Pedersen, 2019: 11). The analysis proceeds from a relational conception of power in which constraint is produced through social relations and infrastructural arrangements rather than merely possessed by actors or offices, which justifies treating “plausibility environments” and “accountability routing” as empirical objects rather than rhetorical descriptors (Barnett and Duvall, 2005: 39–42).
Comparative leverage is generated through structured, focused comparison in which the same mechanism-guided questions are applied manually to each setting, and the resulting patterns are then compared cross-case to identify what recurs, what varies, and which combinations appear sufficient for hollowing under intact legality (George and Bennett, 2005: 59). The United States, Canada, and Europe are selected as distinct plausibility environments rather than representative regime types: the United States offers high polarization and rapid credibility conflict; Canada offers high procedural density with a lower-spectacle political style; and Europe offers multi-level governance in which classification and administrative discretion become routinized across national and supranational sites (Huysmans, 2000; Bigo, 2002). Israel–Gaza is included as a bounded stress test rather than as an additional democratic case, used to observe how the same plausibility and accountability dynamics accelerate under conflict conditions where emergency framing, classification struggles, and evidentiary non-convergence intensify. The stress test is governed by an explicit scope condition: it is used to illuminate tempo and plausibility dynamics, not to adjudicate substantive claims of the conflict. Empirically, the stress test is limited to information-environment features (verification latency, provenance contestation, classification regimes, and patterns of evidentiary non-convergence), rather than adjudicating contested factual claims about events on the ground.
The evidentiary base is constructed from triangulated public materials, including institutional texts (legislation, court documents, oversight reports, formal statements), news and investigative journalism, platform and media artifacts (official communications, interviews, strategic messaging), and benchmark infrastructures for contextual calibration. This triangulation is not treated as a generic “more sources is better” rule. It is methodologically necessary because the paper’s mechanisms operate precisely by reshaping how claims are rendered credible, how enforcement expectations are taught, and how procedural routing converts harm into either consequence or repetition. For that reason, the study treats public claims not only as propositional content but as circulating artifacts whose durability, mutability, and uptake matter politically. The information disorder framework is used here as a sensitizing lens for the contemporary conditions under which plausibility is made and unmade: Wardle and Derakhshan emphasize that contemporary problems are not reducible to false content, but include the phases of creation, production, and distribution, and the role of agents and incentives across those phases (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 12–13). They further stress that interpretive uptake is shaped by identity and group affiliation, and that accepting information that challenges one’s sense of self can be jarring, which helps explain why evidence can circulate without producing shared judgment or institutional action even when it appears persuasive to a neutral observer (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 28). These premises justify the paper’s decision to analyze plausibility environments rather than treating “misinformation” as a standalone variable.
Analytically, the paper distinguishes causal mechanisms from contextual amplifiers and from observable micro-signatures. The causal engine is the coupled operation of narrative control, impunity signaling, and weak accountability loops. Platform tempo, generative AI, and scandal-file circulation are treated as amplifying conditions that alter verification costs and attention dynamics, increasing the effectiveness of mechanism deployment without constituting mechanisms on their own. Conspiratorial inference governance and apocalyptic compliance framing are treated as micro-signatures primarily within narrative control, meaning observable packages that shift inference norms and adjudicative standing rather than independent causal engines.
The three mechanisms are operationalized as signatures of infrastructural power rather than as psychological states. The mechanisms are identified through a portable indicator scheme applied consistently across cases. What makes this comparison analytically difficult is precisely what makes it useful: the arenas are close enough in form and vocabulary to appear interchangeable, yet different enough in institutional routing to produce distinct pathways. The proximities and similitudes, shared democratic language, shared transparency rituals, shared platform infrastructures, make mechanism identification harder because the same surface signals can be generated by different underlying processes. This is why the paper does not code hollowing from outcomes alone, but from patterned indicator convergence: how inference rules are governed, how enforcement expectations are taught, and how procedural repetition absorbs consequence. The stress test value of the Israel–Gaza information environment is not that it is “like” the other cases, but that it compresses tempo and classification pressures so the upstream governance of plausibility becomes more observable. The comparative payoff is therefore diagnostic rather than typological: the paper traces how similar democratic forms can host different hollowing dynamics.
Narrative control is indicated by repeated moves that govern inference rather than merely messaging, including referee delegitimation, evidentiary reclassification, identity-sorting cues that convert factual dispute into allegiance conflict, and rhetorical packaging that makes doubt socially costly, including apocalyptic compliance framing (false precision, binary compression, personification of systems, immunization against doubt). Impunity signaling is indicated by patterned nonconsequence or selective sanction that recalibrates enforcement expectations, including strategic silence under reputational threat, procedural stalling that outlasts attention windows, and routinized ambiguity that shifts interpretive burden onto critics. Weak accountability loops are indicated by recurrent institutional processing that preserves legitimacy while leaving incentive structures intact, including repeat inquiries or reviews without measurable enforcement change, jurisdictional diffusion of responsibility, symbolic reforms that do not alter sanction capacity, and outcome latency that repeatedly severs visibility from consequence. Counterindicators are tracked in parallel: dispute resolution through shared evidentiary standards tempers narrative control claims, reliable sanctions and deterrent effects temper impunity signaling claims, and reforms that measurably increase enforcement capacity or reduce recurrence temper weak-loop claims.
Narrative control is operationalized as the governance of intelligibility, meaning patterned efforts to shape who counts as credible and what counts as evidence, including recurrent moves that delegitimate referees, reclassify evidentiary claims, and convert factual disputes into identity disputes (Benford and Snow, 2000: 614). In this paper, conspiratorial interpretation is treated as a specific mode of plausibility engineering within narrative control, because it collapses complex causal fields into agentic intention, distributes epistemic status through in-group loyalty, and converts uncertainty into moral urgency. This operational extension is grounded in the social psychological literature that identifies epistemic, existential, and social motives for conspiracy belief, including the desire to understand one’s environment, feel safe and in control, and maintain a positive image of the self and the social group (Douglas, Sutton and Cichocka, 2017: 539). Methodologically, conspiratorial inference is therefore coded not primarily as a content category but as a shift in inference norms, especially when evidentiary standards are displaced by loyalty tests and skepticism is reframed as moral danger.
A further micro-signature is coded as apocalyptic compliance framing, treated as a portable subset of narrative control that becomes visible across conspiratorial subcultures, crisis discourse, and certain techno-apocalyptic framings. It is identified through four observable features: false precision (specific dates or countdowns without testable mechanism), binary compression (compliance versus catastrophe), personification of systems (AI, “the state,” or “the planet” treated as intentional moral actors), and immunization against doubt (rhetorical conversion of evidentiary caution into moral failure). This signature is included because it governs inference norms upstream of factual dispute by transforming uncertainty into urgency and urgency into deference, intensifying the identity-sorting dynamics emphasized in information disorder research (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 27–28). In practical terms, the analysis tracks whether such frames narrow perceived options, delegitimate verification, and convert procedural latency into interpretive certainty.
Impunity signaling is operationalized by tracing repeated norm violations, legal breaches, or harmful practices that are met with delayed, partial, or absent sanction, with emphasis placed on the enforcement environment as a teacher of expectations rather than on any single event. This mechanism is coded when patterns of nonconsequence or selective enforcement plausibly recalibrate what actors believe they can do without penalty, including when institutions repeatedly fail to attach sanctions in ways that would ordinarily deter repetition. Strategic silence is incorporated here as an active tactic within impunity signaling. Maor defines strategic silence as an organization’s calculated decision to avoid internal and/or external communication in order to advance long-term goals, particularly under reputational threat and anticipated negative media coverage (Maor, 2016: 2). This supports coding nonresponse, ambiguity maintenance, and deflection as actions that can delay accountability traction, shift interpretive burdens onto critics, and exploit institutional tempo.
Weak accountability loops are operationalized as repetitive institutional processing that preserves legitimacy while allowing recurrence. The empirical signature is procedural routing that produces hearings, reviews, reforms, or internal investigations without altering incentive structures, enforcement capacity, or jurisdictional authority in ways that would reduce recurrence. The analysis codes loop dynamics when institutional response becomes routinized as performance, when responsibility disperses through jurisdictional boundaries, and when each cycle closes without changing the probability of repetition.
This paper treats contemporary “file politics” as a high-salience observation site where all three mechanisms can become unusually legible. “Files” refers to leak cycles, document dumps, dossier-like releases, and scandal archives whose circulation is often treated publicly as an epistemic endpoint. Methodologically, the study does not assume that the existence of an archive settles disputes or guarantees consequence. Instead, it tracks how archives are metabolized within plausibility environments: who is authorized to interpret, what classificatory moves determine what counts as proof, what procedural gates are invoked, and how long consequence can be deferred without reputational collapse. Fenster’s analysis supports this stance by demonstrating that government secrecy frequently fails and high-profile disclosures recur, yet the circulation of information does not automatically produce democratic accountability or institutional consequence (Fenster, 2014: 309). Paquin’s analysis further supports the mechanism link between transparency, scandal amplification, and democratic trust erosion by showing how transparency and press-freedom mechanisms can be leveraged by radical-right populist strategies to expose alleged misconduct, selectively amplify scandals, and thereby strengthen anti-establishment narratives that corrode political trust (Paquin, 2025: 2). Together, these sources justify treating file circulation not as resolution but as an arena in which narrative control, impunity signaling, and accountability looping can interact.
Generative AI is treated as a contextual capacity shift that intensifies narrative competition and complicates evidentiary stabilization, not as a separate mechanism. Fisher, Howard and Kira argue that contemporary generative systems facilitate the production of persuasive synthetic media and fluent text at scale, such that ordinary people now have unprecedented power to produce content for benign or malicious purposes (Fisher, Howard and Kira, 2024: 2). They also emphasize limitations in synthetic-content detection tools and caution against governance approaches that presuppose reliable detection at scale, which motivates analysis centered on platform responsibility, circulation, and harm rather than on an assumption that authenticity can always be cleanly and quickly verified (Fisher, Howard and Kira, 2024: 3). In this study, AI is therefore incorporated as a background condition that inflates verification costs, increases volume and velocity, and intensifies the incentive for heuristic sorting under overload. Roberts’s framework helps formalize overload as governable rather than accidental: she theorizes online information control through small changes in the costs of access and attention, including information friction and information flooding, as mechanisms that shape spread and impact under high-information conditions (Roberts, 2014: 13). This supports treating “cognitive overload” not merely as a psychological byproduct of modernity but as a structural condition that can be exploited by actors seeking to reroute public reasoning from adjudication toward allegiance.
Analytically, each case is examined manually using the same mechanism-guided prompts, tracking recurring moves that (1) govern credibility and evidentiary standards, (2) signal nonconsequence, and (3) route accountability through procedures that preserve legitimacy while allowing repetition. The same prompts are extended to track conspiratorial inference governance, apocalyptic compliance framing, file-politics dynamics, and AI-related provenance stress, treated as empirically traceable intensifiers within the same mechanism family (Douglas, Sutton and Cichocka, 2017: 539; Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 12–13, 28; Fisher, Howard and Kira, 2024: 2–3; Roberts, 2014: 13; Fenster, 2014: 309; Paquin, 2025: 2; Maor, 2016: 2). Validity is pursued through triangulation and negative-case attention. Mechanism presence is asserted only when multiple source types converge or when the same pattern is observable across distinct arenas, and counterindicators are actively tracked: narrative control claims are tempered when disputes resolve through shared evidentiary standards; impunity signaling claims are tempered when sanctions reliably attach and deter repetition; and weak accountability loop claims are tempered when reforms measurably alter enforcement capacity or incentive structures. This architecture preserves the paper’s comparative design while integrating the added material as mechanism-consistent extensions rather than as topic drift.
Results
The findings below report the cross-case patterning of the indicator scheme specified in the Methods, showing how the same mechanism family appears across arenas while varying by institutional pathway and tempo. Across the four arenas examined (United States, Canada, Europe, and the Israel–Gaza information environment), the analysis shows that democratic hollowing can proceed through public visibility rather than institutional collapse. Elections, courts, inquiries, and press infrastructures remain formally active, yet the binding force of accountability is repeatedly weakened because the struggle shifts upstream, from adjudicating facts to governing what can count as credible, actionable, and sanctionable in the first place. This is the practical meaning of governance of public reality in the paper’s conceptual framework: the durable capacity to shape plausibility conditions so that institutions appear to process conflict while consequences remain optional, delayed, or selectively applied.
To avoid definitional overreach, the analysis tracks counterindicators case by case. In the United States, counterevidence would include stable cross-partisan referee legitimacy and routinized sanction that produces deterrent effects even under polarization. In Canada, counterevidence would include closure that reliably converts inquiries and oversight into enforceable remedies within attention-relevant tempo windows, rather than diffusing into normal non-closure. In European settings, counterevidence would include transparency and press-freedom infrastructures that increase accountability without being repurposed into systematic trust-destruction, alongside classification regimes that do not pre-sort credibility and enforceability by threat categories. In the Israel–Gaza stress test, counterevidence would include rapid cross-network evidentiary convergence and verification practices that remain institutionally authoritative despite conflict tempo and moral sorting pressures.
A first consistent finding concerns narrative control as plausibility engineering, not spin in the usual sense. In each arena, actors compete to set the rules of inference: who is authorized to interpret, what evidentiary thresholds are considered legitimate, and which interpretive frames become socially safe to adopt. Where narrative control is strongest, contestation does not end because counterevidence is unavailable. Contestation becomes nonresolving because evidence is continually reclassified, and because audiences are routed into identity-aligned plausibility rather than shared verification. This is where conspiratorial uptake becomes methodologically relevant. Conspiracy is not treated here as eccentric belief content but as a shift in adjudication norms that substitutes loyalty tests for evidentiary standards. This shift is reinforced by the fact that conspiracy frames can satisfy epistemic motives (certainty), existential motives (control), and social motives (positive group image). Under stress or polarization, these motives increase the attractiveness of interpretive packages that promise total explanation and moral clarity. The comparative variation concerns the style and locus of this plausibility engineering: in the United States it tends to surface as explicit credibility warfare and referee delegitimation; in Canada it more often travels through institutional tempo and a quieter normalization of non-closure; in European settings it is frequently intensified by classificatory pre-sorting and multi-level administrative discretion; and in the Israel–Gaza information environment it becomes most visible as accelerated inference conflict under high-tempo conditions where verification lateness is itself politicized. Across cases, referee delegitimation, evidentiary reclassification, and identity-sorting cues function as recurring narrative-control indicators, because they shift contestation from what is true to who is authorized to decide what can count as true.
A second finding is that conspiratorial inference does not merely misinform. It can stabilize an otherwise unstable accountability environment by providing a cognitively efficient explanation for why institutions fail to attach consequences. That stabilization is purchased through compression: complex causal fields are collapsed into agentic intention, and ambiguity is retrofitted into narrative certainty. This dynamic becomes most visible in what the paper codes as apocalyptic compliance framing: false precision, binary compression (compliance versus catastrophe), personification of systems (AI as enforcer, society as host), and immunization against doubt through “better safe than sorry” logic. The output is not simply a false claim. It is an interpretive technology that converts uncertainty into urgency, and urgency into deference, thereby governing inference norms upstream of factual dispute. In practical terms, these frames narrow the perceived option set, delegitimate verification as delay, and convert hesitation into moral failure. Comparatively, the mechanism’s function is constant while its triggers vary: in the U.S. and Europe, urgency is often routed through polarized credibility contests and scandal logics; in Canada, it is more likely to attach to procedural latency and institutional “waiting rooms”; and in the Israel–Gaza information environment, urgency and moral sorting are intensified by conflict tempo and asymmetric recognition conditions, making binary compression and immunization against doubt especially politically effective.
The third, and most structurally revealing, result is that file politics operates as a high-salience mechanism amplifier rather than an epistemic endpoint. In the U.S. “Epstein files” imaginary, but also in other leak cycles and dossier-like releases across contexts, document circulation is commonly treated as if it should settle disputes. The analysis finds the opposite pattern. Files routinely become contested objects whose political life is governed by classification, timing, interpretive authorization, and procedural gating. Analytically, file politics is treated as an observation site where narrative control (interpretive authorization and evidentiary reclassification), impunity signaling (nonconsequence despite visibility), and weak accountability loops (procedural absorption of scandal) become simultaneously observable.
The central empirical move is not that secrecy prevents truth. It is that recurring leak cycles and partial disclosure can coexist with deferred consequence. That coexistence is the precise space where the paper’s three mechanisms couple together: narrative control governs how the archive is read, impunity signaling teaches that consequence is optional, and weak accountability loops routinize institutional processing without reducing recurrence. Comparatively, this is where arenas differ most sharply in how the archive is metabolized: in the United States, file politics tends to be absorbed into polarized plausibility battles that convert evidentiary disputes into identity disputes; in Canada, file politics more often produces a procedural absorption pattern in which legitimacy is preserved through “proper process” while enforcement expectations thin; in Europe, transparency and scandal infrastructures can be repurposed into trust-destruction strategies that treat exposure as an end in itself; and in the Israel–Gaza information environment, the file logic is intensified by tempo and contestation over classification, making evidentiary non-convergence a durable outcome rather than a temporary phase.
File politics also clarifies why redaction and incompleteness matter sociopolitically. Redaction does not only remove content. It can produce meaning by revealing the machinery of withholding, intensifying public speculation, and inviting plausibility contests over what the gaps signify. In scandal environments, incompleteness is rarely experienced as a limit on knowledge. It is often experienced as evidence of concealed intent, which invites interpretive escalation. This dynamic helps explain how “the files” can become an endlessly renewable political object: every absence becomes another prompt for narrative capture, and every delay becomes another proof claim. The result is a perverse accountability inversion. Archives can increase attention while decreasing the probability that attention produces enforceable consequence, because plausibility fragmentation outruns institutional processing capacity and because procedural time becomes politically weaponizable. The comparative difference is primarily one of tempo and institutional routing: some settings convert incompleteness into rapid plausibility capture, while others convert it into slow procedural absorption, but the effect converges when consequence fails to attach within a realistic attention window.
A fourth result concerns impunity signaling as a teachable environment. Across arenas, repeated norm violations, legal breaches, or harmful practices that meet delayed, partial, or absent sanction recalibrate expectations about what can be done without penalty. The mechanism is not the violation itself but the enforcement pattern that follows. These signals are amplified when sanction is inconsistent, when investigations remain open without closure, or when procedural responses substitute for binding remedy. Strategic silence functions here as an active tactic rather than an absence. Under reputational threat, noncommunication can delay traction, shift interpretive burden onto critics, and exploit institutional tempo, particularly when media cycles move faster than oversight capacity. When strategic silence co-occurs with procedural routing (reviews, inquiries, “lessons learned” reports), publics can struggle to distinguish genuine enforcement from institutional theater, even while legality remains intact. Comparatively, the U.S. pattern tends to be visible as selective enforcement narratives and overt sanction conflict; the Canadian pathway tends to normalize non-closure and diffusion as “due process”; European settings often express impunity through classification and administrative discretion that pre-sorts enforcement priorities; and the Israel–Gaza stress test makes sanction asymmetries and recognition struggles more immediately visible, clarifying how impunity signaling can be accelerated by emergency framing.
A fifth result extends the framework into contemporary media capacity conditions by showing that generative AI functions as a verification-cost inflator that increases the payoff of plausibility engineering. The analysis does not treat AI as a single causal driver. It treats AI as a capacity shift that increases the volume, velocity, and stylistic persuasiveness of narrative artifacts, thereby increasing incentives for heuristic sorting under overload. Accordingly, AI is treated here as an amplifier of plausibility competition and verification costs that strengthens the effectiveness of narrative control and the durability of weak loops, rather than as an independent mechanism of hollowing.
Under these conditions, provenance disputes become easier to trigger and harder to settle quickly, which strengthens narrative control strategies that keep publics inside suspension long enough for consequence to dissipate. The relevant point is not simply that synthetic content exists. It is that the credibility environment is increasingly organized around contested authenticity, and contestation itself can become the goal because it delays adjudication and weakens the attachment between evidence and enforcement. Comparatively, the effect is mediated by institutional tempo and public trust baselines: where verification infrastructures are politicized or slow relative to circulation, contested authenticity more readily becomes a stable equilibrium; where administrative routing is complex, provenance disputes are easier to proceduralize; and under conflict tempo, authenticity contestation is folded into moral sorting, intensifying non-convergence.
A sixth result is that transparency itself can be weaponized as a trust-destruction toolkit, particularly in European contexts where radical-right populist strategies reframe transparency and press freedom not as accountability mechanisms but as resources for scandal amplification and institutional delegitimation. Here, visibility becomes ambivalent. It can expose wrongdoing, but it can also be mobilized to portray institutions as irredeemably corrupt, thereby reducing willingness to accept institutional adjudication even when it is procedurally sound. This is a direct instance of narrative control and weak accountability loops coupling together: public visibility increases while collective capacity to translate visibility into enforceable sanction decreases, and procedural outcomes are more easily dismissed as either rigged or irrelevant. The comparative implication is that visibility does not travel with consequence: across cases, transparency may increase informational supply while simultaneously degrading the shared adjudicative ground needed for sanction to bind.
Finally, the comparative stress test across the Israel–Gaza information environment and the North American and European arenas underscores the centrality of tempo. High-intensity conflict conditions make plausibility engineering unusually salient because verification is structurally late, and lateness can be reframed as proof of bad faith. Under these conditions, the mechanisms converge. Narrative control sets inference rules and defines who is credible. Impunity signaling teaches that consequence is optional or asymmetrical. Weak accountability loops routinize response without reducing recurrence. The comparative pattern is not that the cases are identical. It is that they exhibit the same underlying mechanism family operating at different speeds and through different institutional pathways, which is precisely what the paper’s framework was built to identify. Queer, disability, and postcolonial analytics are mobilized here as a refusal of stabilizing narrative and a diagnostic of institutionalized power, foregrounding how “individual failure” is produced when power stays silent through administrative deferral and credibility denial, while episodes of excessive loudness are read as reactive performances of authority under perceived loss rather than as evidence of renewed democratic constraint.
Viewed through the title axes, the United States case most clearly foregrounds legibility struggle, as referee legitimacy and evidentiary standing become objects of direct political contestation. Canada foregrounds capacity, as procedural density and administrative burden shape whether oversight can attach consequence within meaningful tempo windows. The European settings foreground classification, as risk and belonging categories pre-sort credibility and enforceability and enable transparency to be repurposed into distrust. Empirically, classification appears as the administrative sorting of persons and claims into threat, risk, belonging, and credibility categories that pre-structure what counts as admissible proof, what triggers exceptional handling, and whose harms are treated as presumptively actionable. The Israel–Gaza stress test compresses all three, revealing how tempo and classificatory asymmetries accelerate evidentiary non-convergence and sanction asymmetry.
Discussion
The results sharpen the paper’s central claim: democratic hollowing is less a story of institutional disappearance than a story of constraint being relocated upstream into the governance of public reality. Across the United States, Canada, and Europe, and under the bounded stress test of the Israel–Gaza information environment, formal democratic practices remain visible. Elections continue, courts sit, oversight bodies operate, journalists publish, and transparency artifacts circulate. Yet the binding force of those practices thins when credibility, sanction, and denial are reorganized so that institutions can “function” without reliably attaching consequence. What changes is not the existence of democratic form, but the dependable conversion of evidence into enforceable response, within a tempo that still matters politically.
This is why the paper’s mechanism family matters. Narrative control, impunity signaling, and weak accountability loops do not operate as separate explanations stacked beside one another. They operate as a coupled system. Narrative control governs intelligibility by shaping inference rules: what counts as evidence, who counts as a credible interpreter, and which interpretive moves are socially legible without penalty. Impunity signaling recalibrates expectations of enforcement by teaching publics and institutions which violations will be absorbed, delayed, or selectively pursued, including through strategic silence when reputational threat is high (Maor, 2016: 2). Weak accountability loops stabilize the arrangement by routing harm through procedural repetition that preserves legitimacy while leaving incentive structures intact. The combined effect is a plausibility environment in which accountability is technically possible yet practically unreliable.
The comparative contribution is not that the four arenas are equivalent. It is that the same upstream mechanism family appears across them, while expressing itself through different institutional pathways and narrative styles. In the United States, narrative control is most visible as explicit credibility warfare. Referees are not merely contested, they are actively delegitimated, and adjudication is recoded as partisan aggression. This is the environment in which conspiracy frames become politically effective not because they “replace facts” but because they rewire the rules of settlement, turning skepticism into a loyalty failure and verification into complicity. The motivational literature helps explain why this style takes hold under stress and polarization: conspiracy narratives can satisfy epistemic motives (certainty), existential motives (control), and social motives (positive group image), which makes identity-aligned plausibility a resilient equilibrium (Douglas, Sutton and Cichocka, 2017: 539). The U.S. implication is not that institutions vanish. It is that the credibility environment becomes structured so that institutional action, including enforcement, can be narratively framed as illegitimate regardless of evidentiary quality.
In Canada, the same mechanism family is less spectacular but no less relevant. The pathway is often routed through capacity, tempo, and procedural density. Hollowing can accumulate when complex administrative routing, jurisdictional diffusion, and high transaction costs normalize non-closure while preserving the appearance of responsiveness. This does not require overt referee war. It can proceed through “proper process” that repeatedly outlasts attention windows, teaching publics that resolution is improbable even when institutions appear to be working. The effect is a quiet form of impunity signaling: not necessarily the dramatic message that “nothing will happen,” but the slower lesson that consequence is unlikely to attach in time to matter. Here, strategic silence and procedural repetition can interlock: silence buys time, and loops consume time, producing an accountability experience that is formally lawful yet practically exhaustible.
European settings clarify a third pathway that hinges more heavily on classification and administrative discretion within multi-level governance. In these environments, plausibility is often pre-organized by categories of risk, threat, legality, and belonging, which shapes whose claims are treated as presumptively credible, whose harms are routed into bureaucratic recognition, and whose speech is treated as noise. That classificatory pre-sorting matters because it makes narrative control less dependent on overt propaganda. Narrative control can be exercised by governing the standing conditions under which speech counts and by structuring the administrative legibility of harm. These are precisely the conditions under which transparency can become ambivalent. Visibility can expose wrongdoing, but it can also be weaponized into scandal amplification and institutional delegitimation strategies that erode trust while leaving governance machinery intact (Paquin, 2025: 2–3). In the paper’s terms, this is a tight coupling between narrative control and weak accountability loops: scandal can proliferate while institutional consequence remains optional, particularly when adjudication itself becomes a contested political object.
The Israel–Gaza information environment functions as a bounded stress test that compresses these dynamics and makes tempo decisive. Under conflict conditions, verification is structurally late relative to circulation, and lateness is easily reframed as proof of bad faith. Information disorder research helps specify why this matters: interpretation is shaped by identity and group affiliation, and accepting information that challenges one’s sense of self can be jarring, making resistance to dissonant evidence easier even when the message appears persuasive to a neutral observer (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 28–29). In conflict tempo, this identity-sorting becomes more intense and more morally saturated, making evidentiary non-convergence durable. What the stress test clarifies is not a unique “conflict mechanism,” but the acceleration of the same upstream family: narrative control sets inference rules, impunity signaling becomes asymmetrical in recognition and enforcement, and weak loops routinize response without reducing recurrence. The stress test therefore protects the paper from overclaiming: it shows how quickly the infrastructure can collapse into churn when tempo outruns institutional adjudication capacity.
Seen comparatively, the paper’s added concepts are best understood as intensifiers inside the same mechanism family rather than as separate causal engines. Conspiratorial inference governance functions as a style of inference management that turns adjudication into allegiance. File politics functions as a high-salience arena in which all three mechanisms become legible at once: archives circulate as if they should settle disputes, yet they often become contested objects whose meaning is fought over through authorization, classification, timing, and procedural gates. Generative AI functions as a verification-cost inflator. The crucial point is not simply synthetic content, but the shifting ratio between production and verification. Generative systems lower the cost of producing persuasive artifacts while raising verification burdens and destabilizing provenance cues (Fisher, Howard and Kira, 2024: 2–3). Under these conditions, contested authenticity becomes a routine political condition, and suspension itself becomes strategically valuable. Roberts’s account of information friction and flooding gives language to this structural feature: small changes in the cost of access and attention can shape what spreads and what stabilizes, making overload a governable environment rather than a mere byproduct (Roberts, 2014: 13). This aligns with the paper’s broader claim that public reality is governed upstream, in the conditions under which claims can become actionable.
The apocalyptic compliance script clarifies one especially potent form of narrative control in this environment. Its four markers, false precision, binary compression, personification of systems, and immunization against doubt, should be read as an observable package that governs agency by narrowing perceived options and delegitimating verification as delay. As a package, it converts uncertainty into urgency and urgency into deference. Comparatively, its uptake is most likely where plausibility environments are already saturated: when identity-sorting is strong, when trust baselines are low, when verification is structurally late, and when institutions cannot attach consequence within a tempo that publics still experience as meaningful.
A further contribution of the comparative framing is to return the paper’s title terms, legibility, capacity, and classification, from abstraction to mechanism terrain. Legibility refers to the conditions under which harms and claims become institutionally actionable, including evidentiary thresholds, standing, and recognized forms of proof. Capacity refers to what institutions can operationally do in time, including the tempo of investigation, enforcement bandwidth, administrative burden, and the practical costs of converting visibility into remedy. Classification refers to how categories of risk, threat, belonging, and credibility pre-sort subjects and claims, shaping who is presumptively trusted, who is presumptively suspect, and what kinds of harms are recognized as sanctionable. Read this way, the cases show different emphases without changing the engine: the U.S. pathway foregrounds legibility war and referee conflict; Canada foregrounds capacity, tempo, and the normality of procedural absorption; Europe foregrounds classification and the weaponization of transparency into distrust; Israel–Gaza foregrounds the acceleration of all three under conflict tempo and asymmetric recognition.
These findings also sharpen what is at stake in the paper’s policy implication without overpromising quick fixes. Common remedies can misfire because they often add information without rebuilding adjudication capacity, or add procedures without restoring enforceable consequence. Transparency initiatives can increase informational supply while weakening shared standards of settlement. Procedural reforms can proliferate oversight artifacts while leaving sanction capacity unchanged. Platform interventions can remove some harmful content while leaving provenance contestation and identity sorting intact. The paper’s implication is therefore infrastructural: democratic repair requires governance with teeth, meaning the redesign of credibility and enforcement infrastructure so that evidence can once again attach to consequence within a realistic tempo, and so that legibility, capacity, and classification do not silently route some harms into permanent nonconsequence.
Taken together, the comparative analysis supports a hard conclusion. A polity can learn to live inside degraded evidentiary conditions. It can normalize permanent suspicion toward some claims and permanent indulgence toward others. It can treat accountability as ritual and truth as strategic. When that happens, democratic vocabulary remains available, but it no longer binds. The paper’s contribution is to show how that learning occurs, mechanism by mechanism, and why contemporary conspiratorial cultures, file politics, and generative AI intensify rather than replace the older dynamics of narrative control, impunity, and procedural deferral. The forward-facing question that follows is not whether democracy still “exists” as form, but whether the credibility environment has been rebuilt so that democracy can still bind as force.
