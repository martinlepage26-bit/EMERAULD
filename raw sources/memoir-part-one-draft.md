## CHAPTER ONE: THE FERAL CAT BITE

The cat was feral. No one had ever taught it that humans were safe. I was four, maybe five—the memory has the texture of things held underwater, the edges soft, the colors slightly wrong.

What I can reconstruct: a yard. My old house. The cat near something—near the fence or near the step or near wherever small children stand when they're occupying the world alone, which I was often doing. The cat's teeth in my skin. The hospital after. The word *tetanus* arriving before I had the right size for it.

I remember the shot more than the bite.

What I remember best is the afterward.

There is a shape that follows certain childhood injuries—not the sharp shape of the wound itself, but the shape of the room reorganizing around it. The adults check for damage. They determine severity. They administer the appropriate response. And then, having done so, they return to whatever they were doing before. The incident is filed. The file is closed. The child is left with the open thing the file was meant to contain.

The cat bite was the first time I noticed this shape.

This is not a story about bad parents. I want to say that clearly, at the beginning, because the memoir form is brutal in what it produces—it produces monsters, because narrative requires causality and causality requires someone to carry the weight. My parents were not monsters. They were people in their twenties with children they hadn't been fully prepared for, navigating a divorce, managing their own versions of a world that hadn't made much room for them either.

What I am describing is structural. A gap between what a child needs from the people around them and what those people had available to give.

The gap was attention. Not love—I don't think the word is love. Attention: the sustained, organized registering of what is actually happening to someone. Children need to be seen happening. They need the room to rearrange itself slightly when they are hurt, to say *yes, you are here, yes, this is real, yes, it matters*. Without that registration, the wound doesn't disappear. It goes underground.

The wound from the cat bite was not serious. In the medical sense. The tetanus shot was precautionary. The bite healed. Medically, this is a story with a good outcome.

But the lesson that arrived with the bite was not medical. It was structural. It arrived not from the bite itself but from the shape of the aftermath:

*You can be hurt here. The room will not be interrupted.*

---

I was the youngest of three. My eldest sister Patricia is ten years older than me and was already, by the time I was four, becoming a different kind of person—old enough to begin the adult life that would eventually carry her away from the particular weather of our household. She is my godmother. There is a formal relationship underneath the sisterhood, a contract, a named responsibility that I will return to later in this book because it becomes important.

Martine is the middle child. Five or six years between us. What I remember of Martine at four, at five, is the vague warmth of her presence—the way she sometimes included me and sometimes didn't, which is ordinary enough for a child that age, a child navigating her own version of the same geography.

The geography was fractured from the beginning. My parents divorced when I was young—I have no memory of them together—which means my childhood was distributed across multiple addresses, multiple city grids, multiple domestic logics. My father's house, my mother's house, my stepfather's house, and the negative spaces between them—weekends, school breaks, the unreliable schedule of a custody arrangement that suited the adults who had made it.

Feral things live in negative spaces. The cat understood this.

---

The pattern the cat bite introduced repeated itself, as patterns do, because patterns are what early experience writes into the nervous system before the mind has the capacity to read them.

Age seven or eight. A camping trip with Martine and her friends—older kids, teenagers, not quite my world but I was there, included by proximity rather than invitation. Which is how I often ended up in things. When we left, someone gave me a city bike, thin wheels, not made for hills. I don't know whose decision it was. I don't know if anyone thought about it.

I went down the hill. The brakes weren't right or I didn't know how to use them. I ended up in the ditch with my lip split open.

Martine was somewhere else. I was comforted by people I didn't know well—camp staff, strangers, the ambient kindness of adults who happened to be present.

Not mine.

That phrase—*not mine*—is the one I've been turning over for years. Not mine doesn't mean the comfort wasn't real. It means it existed in the wrong relational field. I was being held by people who didn't know me, which is not the same as being held by people who do.

A few weeks later, or maybe the same summer—time is elastic at that age—I slept at my sister's friend's house. There were older teenagers around. Some of them were sniffing gas or paint. A young man came into the room where I was sleeping and kicked me because I was in the way.

He didn't know me. I was in his way. He kicked me.

What I remember about that event is not the kick. What I remember is the afterward: the same shape. The room reorganizing briefly, returning to what it had been doing. The incident filed. The file closed.

---

I want to pause here and name what I'm doing, because it matters for how you read this book.

I am not collecting grievances. I am not building a case. What I am doing—what I have always done, in the scholarship, in the journals, in the late-night inventory-taking that passes for sleep—is identify structure.

The structure in Part One of this book is this: *a child's pain, in these years, did not interrupt the room.* The room had a logic. The logic did not include the child as a primary variable. And the child—I, at four and five and seven and eight—absorbed this and adapted. The adaptation was useful. It was also the beginning of everything else this book describes.

Borderline personality disorder. I'll say the words early and not return to them very often, because they are a map and I am interested in the territory. What I am going to describe, throughout this book, is the territory.

The territory is this: a person for whom the room's indifference, experienced early enough and often enough, became the expected condition of all rooms. Who learned, before language arrived to name it, that the safest posture was the minimum footprint—hurt quietly, occupy efficiently, do not interrupt.

It is a useful posture. I want to be honest about that. It produces things: the observation, the analysis, the capacity to stay when leaving would be easier, the capacity to endure without making too much noise.

It also produces things I haven't yet named, things this book is in part an attempt to name.

---

There is one more thing about the cat I want to say.

It was feral, which means no one had taught it that humans were safe. No one had given it evidence. And so its instinct was correct, from the inside—everything it had experienced confirmed that the right response to a strange presence was to bite first.

I spent a long time being that cat.

Pre-empting abandonment by leaving first. Pre-empting rejection by refusing before being refused. Pre-empting the room's indifference by not entering the room at all, or by entering it in a way already calibrated to the minimum. Already managing the room's experience of me before the room had a chance to register what it thought.

This is not a flaw, exactly. Or not only a flaw. It is a survival technology. The question this book asks—and does not always answer—is: *what does the person who mastered surviving pay, across a life, for having had to master it?*

The cat bit me.

The room reorganized briefly and returned to itself.

I became, in response, someone who learned not to expect the room to reorganize.

That's where this begins.

## CHAPTER TWO: THE WOODS AT LAC KÉNOGAMI

When I was nine years old, my father moved us to the woods near Lac Kénogami, in the Saguenay, and I stayed there for approximately a year and a half. That period is, depending on which calendar I apply to it, either the worst sustained abandonment of my childhood or the beginning of everything I have ever loved about being alive.

Both things are true. They do not contradict each other. That is a lesson this book keeps returning to.

The house was large for two people—my father and me. It was surrounded by trees the way a sentence is surrounded by silence. Lac Kénogami was nearby. There was a horse, and two dogs, and rabbits and chickens we kept out back, and the particular texture of winter in that landscape—cold that means something different than city cold, cold that has a physical presence, a weight, a color. The blue-white of serious intent.

My father often left me alone.

I'm going to write that simply, because elaboration would turn it into something it wasn't. It wasn't dramatic. He had a life to live, things to do, and at nine I was old enough, by certain measures, to be left. The house was safe. The woods were safe. I knew where things were.

I was also nine.

But the aloneness—I need to say this carefully—was not only loss. I want to resist the memoir convention that transforms every childhood solitude into wound. Because what happened in that aloneness, in those woods, in that particular cold, was something I cannot fully explain and have spent thirty years trying to explain.

---

I remember lying in the snow.

Not falling in the snow. Not playing in it. Deliberately lying down, flat on my back, the cold seeping through my coat—and feeling something from the ground that I can only describe as warmth. Not physical warmth. Not thermal warmth. Something that came up through the frozen earth and the snow and into my back, and that said—in a language that is not quite language, in the register of things known before knowing—*I am here. You are here. This is real.*

The trees had presences. I knew this without having been taught it. The energy around them was distinct and legible, the way different people have distinct and legible presences when you're sensitive to that kind of thing. Some trees were attending to something. Some were simply there. Some had a quality I can only describe as *watching*, though watching is inadequate.

I did not know the word *pagan* at nine. I did not know *animism*, or *earth religion*, or *Wicca*, or any of the frameworks I would later encounter that gave intellectual structure to what I was experiencing. I knew only what I knew: the earth was alive. The earth was not indifferent. And here, in this cold, on this ground, lying in the snow and feeling warmth rise up to meet me, I was being held by something that did not require anything from me in return.

This is the first time I remember being held.

I want to sit with that for a moment, because it is not a small thing to say. The first time I remember being held—not by a person, not in the social register, but in the way a body needs to be held, which is: received, witnessed, recognized as present and real—the first time I remember that, I was nine years old and lying in the snow in the woods by Lac Kénogami.

The earth was the first container that didn't have somewhere else to be.

---

We had rabbits and chickens, my father and I. We had the horse, and the two dogs. We collected eggs in the morning, which is one of the simplest and most reliable satisfactions available to a human body: the warm weight of an egg in a cold hand. I remember that sensation with a precision I cannot explain.

We also ate rabbits. We also ate chickens.

My father treated this as ordinary, which it was, because it is. Animals eat other animals. That is the logic of the world before sentiment is added to it. He was not cruel in his treatment of the animals. He was matter-of-fact. This is an animal. This animal will be food. This is how food happens.

What it taught me was something I've had to think hard about to articulate.

It taught me the value of life by placing it in direct relation to death. Not as tragedy—as fact. Things are alive. Things die. The living has weight because the dying is real. You cannot eat a rabbit without registering, somewhere in the body, that the rabbit was alive. That it had warmth. That warmth is the thing that goes.

I think that year in the woods made me a pagan before I had the word for it. The earth was sacred—I felt this in my body. Life was valuable—I felt this every morning collecting eggs. Death was real—I felt this too, and feeling it made life not sad but weighted, serious in the right way.

Sacred doesn't mean untouchable. Sacred means: *this has weight. This is in the real. This deserves attention.*

The earth was sacred. The animals were sacred. I was nine, alone most of the time, and the world around me was more present and more reliable than any room I had yet encountered.

---

The summers before, I had been learning something else.

The camping trip, the bike in the ditch. The sleepover, the kick. I had been accumulating evidence about the logic of human rooms. The woods offered a different logic. In the woods, things were exactly what they were. The cold was cold. The warmth was warmth. Nothing pretended. Nothing reorganized itself for the sake of appearance. The tree didn't decide to be a tree to make an impression on you. It simply was.

I found this restful in a way I could not have named at nine but can name now: the woods did not require me to manage their perception of me. I could exist in them without performance, without the particular vigilance required in human rooms. I was not adjusting, not reading, not calculating the minimum footprint. I was just there.

I think of that year as the year I became religious.

Not religious in the institutional sense—the church was a human building with human rules and human power dynamics. Religious in the old sense: *religio*, from *religare*, to bind again, to reconnect. What I was reconnecting to, in those woods, was something I must have known before—something the first years of fractured geography and unregistered wounds had partially severed.

The earth bound me back.

---

There is something I need to put in this chapter, and I'm going to put it without softening.

One December weekend—I was nine—my father told me on a Friday that I would go to my mother's after school and spend the weekend there. The instructions were clear: *go to your mother's, it's the weekend.*

I arrived at my mother's house around four in the afternoon. My stepfather was home. He was furious—I don't know the reason and I don't think I ever knew the reason—and he drove me back to my father's place immediately. He didn't ask me anything. He didn't explain anything. I was a problem to be returned to its origin.

My father wasn't home.

I was nine years old. It was December in the Saguenay. Dark by four-thirty. The house was large. The woods were around it.

I was alone.

I don't know how long I waited. Time does strange things in moments like this—it expands and compresses simultaneously, the way wounds do. I was in a big house in the woods at night in December, alone, at nine, and no adult in my life had organized the weekend in a way that accounted for the possibility that one piece of the plan might fail.

What I remember: the sound of the house. The way silence in the woods is not empty—it has texture, it moves, animals in it, wind—but the silence inside the house was different. The specific silence of a space designed for people that currently contained none. The lights I turned on, making rounds. The cold the house was already beginning to hold now that no one was managing the heat.

I don't remember crying. I think I was past the developmental point where crying registers as something with potential to produce help. Crying is a communication. Communications require a receiver. I was already learning not to send signals into rooms with no one in them.

What I did was sit down and wait. Very still. The way a child waits who has learned that waiting is the only available action.

My father eventually returned. He must have. I slept in the house that night. The record closes there.

But the stone settled a little further down.

---

That year in the woods ended, as things always end. My father's arrangements shifted, or I shifted back to my mother's, or the adult calendar reorganized in the way adult calendars reorganize with minimal consultation with the people most affected by them.

I took two things with me when I left.

The first was the knowledge that Earth was alive. That it was sacred. That lying in the snow at nine years old, I had been held by something real.

The second was the accumulating knowledge of my own capacity for solitude—not the healthy solitude that builds interiority and creativity, though that too, but the specific solitude of a child who has learned to stop expecting rooms to reorganize.

These two things are not unrelated. The capacity for communion with the earth and the capacity for radical self-reliance in the absence of human holding—they grew from the same root. The same aloneness that left me on the floor of a dark winter house also sent me out into the woods and gave me the silence to hear what the trees were saying.

I don't know how to feel about that. I mean that literally. I have turned it over many times and I don't know whether to be grateful for what the abandonment built in me, or furious at the condition that required it. Probably both. The memoir form keeps pushing me toward resolution on this point, and I keep refusing.

What I know is that by the time I left the Saguenay, the earth had become my first reliable container.

The woods gave me a God that didn't betray me.

I've been looking for that God in human rooms ever since.

## CHAPTER THREE: THE BODY AS EVIDENCE

My father and I took a snowmobile to buy milk once, that winter in Saguenay. I sat behind him holding the bags. We hit a curb—or maybe a buried rock, the kind that stays invisible until the ground decides to reveal it—and I got thrown off.

Still holding the milk.

When he finally noticed I was gone—it took a moment, maybe longer than a moment—he turned back and found me sitting in the snow with both bags intact.

He laughed.

Not cruelly. I want to be accurate about this. He laughed the way people laugh at the genuinely comic image of a small child thrown from a snowmobile and still holding the grocery bags, because the image is objectively funny. A child with more distance from the situation might have laughed too.

I was the child inside the situation.

I have thought about this incident many times. Not obsessively—I am not in this book to perform the obsessive accounting of wounds, which is its own kind of theater—but I've returned to it because of what it reveals about a particular dynamic. In the comedy of the image, my physical experience of being thrown was not the salient fact. The retained milk was the salient fact. The child's body was, briefly, an afterthought.

This is a minor incident. I want to be clear about that. This is not the kind of incident that should require therapeutic intervention. Children fall off things. Fathers laugh at the wrong moments. This is not the story of an extraordinary childhood. It is the story of an ordinary one, and the specificity of how an ordinary childhood can accumulate—how ordinary indifferences layer into structural patterns.

The snowmobile was one more piece of evidence.

The body recorded it.

---

When I was around ten, someone dunked my head underwater.

My father's girlfriend's brother's place. A pool. A summer. The details are inexact in the way early memories are inexact when the mind has decided to soften the periphery and retain only the core.

The core: a woman—connected to my father's life, not to mine—plunged my head hard into the water and held it. When she let go, and I came up, she explained that it was her birthday. She could do whatever she wanted.

There was laughter.

I remember the pressure of the water and the logic of the explanation arriving simultaneously—*birthday, therefore rights over bodies near me*—and the way the adults around us processed this as an acceptable event in the category of *summer fun*.

What I felt, coming up from the water, was not panic. I was not in danger of drowning. I was just, briefly, forcibly submerged by someone who had decided my body was available for her use.

The afternoon continued. The adults continued.

The body recorded it.

---

These are small events. I keep saying this because I mean it. I am not writing a book about extraordinary abuse. I am writing a book about a specific, ordinary calibration—the calibration of a child's body to a world in which its signals are routinely filed and closed.

The medical literature on adverse childhood experiences operates on a scoring system: the more exposures, the higher the risk for later outcomes. It is a blunt instrument. Blunt instruments have their uses. What they miss is the texture of the accumulation—the way small, filed incidents layer into a posture, into a nervous system setting, into a way of being in rooms.

I am documenting the texture.

And then there are the events that are not small.

---

When I was around twelve, I broke my leg.

I jumped off a roof—into snow, which had hardened, which I had not anticipated. The impact was wrong. The leg was wrong.

What happened next was not that I was taken care of. What happened next was that my stepfather had a major motorcycle accident at approximately the same time.

I'm going to let that land.

He had a motorcycle accident. He was injured seriously. These things happen. And with two injured people in the household—an adult with a serious accident and a child with a broken leg—the gravity of the room reorganized around the adult.

I went to live with him.

I want to be precise about the logic, because the logic is the thing: I was sent to live with my stepfather—the person who had just had the major accident—in a context where I would ostensibly be helpful. The reasoning was that I was mobile enough (my leg was broken, but already beginning to heal) to be of use, and that being useful in someone else's household was better than being an additional burden in my mother's.

What happened to my leg in this arrangement: it became invisible.

Not medically. Medically it was still broken, still healing, still the thing it was. But narratively, socially, relationally—in the room's logic—it ceased to be the operative injury. The adult's injury was the operative injury. The child's injury was the background.

I carried that broken leg through that period in the background.

This is the event that most precisely names the pattern of Part One: *the body as a fact that the room's logic overrides.* The cat bite. The bike crash. The pool. And now the broken leg made invisible by the proximity of a more serious injury in a body the room considered more important.

The body was becoming very good at recording things no one else was recording.

---

There is one more thing in this period. I am going to name it and not illuminate it, because it exists in my memory as a gap rather than a scene.

Around twelve or thirteen, I became aware that something very dark had happened involving my former stepfather's son. I know this because there are parts of that house I cannot access in my memory. When I approach them, the memory stops. Not the way a memory fades—the way a memory has been blocked. There is a surface there, solid and opaque, and behind it something I have not been able to retrieve.

I am not going to speculate about what's behind it. I don't know what's behind it.

The point of the gap is that it is a gap.

The body recorded something the mind has refused to give back.

The forensic record includes the gap itself. A book about a body that learned to make itself invisible would be incomplete if it didn't name the thing the body made invisible even from its owner. This is where the record stops being fully mine. That is part of the record.

---

I said earlier that I became a pagan in the woods by Kénogami. But paganism, in the way I came to practice it, is also a discipline of evidence: the body is a sensing instrument. The body knows things. The body records things.

Lying in the snow at nine, I felt warmth rise from the frozen earth. That was the body as instrument.

The body that kept the milk on the snowmobile. The body that came up from the pool. The body whose broken leg was filed behind the adult injury.

These are the same body. Both things are true of it simultaneously. The body that can receive the sacred and the body that is routinely not the primary variable in any given room.

I have spent forty years trying to reconcile these two facts about the same body.

This book is part of that attempt.

## CHAPTER FOUR: THE FOUR OATHS

In 1993, the year *Hocus Pocus* came out, my friends tricked me into believing I had turned into a black cat.

Here is how it happened: at sunrise, after a night of sleepover mythology—the kind children construct from whatever materials are available, which at nine in 1993 were thin but earnest—they told me I had turned back. Which implied that I had turned. I believed them. I went home and I told people. People thought I was crazy, and I stopped talking. Not because I stopped believing, but because I understood—at nine, without having the framework for it—that the belief was the kind of thing that had to be held privately.

The trick was a humiliation. The mechanics of it are the mechanics of a particular kind of social cruelty: create the vulnerability first, then exploit it. I had believed them not because I was stupid but because I was already, at nine, capable of genuine metaphysical conviction. That capacity is what they saw. That is what they used.

But I want to say something about what the trick seeded.

It worked—in the wrong direction. It didn't break the belief. It confirmed the belief, underground, where it couldn't be used against me. The lesson was not *magic is not real*. The lesson was: *don't tell people what you know until you know what kind of room you're in.* Don't send signals into rooms that haven't earned them.

This was the fourth oath in advance, arrived at by humiliation rather than intention.

*Hocus Pocus* made me a witch. The trick made me a witch who kept her practice private.

Both of those things are still true of me.

---

My father hit me sometimes.

I need to say this with precision and without excessive narration, because it is part of the record and the record requires it, but I am not interested in producing the expected memoir moment around it—the scene of dramatic violence, the before-and-after arc, the pivot point. What happened was not unusual for a particular time and place and class formation. It was a hand, open, leaving a print.

I remember the print. I remember the hand-shaped mark on my face.

I do not remember what I had done. I don't think it matters what I had done.

What I remember more clearly is the atmosphere of it—not one specific incident but the pattern. The understanding that certain thresholds, crossed, produced a physical consequence. The way this becomes background rather than event. The way a child calibrates their behavior to a world where correction arrives in the body.

The calibration produces skills. It produces the observer. It produces the person who reads rooms. It produces the person who knows, before the room knows, what's coming.

My father was not a monster. He was a man who hit his children sometimes, which was ordinary in the world he'd grown up in, which was itself a world that hit children.

But what it did to my sense of the body as a site of consequence—what it did to the understanding that certain transgressions are paid for in the body—that persists.

The body remembered.

---

My mother tried to slap me once when I was around thirteen.

I caught her hand.

I don't know where the reflex came from—the speed, the certainty. I stopped her hand in the air. And I told her: *you can't do this anymore.*

What I said was for my sister too. I knew this as I was saying it. Martine was somewhere in the logic of that moment even if she wasn't in the room. I was drawing a line not only for my body but for both of us.

I don't think this was heroic. I think it was the product of a specific accumulation—of a body that had been recording evidence long enough to finally produce a reflex.

There is something in the architecture of borderline experience—I'm going to use the clinical word once, here, because it names a real structure—that has to do with defiance as self-definition. When someone tells you *you can't do this*, the response is sometimes *then I will definitely do this.* Not because the thing was wanted, but because the prohibition is an identity threat. To be told *no* by an authority is, when your sense of self is sufficiently organized around opposition, to be told *you don't exist.* And the most available response to that is: *watch me.*

I am not proud of every instance of this in my life. It has cost me. It has cost other people.

But at thirteen, stopping my mother's hand, it was right. The reflex was right. The line was right. The body had been absorbing for nine years and it started drawing lines.

---

There is a blocked memory in this period. I've named it already: something involving my former stepfather's son, something around twelve or thirteen, something behind a surface I can't penetrate.

I want to say one more thing before I leave it.

A block is not nothing. A memory that is blocked has still done its work in the body. The nervous system records what the mind refuses to store consciously. Whatever is behind that surface has been shaping the way I orient to certain kinds of situations—certain kinds of men, certain kinds of power, certain kinds of rooms—without my having access to the specific content that produced the shaping.

This is part of the ledger too. Not the event, which I cannot access. But the negative space around it, and what it has cost to carry a gap in myself that I cannot see into.

Forensic work includes the evidence you can't retrieve.

---

At eleven, I made a decision.

Not a game. I want to be clear about that, because the temptation when describing an eleven-year-old's solitary religious dedication is to soften it into precocious whimsy—something charming and harmless and temporary. This was not that.

I decided to dedicate myself as a solitary witch.

I had been accumulating the material for this decision since nine: since the woods by Kénogami, since *Hocus Pocus*, since the trick, since the four years of evidence that the human rooms I moved through would not hold me the way the earth had held me. I had been looking for the container that didn't break. At eleven, I understood that I would have to build it myself.

The dedication was formal. Private. I don't remember every detail of the ritual—I had limited resources, a child's understanding of ceremony, the scraps of knowledge gathered from whatever was available. But I remember the quality of the intention. The way it landed in the body differently than other decisions I had made. The weight of it.

And then the four oaths.

*To know.* This one I understood immediately: the obligation to learn, to study, to refuse the comfort of ignorance. Knowledge is not a pleasure—it is a responsibility. The witch who does not know is the witch who is controlled by what she doesn't know.

*To will.* The obligation to act, to make things happen, to use the power once recognized. Not to wait for permission. The will is the engine. Knowledge without will is paralyzed. To will is to take the knowledge seriously enough to move.

*To dare.* The obligation to risk. To enter the unknown. To do the thing that has no guarantee. The witch who does not dare is the witch who stays safe and small—which is not a witch at all.

*To be silent.* This one I had already been practicing for years. The oath not to speak the practice into hostile ears. Not out of shame—silence in the craft is not shame, it is discretion. Knowing what the room can hold and giving it only what it can hold.

The four oaths are a complete ethics for someone who has learned to move through rooms that don't account for them. You learn everything you can. You use what you've learned. You take the risk required. You know when not to speak.

I was eleven. I was already practicing three of the four without the framework. The dedication gave them a frame. The frame gave them meaning. The meaning gave them—and this is the word I want—*authority.*

Authority: not power-over, but power-from-within. The first kind I had ever held. The first container I had built that belonged to no one but me.

---

What Part One has been about, understood from the outside, from the position of the adult writing it:

A child who was present in adult worlds while being essentially unprotected in them.

A child whose body learned, across many small and a few large events, that pain does not interrupt the room.

A child who found, in the woods, in the sacred, in the earth, a container that held them without requiring them to manage the container's experience of being asked to hold.

A child who, at eleven, having accumulated enough evidence, built their own container out of oaths and will and the particular silence that protects what cannot yet defend itself in the open.

That child is the person this book describes.

The oaths, once made, were kept.

To know: I went on to the doctorate.

To will: I built, and tried to build, and built again, over and over, from rubble.

To dare: this book.

To be silent: I waited until now.

Now I'm not waiting anymore.

---
