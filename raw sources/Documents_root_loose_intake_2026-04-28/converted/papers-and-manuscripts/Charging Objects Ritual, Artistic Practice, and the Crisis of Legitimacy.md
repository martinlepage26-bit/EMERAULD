**Charging Objects: Ritual, Artistic Practice, and the Crisis of Legitimacy**

### **Abstract**

This article develops *charge* as an analytic concept for examining how objects acquire efficacy and legitimacy outside institutional regimes of authorization. Drawing on contemporary Pagan material practices and art theory, it argues that charge should be understood not as belief, symbolism, or voiced invocation, but as a relational and procedural condition emerging from material arrangement, temporal persistence, and constraint. The analysis centers on *Wheels of Will*, an independent Pagan art project composed of ritual assemblages, sealed vessels, and ephemeral installations that deliberately refuse explanatory discourse and institutional validation.

Using visual analysis, material semiotics, and practice-based inquiry, the article examines how charge operates through aftermath, decay, containment, and repetition rather than through declaration or transparency. By withholding markers of authorship, intention, and ritual explanation, *Wheels of Will* functions as a counter-model to dominant regimes of legitimacy in both contemporary Paganism and academic knowledge production, where efficacy is increasingly recognized only when traceable to a clearly identifiable human source.

Situating this intervention within debates on material religion, ritual efficacy, and non-human agency, the article shows how charge can persist without authorization and how recognition itself has become a primary site of instability. In doing so, it reframes contemporary anxieties about authenticity, authorship, and artificial intelligence as part of a broader crisis of legitimacy grounded in human-centered assumptions about agency and value.

**Keywords:** Charge; Material Religion; Ritual Efficacy; Pagan Material Culture; Legitimacy; Material Agency; Visual Practice; Non-Human Agency

**Introduction**

Across ritual studies, art theory, and the social sciences, objects are rarely treated as agents in their own right. When they are granted analytic attention, it is most often as symbols, representations, or extensions of human intention. Yet in many contemporary Pagan practices, objects are not merely expressive. They are *charged*. To charge an object is not simply to assign meaning to it, but to render it operative, consequential, and capable of producing effects beyond its immediate context. Charge, in this sense, is neither belief nor symbolism alone. It is a condition through which objects come to matter, persist, and act. Recent work in material religion has emphasized that religious life is constituted through objects, images, and sensory practices that do not require belief-centered explanation to function as sites of efficacy and authority.

This article takes *charge* seriously as an analytic category and asks what happens when it is detached from the human-centered regimes that typically authorize reality. In Pagan ritual contexts, charge is often understood as something transmitted through intention, repetition, or embodied practice. However, as contemporary debates around artificial intelligence, authorship, and academic legitimacy increasingly reveal, modern institutions tend to recognize efficacy and legitimacy only when charge can be traced back to a clearly identifiable human subject. Knowledge, like ritual power, must be demonstrably human-charged to count as real.

This tension becomes particularly visible in the context of the Anthropocene, where agency is no longer easily confined to human actors alone. Ecological systems, technological infrastructures, and algorithmic processes routinely produce effects without clear human intentionality, challenging long-standing assumptions about authorship, responsibility, and legitimacy (Latour 2017; Bennett 2010). In this context, the question of who or what is permitted to “charge” reality becomes more than a metaphysical concern. It becomes a political and epistemological one.

This article argues that contemporary Pagan material practices offer a privileged site for examining these tensions because they operate outside centralized institutions of authorization while remaining deeply invested in questions of efficacy, legitimacy, and consequence. Rather than approaching Paganism as a belief system or identity category, the analysis focuses on material practices that foreground charge as a relational and situational condition. These practices do not ask to be believed. They ask to be reckoned with.

The central case study is *Wheels of Will*, an independent Pagan art project composed of ritual assemblages, charged objects, sealed bottles, and ephemeral installations. The project deliberately refuses explanatory discourse, institutional validation, and stable interpretive frames. Objects are presented not as artworks in the conventional sense, nor as ritual tools accompanied by doctrinal explanation, but as charged entities whose legitimacy is enacted through material persistence, containment, decay, and repetition. In doing so, *Wheels of Will* operates as a counter-model to dominant regimes of academic and artistic legitimacy, which privilege transparency, authorship, and discursive justification.

The concept of charge developed in this article draws on, but also departs from, existing theoretical frameworks. While ritual studies has long emphasized the role of materiality, repetition, and embodied practice in producing meaning (Bell 1992; Schechner 2003), charge has often been subsumed under broader discussions of symbolism or belief. Similarly, theories of biopower have examined how life is managed, optimized, and regulated through institutional frameworks (Foucault 1978), but have paid less attention to the ways in which objects themselves are rendered dangerous, contained, or excessive. By bringing these literatures into dialogue with contemporary Pagan material practices, this article reframes charge as a condition that operates prior to, and sometimes in defiance of, institutional authorization.

This reframing has direct implications for current debates about artificial intelligence and academic legitimacy. As AI-generated texts, images, and analyses proliferate, institutions have responded by reasserting the necessity of human authorship as the guarantor of meaning and value. Authorship statements, disclosure requirements, and originality checks function as mechanisms for ensuring that intellectual work remains properly human-charged. Yet this insistence reveals a historically contingent assumption: that legitimacy depends not on effects, coherence, or relational uptake, but on the presence of a recognizable human origin.

*Wheels of Will* exposes the fragility of this assumption. The project demonstrates that charge can be produced, sustained, and recognized without recourse to human testimony, authorial explanation, or institutional certification. Objects act not because they are believed in, but because they are arranged, contained, and allowed to persist. Their legitimacy is not declared. It emerges relationally.

The article proceeds in seven sections. Following this introduction, the literature review situates the concept of charge at the intersection of ritual studies, material religion, art theory, and science and technology studies, with particular attention to debates around agency, legitimacy, and non-human actors. The theoretical framework then develops charge as a relational condition distinct from belief, intention, or symbolic meaning, drawing on theories of biopower, assemblage, and material agency.

The methodology section outlines an analytic approach grounded in visual analysis, material semiotics, and reflexive autoethnography, treating *Wheels of Will* as both an artistic practice and an analytic intervention. The analysis section provides a close reading of selected objects and assemblages from the project, focusing on practices of containment, opacity, decay, and repetition. The findings section synthesizes these analyses to articulate how charge operates independently of institutional authorization and human-centered legitimacy regimes.

To situate this intervention, the article proceeds by first establishing the contemporary context in which questions of ritual efficacy and legitimacy have become especially unstable. Before turning to existing scholarship, it is necessary to clarify why contemporary Pagan material culture, and its circulation through digital media, constitutes a particularly revealing site for examining how charge is produced, recognized, and contested. The background section that follows does not provide a general account of Paganism, but rather situates *Wheels of Will* within a field where authority is decentralized, material practices are central, and recognition is increasingly mediated by visual circulation.

**Background and Context**

Contemporary Paganism provides a particularly revealing context for examining how legitimacy, efficacy, and recognition are negotiated in the absence of centralized authority, especially in traditions where material practice carries more weight than doctrinal consensus. Unlike institutionalized religions organized around formal doctrine, clergy, and canonical texts, most contemporary Pagan traditions operate through decentralized, loosely coordinated networks of practice. Authority is rarely vested in stable institutions and is instead distributed across individuals, small groups, and informal communities. As a result, questions of authenticity are not resolved through reference to orthodoxy but are continually renegotiated through practice, material engagement, and perceived efficacy.

This structural openness has long been noted as both a strength and a source of tension within Pagan communities. On the one hand, it enables experimentation, inclusivity, and adaptation to local contexts. On the other, it produces an enduring uncertainty about where boundaries lie between meaningful ritual practice, personal creativity, and aesthetic performance. Material culture occupies a central role in this uncertainty. Ritual tools, altars, offerings, and ceremonial objects are not merely accessories to belief but are often treated as primary sites where intention, efficacy, and legitimacy converge. Objects are expected to *do* something, even when what they do cannot be easily articulated or verified.

The rise of digital media has intensified these dynamics. Over the past decade, social media platforms have become key sites for the circulation of Pagan knowledge, ritual forms, and material culture. Images of sabbats, esbats, spellwork, and ritual tools proliferate across platforms, creating new modes of access and community formation. However, this visibility also accelerates the transformation of ritual practice into aesthetic content. Objects circulate as images detached from their contexts of use, and ritual labor is increasingly compressed into visually coherent displays. In such environments, legitimacy is inferred from circulation, coherence, and recognizability rather than from sustained engagement or material consequence.

This shift places particular pressure on Pagan material culture, where the absence of centralized authority already makes authenticity difficult to adjudicate. When nearly any object can be framed as a ritual tool and nearly any image can be read as evidence of practice, the distinction between efficacy and representation becomes unstable. The problem is not that ritual practices lose meaning, but that existing modes of recognition struggle to distinguish between objects that have been materially engaged and those that have been staged for visibility. The abundance of images does not clarify authenticity; it renders it increasingly undecidable.

*Wheels of Will* emerges directly from this context. The project does not seek to resolve debates about what constitutes “real” Pagan practice, nor does it attempt to recover an imagined pre-digital authenticity. Instead, it treats contemporary Paganism as a diagnostic field in which broader transformations of legitimacy can be observed with particular clarity. The project takes seriously the idea that ritual efficacy cannot be assumed to be visually legible and that demands for immediate recognition may themselves distort the conditions under which charge is produced.

*Wheels of Will* is not a gallery-based exhibition nor a publicly staged ritual series. The project exists as a privately produced material practice whose primary mode of circulation is photographic documentation. Objects and assemblages are fabricated, used, and allowed to persist or decay outside of public ritual settings, and are subsequently photographed in controlled conditions. The images circulate selectively through digital platforms rather than institutional art spaces, without accompanying explanatory text, curatorial framing, or claims of ritual authenticity. This mode of circulation is integral to the project’s analytic function, as it situates charged objects within regimes of visibility that demand legibility while withholding the contextual cues typically used to authorize meaning or efficacy.

By organizing its inquiry around the Pagan Wheel of the Year and lunar cycles, *Wheels of Will* situates itself within widely shared temporal frameworks without claiming historical continuity or doctrinal authority. The use of familiar ritual materials such as fruit, wax, glass, herbs, and metal anchors the project in recognizable practices, while the photographic presentation isolates these materials from communal settings, narratives of belief, and explanatory discourse. This isolation is not an abstraction but a methodological choice designed to foreground the problem of recognition itself.

The project’s relevance extends beyond Paganism. The instability of legitimacy observed in digitally mediated Pagan practices mirrors parallel dynamics in other domains where authority is decentralized and circulation is rapid. In contemporary art, institutional validation increasingly determines whether practices grounded in process, materiality, or ephemerality are recognized as meaningful. In academic knowledge production, legitimacy is secured through procedural markers that certify authorship, originality, and methodological compliance, even as knowledge itself circulates across distributed networks of human and non-human actors. In each case, recognition depends less on efficacy than on conformity to established regimes of authorization. In this sense, the instability of recognition in contemporary Paganism is not anomalous, but anticipatory of broader transformations affecting artistic and academic fields alike.

Situating *Wheels of Will* within contemporary Paganism thus serves an analytic rather than descriptive purpose. Paganism is not treated as an exceptional or marginal case, but as a field in which the fragility of legitimacy is rendered especially visible because institutional safeguards are minimal and material practices are central. The project leverages this visibility to interrogate how charge is recognized, misrecognized, or ignored when it no longer aligns with dominant expectations of transparency, authorship, and control.

This background provides the necessary context for understanding why *Wheels of Will* functions as a counter-model to institutional legitimacy. By foregrounding material persistence, containment, and decay without offering interpretive guarantees, the project exposes the assumptions that govern how authenticity is inferred in contemporary knowledge cultures. In doing so, it prepares the ground for the theoretical and analytic sections that follow, which examine charge as a relational condition operating across ritual, artistic, and academic domains.

**Literature Review**

The instability of legitimacy described in the preceding section has been addressed across multiple bodies of scholarship, though rarely under a shared analytic vocabulary. Research in ritual studies, material religion, art theory, and science and technology studies has converged on the insight that efficacy, meaning, and authority are not intrinsic properties of objects or practices, but are produced through relational, procedural, and institutional processes (Bell 1992; Latour 2005; Meyer et al. 2010; Morgan 2010; Bennett 2010; Malafouris 2013). Within these literatures, attention has increasingly shifted toward how material arrangements, mediation, and circulation shape what comes to count as real, effective, or legitimate (Bishop 2012; Groys 2008; Campbell 2013). What remains less clearly articulated, however, is how these processes operate when recognition itself becomes unstable, contested, or saturated by visual and discursive abundance, particularly in contexts where institutional authority is weak or deliberately refused.

This material turn has been particularly influential in rethinking how objects function within religious and ritual contexts. Rather than treating ritual artifacts as symbols to be decoded, researchers increasingly approach them as mediators that enable, constrain, and transform action. Patera has shown that ritual objects operate as “material supports” that make ritual practice possible by stabilizing relations between bodies, gestures, and temporal sequences, rather than by representing abstract meanings (Patera 2012). Archaeological and anthropological studies similarly emphasize that objects should be understood as possessing a form of agency derived from their placement within assemblages, practices, and histories of use (Knappett and Malafouris 2008; Malafouris 2013). These perspectives complicate modern distinctions between subject and object by highlighting how agency emerges relationally through material engagement.

Within the study of contemporary Paganism, questions of authenticity, authority, and material culture have long been contested. Scholars have noted that Pagan traditions are characterized by decentralized authority structures, creative adaptation, and an emphasis on personal experience over doctrinal coherence (Hutton 1999; Berger, Leach, and Shaffer 2003). This openness has allowed Paganism to flourish in diverse cultural contexts, but it has also intensified debates over what constitutes legitimate practice. Material culture plays a central role in these debates, as ritual tools, altars, and ceremonial objects become sites where authenticity is negotiated rather than prescribed (Magliocco 2004; Pike 2001). Importantly, authenticity in Pagan contexts is rarely enforced through institutional mechanisms; it is inferred through perceived efficacy, consistency of practice, and relational recognition within communities.

The rise of digital media has further transformed these dynamics. Research on digital religion and online Paganism demonstrates how social media platforms have reshaped religious authority by privileging visibility, aesthetic coherence, and circulation over apprenticeship, lineage, or sustained practice (Campbell 2013; Klassen 2002). Visual abundance allows ritual forms to circulate rapidly, but it also risks flattening distinctions between practice, performance, and commodification. Scholars have noted that online environments tend to collapse ritual labor into consumable imagery, raising concerns about the displacement of embodied practice by representation (Cheong et al. 2012). These tensions are especially acute in Pagan communities, where ritual authority is already loosely defined and where material artifacts circulate freely as both spiritual tools and market commodities.

Parallel concerns have emerged in art theory, particularly in discussions of process-based, ephemeral, and practice-led art. Contemporary art scholarship has increasingly interrogated how artworks acquire legitimacy through institutional framing, documentation, and discourse rather than through intrinsic qualities (Bishop 2012; Groys 2008). Practice-based and socially engaged art forms often resist traditional modes of validation by privileging process, temporality, and relational effects over finished objects. However, critics have observed that such practices remain vulnerable to reabsorption into institutional economies that demand legibility, authorship, and explanatory narratives (Bourriaud 2002). This tension between refusal and recuperation mirrors broader questions about how legitimacy is conferred in knowledge-producing systems.

These debates intersect productively with theories of biopower and posthuman agency. Foucault’s account of biopower highlights how modern institutions govern life by regulating bodies, circulation, and risk, producing norms of legitimacy tied to health, productivity, and visibility (Foucault 1978). Building on this framework, scholars have examined how legitimacy increasingly depends on traceable human agency and accountability, particularly in scientific and academic knowledge production. At the same time, new materialist and posthuman theorists have challenged human exceptionalism by emphasizing the distributed agency of objects, technologies, and ecological systems (Bennett 2010; Latour 2005). These perspectives destabilize the assumption that meaningful action must originate from a sovereign human subject.

Recent anxieties surrounding artificial intelligence have intensified these debates. As AI-generated texts and images proliferate, academic and cultural institutions have responded by reasserting the primacy of human authorship as a guarantor of legitimacy. Disclosure requirements, originality checks, and authorship declarations function as mechanisms to ensure that knowledge remains properly human-charged. Scholars have noted that these responses reveal less about epistemic rigor than about deeply embedded norms concerning agency, responsibility, and value (Birhane et al. 2021; Crawford 2021). The insistence on human origin reflects a historically specific configuration of legitimacy rather than a universal condition of knowledge.

Taken together, these bodies of scholarship converge on a shared insight: legitimacy is not inherent in objects, practices, or texts, but is produced through relational, procedural, and institutional processes. What remains under-theorized, however, is the concept of *charge* as a distinct analytic that cuts across ritual efficacy, material agency, artistic practice, and academic legitimacy. By examining *Wheels of Will* as a Pagan art project that deliberately withholds explanatory authority while foregrounding material persistence, containment, and decay, this article contributes to ongoing debates about how legitimacy is recognized and contested in contemporary knowledge cultures.

**Theoretical Framework**

This article approaches *charge* as a relational condition through which objects, practices, and forms of knowledge acquire efficacy and legitimacy. Rather than treating charge as a metaphor or as a residual category of belief, the framework developed here understands charge as a procedural accomplishment, emerging from the alignment of material arrangements, temporal persistence, and regimes of recognition. Charge is not a property that resides in objects, nor an intention that originates in subjects. It is the effect of sustained relations that authorize certain entities to act, endure, and matter.

The term *charge* also carries an emic resonance within Wiccan traditions, most notably through the ritual text commonly known as the *Charge of the Goddess*, which functions as an invocatory utterance spoken in the voice of a divine figure. As Ronald Hutton has observed, the text operates less as a doctrinal statement than as a performative liturgical address, authorizing ritual authority through voiced identification with the Goddess rather than through formal theology, a form that became central to modern Wiccan practice in the mid-twentieth century (Hutton 2017). While this text has played an important role in shaping modern Pagan imaginaries of authority, embodiment, and transmission, the analytic use of *charge* developed in this article deliberately departs from that tradition. Rather than treating charge as something spoken, invoked, or personified, the present framework approaches charge as infrastructural rather than declarative, emerging from material arrangements, temporal persistence, and relational constraint. This shift is not a rejection of Wiccan ritual language, but an analytic repositioning that allows charge to be examined independently of voice, belief, or authorial origin, including divine or human. This distinction allows charge to be analyzed without privileging speech, belief, or identifiable authorship as the primary site of religious efficacy.

This conception draws from, but also extends, ritual theory’s emphasis on efficacy as practice rather than belief. Ritual action has long been understood as producing effects through repetition, formalization, and socially recognized sequences rather than through internal conviction alone (Bell 1992; Schechner 2003). However, the language of ritual efficacy often presumes a relatively stable framework of recognition, whether communal, cosmological, or institutional. By contrast, charge as developed here foregrounds situations in which recognition is unstable, contested, or deliberately withheld. In such contexts, efficacy does not disappear; it becomes opaque, deferred, or redistributed.

Material religion and new materialist approaches provide a crucial bridge for this reconceptualization. Scholars working on material agency argue that objects participate in action by virtue of their placement within assemblages, their affordances, and their histories of use (Latour 2005; Bennett 2010; Malafouris 2013). These perspectives reject the sharp separation between active subjects and passive objects, emphasizing instead how agency emerges through relational configurations. Charge, in this sense, can be understood as the condition under which objects are granted or denied participation in these configurations. An object is charged not because it symbolizes power, but because it is allowed to *do* something, even if that doing remains ambiguous or contested.

This framework also engages critically with theories of biopower and legitimacy. Biopower describes how modern institutions regulate life through norms of visibility, health, productivity, and circulation (Foucault 1978). Within academic and scientific contexts, legitimacy is similarly governed through mechanisms that privilege traceability, transparency, and human accountability. Authorship, peer review, and methodological disclosure function as technologies that certify intellectual work as properly human-originated and therefore trustworthy. Charge, within this regime, is tightly coupled to recognizable human agency.

Yet this coupling is historically contingent rather than ontologically necessary. Posthuman and STS scholars have demonstrated that many contemporary effects are produced by distributed systems that exceed individual human intention, including technological infrastructures, ecological processes, and algorithmic operations (Latour 2017; Haraway 2016). The insistence that legitimacy must be grounded in singular human authorship reflects an institutional anxiety about losing control over authorization rather than a principled epistemic requirement. Charge becomes a gatekeeping mechanism: only that which can be demonstrably human-charged is permitted to count as real knowledge.

The concept of charge developed here allows these dynamics to be analyzed without collapsing ritual practice, artistic production, and academic knowledge into a single register. In Pagan ritual contexts, charge is often described as something cultivated through attention, repetition, and material engagement, yet it does not require institutional certification to function. Its efficacy is inferred through aftermath, persistence, and relational uptake rather than through declarative proof. In contrast, academic legitimacy demands explicit authorization, even when the effects of knowledge production operate independently of authorial intention. This asymmetry reveals that charge and authorization are not synonymous. Charge can exist without authorization, and authorization can be conferred in the absence of meaningful charge.

The framework therefore distinguishes between *charged efficacy* and *authorized legitimacy*. Charged efficacy refers to the capacity of an object or practice to produce effects within a relational field, regardless of whether those effects are immediately recognizable or institutionally sanctioned. Authorized legitimacy refers to the recognition granted by formal systems that certify value, truth, or authenticity. While the two often overlap, they can also diverge sharply. *Wheels of Will* occupies precisely this zone of divergence, staging charged objects that refuse authorization and authorized readings that fail to secure recognition.

This distinction becomes particularly salient in the context of contemporary debates around artificial intelligence. As AI-generated texts and images increasingly resemble human-produced outputs, institutions respond by reasserting the necessity of human charge as a condition of legitimacy. Disclosure policies and authorship requirements attempt to stabilize recognition by anchoring it to human origin. However, such measures do not address whether the work in question produces meaningful, coherent, or consequential effects. They merely reassert a normative boundary around who is permitted to charge reality.

By conceptualizing charge as relational and procedural rather than human-centered, this framework provides a way to analyze legitimacy without defaulting to origin-based criteria. It allows for a comparative examination of how charge is cultivated, withheld, misrecognized, or contested across ritual practice, artistic production, and academic knowledge. In doing so, it reframes contemporary anxieties about AI, authenticity, and authority as symptoms of a broader crisis of recognition in which established regimes of legitimacy struggle to accommodate distributed agency.

This theoretical framework guides the analysis that follows by foregrounding charge as the key site where materiality, agency, and legitimacy intersect. Rather than asking whether objects, artworks, or texts are genuinely powerful or authentically human, the analysis asks how charge is produced, how it is made visible or invisible, and how institutional demands for authorization shape what is allowed to count as real.

**Methodology**

This study adopts a qualitative, interpretive methodology that combines visual analysis, material semiotics, and reflexive practice-based inquiry. Rather than treating *Wheels of Will* as an object of external observation or as a purely subjective artistic expression, the methodology positions the project as a site where material practices generate analytic insight. This approach is grounded in the premise that artistic production can function as a form of knowledge-making when its procedures, constraints, and effects are examined critically rather than treated as self-evident (Borgdorff 2012; Sullivan 2010).

The primary empirical material for this study consists of a corpus of photographic images documenting ritual assemblages and charged objects produced as part of *Wheels of Will*. These images are analyzed not as aesthetic representations but as records of material configurations, temporal processes, and relational effects. Visual analysis focuses on compositional features, material degradation, containment practices, serial repetition, and the absence of explanatory cues. Attention is paid to what the images make visible, what they obscure, and how they structure the conditions under which recognition becomes possible or impossible (Rose 2016; Morgan 2010).

Material semiotics informs the analysis by treating objects not as inert carriers of meaning but as participants in relational networks. This perspective allows the study to examine how charge is enacted through material arrangements, residues, and constraints, rather than inferred through declared intention or symbolic interpretation. Objects are approached as sites where agency is distributed across materials, temporal processes, and contextual relations, without presuming stable origin or authorship as the primary explanatory factor (Latour 2005; Law 2009; Bennett 2010).

The methodology is also informed by practice-based research, in which the act of making is understood as analytically generative. *Wheels of Will* was developed through iterative cycles of fabrication, ritual use, photographic documentation, and selective withholding of contextual information. These procedural decisions are treated as methodological interventions rather than aesthetic preferences. In particular, the deliberate refusal to disclose which objects were used in ritual contexts functions as a controlled analytic constraint designed to test the limits of visual recognition and legitimacy. This refusal is not meant to obscure data but to expose the assumptions that govern how efficacy and authenticity are inferred (Haseman 2006; Borgdorff 2012).

Reflexivity is incorporated without centering personal narrative as evidence. While the project emerges from sustained engagement with contemporary Pagan practices and material culture, the analysis does not rely on autobiographical authority or insider testimony to establish validity. Instead, reflexivity operates at the level of methodological positioning, acknowledging the researcher’s dual role as practitioner and analyst while maintaining analytic distance from claims of belief, sincerity, or experiential truth. This stance aligns with critical approaches that treat reflexivity as a condition of analytic responsibility rather than a substitute for evidence (Finlay 2002; Pillow 2003).

Ethical considerations are addressed through the exclusive use of self-produced materials and the avoidance of identifiable human subjects. No external participants were photographed, interviewed, or observed. The project does not document live ritual events involving others, nor does it disclose private or community-specific practices. This approach minimizes ethical risk while allowing for sustained analysis of material processes without requiring institutional review procedures designed for human-subject research (Israel and Hay 2006).

The methodological aim of this study is not to evaluate the truth or efficacy of Pagan rituals, nor to adjudicate the authenticity of specific practices. Instead, it seeks to examine how charge is produced, withheld, and misrecognized under conditions of visual saturation and institutional demand for legitimacy. By combining visual analysis, material semiotics, and practice-based inquiry, the methodology enables a comparative examination of ritual objects, artistic practice, and academic knowledge production as parallel sites where legitimacy is negotiated rather than given.

This methodological framework supports the subsequent analysis by allowing *Wheels of Will* to function simultaneously as empirical material and analytic intervention. The findings that emerge are not generalized claims about Paganism or art, but theoretically grounded insights into how charge operates as a condition of recognition across ritual, artistic, and academic domains.

**Analysis**

The analysis that follows treats *Wheels of Wil*l not as an artwork to be interpreted, but as a material practice whose arrangements make visible the conditions under which charge becomes recognizable or foreclosed.

Viewed as a corpus rather than as isolated images, *Wheels of Will* establishes a visual and material grammar that consistently frustrates the recognition of ritual efficacy. This corpus-based reading follows directly from the methodological choice to treat the images not as representations but as records of material configurations and temporal processes (Morgan 2010; Rose 2016). Across the series, objects are presented in states of use, decay, containment, or partial collapse, yet never accompanied by explanatory cues that would allow viewers to stabilize their interpretation. This refusal is not incidental. It is the central analytic force of the project. The images do not ask whether ritual charge exists; they ask whether contemporary regimes of visibility still permit charge to be recognized at all.

One of the most persistent features across the images is the normalization of aftermath. Melted wax, dehydrated fruit, sedimented liquids, wilted plant matter, and stained surfaces appear repeatedly, establishing degradation as the baseline rather than the exception. This emphasis on material residue aligns with the theoretical framing of charge as a relational and procedural condition rather than a symbolic one. Charge is inferred not through declared intention but through material consequence and persistence over time (Bell 1992; Bennett 2010). This choice also directly counters the dominant visual economy of digitally mediated Paganism, in which ritual tools are typically displayed in pristine, symmetrical, and market-ready forms. In *Wheels of Will*, objects appear after expenditure. They are not optimized for circulation or longevity. Their material condition insists that something has already happened, even as the nature of that happening remains undecidable.

This undecidability is intensified through serial repetition. The same artifacts reappear in multiple photographic arrangements, sometimes ordered, sometimes unstable, sometimes bordering on collapse. In methodological terms, this repetition functions as a controlled variation rather than as narrative documentation. The viewer is offered no temporal sequence that would allow them to reconstruct a linear ritual arc of preparation, performance, and resolution. Instead, the images oscillate between control and entropy. Charge does not accumulate toward a climax, nor does it dissipate cleanly. It fluctuates. This oscillation directly challenges a shared assumption across ritual pedagogy and academic expertise: that efficacy becomes more legible with increased mastery, repetition, or explanation (Schechner 2003).

The sealed bottles introduced in the later images sharpen this tension by foregrounding containment as a material practice. Unlike the open assemblages of fruit, flowers, and candles, these objects emphasize restriction rather than circulation. Glass vessels are tightly corked, bound with wire or thread, sealed with wax, and filled with opaque or sedimented substances. From the perspective of material semiotics, these gestures redistribute agency by constraining potential action rather than enabling it (Latour 2005; Law 2009). Charge here is not something that flows freely; it is something that must be held, compressed, or prevented from escaping. This introduces what can be described as a politics of charge, in which efficacy is treated as potentially dangerous, volatile, or excessive rather than as inherently generative.

Crucially, these bottles are visually overdetermined without being interpretable. Their density of material cues suggests seriousness, deliberation, and intention, yet none of these qualities can be translated into a stable claim of authenticity. The viewer cannot determine what the bottles contain, how they were used, or whether they were used at all. This tension mirrors the institutional demand for transparency and traceability that structures contemporary regimes of legitimacy in both academic and artistic fields. As discussed in the background section, such demands presuppose that legitimacy must be demonstrable through inspection, documentation, or disclosure. *Wheels of Will* deliberately withholds these guarantees, exposing the assumptions that underpin recognition itself.

Across the corpus, the human subject is conspicuously absent. No hands appear, no bodies perform, no faces authorize. Human presence is registered only through trace: residue, binding, arrangement, and wear. This absence operationalizes the theoretical distinction between charged efficacy and authorized legitimacy developed earlier. Charge, if it exists, is not anchored in testimony, belief, or personal narrative. It survives only as material consequence. By refusing to center human intention, the project severs the link between legitimacy and declared authorship, a link that underpins both ritual authority and academic knowledge production.

The images also refuse symbolic closure. While many of the objects are recognizable within contemporary Pagan material culture, they are not arranged to communicate fixed meanings. Apple skulls do not resolve into allegories of death or harvest. Bottles do not stabilize as symbols of protection or banishment. Instead, the objects hover between familiarity and opacity. This refusal prevents the viewer from resolving uncertainty through cultural literacy alone. Knowledge does not grant access to recognition. Familiarity does not restore legibility. In this sense, the project enacts the methodological refusal described earlier, using non-disclosure as an analytic constraint rather than as an aesthetic gesture.

Taken together, these strategies produce a sustained breakdown of visual certainty. After prolonged engagement with the series, it becomes impossible to identify reliable markers that distinguish ritual use from staging, efficacy from display, or charge from aesthetic coherence. This breakdown is not a failure of representation. It is the project’s central analytic result. *Wheels of Will* demonstrates that under conditions of visual saturation, legitimacy is no longer inferred from material consequence but from alignment with expected forms of readability, circulation, and authorization.

This insight extends beyond Paganism. As outlined in the theoretical framework, the difficulty of recognizing charge in these images parallels the difficulty institutions face when confronted with forms of agency that do not conform to established authorization regimes. In academic contexts, legitimacy is secured through authorship, method, disclosure, and peer review, all of which function as technologies for certifying human charge. When those markers are absent or ambiguous, suspicion follows, even if the work produces coherent or consequential effects. The anxiety provoked by AI-generated knowledge follows a similar pattern. The question is not whether such work functions, but whether it can be anchored to a recognizable human origin.

*Wheels of Will* exposes this anchoring as a contingent requirement rather than an epistemic necessity. By aligning material practice, methodological refusal, and theoretical critique, the project shows that charge can persist without explanation, authorization, or narrative stabilization. Its objects do not become meaningful because they are believed in or validated, but because they endure, resist, and organize attention over time. In doing so, the analysis reveals a structural homology between ritual objects, artistic practices, and academic knowledge. In each case, legitimacy depends less on efficacy than on the availability of recognizable markers that certify charge. The question the project ultimately poses is not whether charge is real, but whether contemporary systems of evaluation remain capable of perceiving it when it no longer announces itself in familiar ways.

**Findings**

The analysis of *Wheels of Will* yields five interrelated findings concerning the operation of charge, legitimacy, and recognition in contemporary ritual, artistic, and knowledge-producing contexts.

First, the analysis demonstrates that charge is not visually legible under conditions of image saturation. Across the corpus of photographs, no stable visual marker reliably distinguishes ritual use from aesthetic staging. Material degradation, residue, and containment appear in both contexts, rendering efficacy undecidable at the level of representation. This finding challenges the assumption, prevalent in both digital Pagan cultures and institutional knowledge systems, that legitimacy can be inferred from surface coherence or recognizable signs of seriousness.

Second, the findings show that charge persists as material consequence rather than symbolic presence. In *Wheels of Will*, charge does not manifest as aura, transcendence, or explicit meaning. It appears instead as aftermath: collapsed fruit, pooled wax, sedimented liquids, bound containers. These material conditions indicate that something has taken place without revealing what that something was. Charge thus operates as trace rather than declaration, resisting interpretive closure while maintaining persistence.

Third, the analysis establishes a structural distinction between charged efficacy and authorized legitimacy. The objects documented in *Wheels of Will* exhibit material density, deliberation, and constraint without being accompanied by institutional validation, explanatory discourse, or testimonial authority. This separation reveals that efficacy does not require authorization to function, even though authorization is often demanded for recognition. Conversely, authorization can be conferred in the absence of meaningful charge, as long as recognizable legitimating procedures are followed.

Fourth, the findings indicate that human-centered authorization is a contingent, not necessary, condition of legitimacy. The absence of visible human presence, authorial explanation, or declared intention in the images does not neutralize the objects’ capacity to organize attention, provoke scrutiny, or endure over time. This suggests that the insistence on human origin as the guarantor of legitimacy reflects institutional norms rather than ontological requirements. Charge can be distributed across material arrangements and temporal processes without being anchored to a sovereign human subject.

Finally, the analysis reveals that recognition has become the primary site of crisis, rather than efficacy itself. The difficulty posed by *Wheels of Will* is not that charge fails to exist, but that prevailing regimes of evaluation lack the tools to perceive it when it does not conform to established markers of legitimacy. This misalignment produces anxiety, suspicion, and demands for transparency that function as defensive responses to the erosion of human-centered control over authorization.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that *Wheels of Will* operates as more than a commentary on Pagan material culture. It functions as an empirical counter-model that exposes how legitimacy is produced, withheld, and misrecognized across ritual practice, artistic production, and academic knowledge. By foregrounding charge as a relational and procedural condition rather than a human-owned property, the project reveals the limitations of recognition systems that equate reality with authorized origin rather than with sustained material and relational effects.

**Discussion and Conclusion**

The findings presented in this article invite a reconsideration of how legitimacy is produced and recognized across ritual practice, artistic production, and academic knowledge. *Wheels of Will* demonstrates that the difficulty is not the absence of charge but the erosion of shared criteria for recognizing it. Under conditions of visual saturation, market circulation, and institutional pressure for transparency, charge no longer announces itself in stable or legible ways. What emerges instead is a gap between efficacy and recognition, a gap that contemporary regimes of legitimacy struggle to address.

This gap is particularly visible when Pagan material practices are placed in dialogue with academic knowledge production. In both domains, legitimacy is increasingly tied to formalized procedures that certify origin, intention, and accountability. In Pagan contexts, these procedures are largely informal and decentralized, relying on relational recognition and perceived efficacy rather than institutional authorization. In academic contexts, they are codified through authorship, peer review, disclosure, and methodological transparency. Despite their differences, both systems depend on recognizable markers that allow observers to decide what counts as real, effective, or trustworthy.

*Wheels of Will* exposes the fragility of these markers. By withholding explanatory testimony and refusing to distinguish visually between staged and ritual use, the project reveals how quickly recognition collapses when familiar cues are removed. This collapse does not indicate a failure of practice or meaning. It indicates a failure of evaluative frameworks that conflate legitimacy with legibility. When recognition depends on immediate readability, anything that resists inspection, narration, or certification is treated as suspect, regardless of its material or relational effects.

This insight has direct implications for contemporary debates surrounding artificial intelligence and academic authorship. Institutional responses to AI-generated knowledge frequently emphasize the necessity of human origin as the guarantor of legitimacy. Disclosure policies and authorship requirements are framed as ethical safeguards, yet they also function as mechanisms for preserving human-centered control over charge. The concern is not solely that AI might produce error or deception, but that it destabilizes the assumption that meaningful knowledge must be traceable to a human interiority. As *Wheels of Will* suggests, this assumption is neither timeless nor universal. It reflects a historically specific configuration of legitimacy that prioritizes authorization over consequence.

At the scale of the Anthropocene, this configuration becomes increasingly untenable. Ecological systems, technological infrastructures, and algorithmic processes generate effects that exceed individual intention and resist straightforward attribution. Insisting on singular human charge as the condition of legitimacy risks misrecognizing the very forms of agency that now shape social, environmental, and epistemic realities. The problem, then, is not that non-human agents act, but that existing regimes of recognition are poorly equipped to account for distributed, opaque, and deferred forms of efficacy.

*Wheels of Will* does not offer a solution to this problem, nor does it propose an alternative system of authorization. Its contribution lies in making the limits of recognition visible. By foregrounding material persistence, containment, and decay, the project demonstrates that charge can operate without explanation, validation, or human-centered certification. Objects act not because they are believed in or endorsed, but because they are allowed to endure and to organize relations over time. Legitimacy, in this sense, emerges not from origin purity but from sustained relational effects.

This reframing does not diminish the importance of ethical accountability, nor does it suggest that all claims should be treated as equally valid. Rather, it calls for a more careful distinction between authorization and efficacy, between recognition and consequence. In academic contexts, this distinction opens space for rethinking how knowledge is evaluated when authorship, intention, and agency are no longer singular or fully transparent. In artistic and ritual contexts, it challenges the demand that practices justify themselves through immediate legibility or market compatibility.

The analysis above has treated charge as relational, procedural, and distributed. Yet to leave the concept at this structural level would be to miss how it is encountered in practice. Charge is not experienced as theory. It is experienced as intensification.

Practitioners often describe a moment — sometimes subtle, sometimes unmistakable — in which an arrangement of materials ceases to feel merely arranged. After repetition, containment, or temporal persistence, something shifts. The object does not change substance, yet it acquires density. Attention sharpens. The surrounding space feels slightly reconfigured. This is not necessarily belief, nor is it symbolic projection. It is the felt convergence of intention, constraint, and material configuration.

In such moments, efficacy is encountered not as external validation but as coherence. The practitioner does not experience themselves as commanding an object from outside; rather, they experience themselves as aligned with it. The relation stabilizes. What was potential becomes actualized in form. Charge, in this sense, names the threshold at which material arrangement becomes consequential — the moment when existence feels beckoned into persistence.

This phenomenological dimension complicates the earlier distinction between human and non-human agency. Charge need not originate in a sovereign subject, yet it is encountered through embodied perception. The crisis of legitimacy described throughout this article is therefore not only institutional but experiential. When evaluative regimes demand traceable origin rather than relational coherence, they obscure the very condition through which charge becomes perceptible.

To recognize charge is not to verify authorship. It is to attend to the moment when alignment holds. Under conditions of saturation and suspicion, such moments are easily dismissed or misrecognized. Yet they persist. They endure wherever material arrangement, temporal persistence, and embodied attention converge.

In conclusion, this article has argued that charge operates as a relational and procedural condition that precedes and exceeds institutional authorization. Through the case study of *Wheels of Will*, it has shown how contemporary regimes of legitimacy struggle to recognize charge when it is no longer anchored in stable human origins or recognizable visual cues. Rather than resolving this tension, the article positions it as a defining feature of contemporary knowledge cultures. The task ahead is not to restore certainty or to reassert human exclusivity, but to develop evaluative practices capable of engaging with forms of agency and efficacy that no longer conform to inherited models of recognition.
