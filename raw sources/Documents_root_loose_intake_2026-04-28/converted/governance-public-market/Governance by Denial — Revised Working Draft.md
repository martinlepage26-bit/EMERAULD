Governance by Denial: How Legibility, Capacity,and Classification Rewire Accountability

Abstract

Democratic hollowing often advances without spectacular rupture. Elections continue, courts sit, oversight bodies multiply, and constitutional language remains in place, yet the conversion of evidence into consequence weakens. This article argues that such hollowing is best understood as a reorganization of the governance of public reality: the patterned capacity to shape what counts as credible, actionable, and sanctionable while democratic form remains formally intact. I specify a coupled mechanism family composed of narrative control, impunity signaling, and weak accountability loops, and I show how legibility, capacity, and classification operate as their principal governance levers. Narrative control governs intelligibility by shaping inference rules and evidentiary standing. Impunity signaling recalibrates expectations of enforcement through patterned nonconsequence, selective sanction, and strategic silence. Weak accountability loops routinize procedural response while leaving incentive structures largely unchanged. Using a theory-driven comparative design oriented toward causal process tracing across the United States, Canada, and Europe, with Israel–Gaza as a bounded stress test, the paper shows how legality and publicity can coexist with declining accountability. Conspiratorial interpretation, file politics, platform tempo, generative-AI-related provenance stress, and asymmetries of administrative burden function here as intensifiers rather than independent causes. The article contributes a portable indicator scheme for diagnosing hollowing in information-rich environments and argues that democratic repair requires more than transparency: it requires institutions able to convert legibility into enforceable consequence within politically meaningful time.

Introduction

Democratic hollowing often proceeds through legality, publicity, and tempo rather than through overt institutional rupture. Elections continue, courts remain open, oversight bodies issue reports, and constitutions continue to speak in the language of restraint. Yet these visible democratic forms can coexist with a quieter reorganization of what institutions are actually able to recognize, process, and sanction. This article argues that hollowing is best understood not primarily as institutional disappearance, but as a transformation in the governance of public reality: the patterned power to shape what becomes credible, governable, and sanctionable while formal democratic architecture remains in place.

The argument begins from a relational understanding of power. Power is not treated here as a possession of offices, leaders, or regimes, but as an effect produced through social relations that structure capacity, expectation, and constraint (Barnett and Duvall, 2005: 39–42). On this view, democratic durability cannot be inferred from constitutional design or from the continued visibility of institutional rituals alone. It must be traced through the conditions under which evidence stabilizes, harms become legible, oversight acquires consequence, and sanctions still bind within a politically meaningful tempo. Hollowing names a situation in which these binding conditions are reorganized upstream of decision making, such that legality and procedure persist while their constraining force weakens.

The paper’s title terms name the principal governance levers through which this reorganization occurs. Legibility refers to the conditions under which claims, harms, and actors become institutionally actionable, including recognized forms of proof, standing, evidentiary thresholds, and authorized interpretive positions. Capacity refers to what institutions can actually do in time, including enforcement bandwidth, administrative burden, investigatory tempo, and the costs of converting visibility into remedy. Classification refers to the categories of risk, threat, belonging, and credibility through which persons and claims are pre-sorted before any apparently neutral adjudication begins. These are not background conditions. They are the terrain through which democratic constraint is either reproduced or hollowed out. Legibility, moreover, is not only evidentiary or bureaucratic. It is often aesthetic and communicative as well. Institutions govern credibility through styles of composure, fluency, tone, and presentational order, deciding not only which claims count, but which forms of saying are likely to be heard as serious, governable, and sanctionable in the first place.

To make this terrain analytically tractable, the paper specifies a coupled mechanism family composed of narrative control, impunity signaling, and weak accountability loops. Narrative control governs intelligibility by shaping who counts as credible, what counts as evidence, and which inferences become socially reasonable or institutionally admissible (Benford and Snow, 2000: 614). Impunity signaling recalibrates expectations of enforcement by teaching publics and institutions which violations will not reliably trigger consequence, often through patterned nonconsequence, selective sanction, and strategic silence (Maor, 2016: 2). Weak accountability loops stabilize these lessons by routing harm through procedural repetition that preserves legitimacy while leaving incentive structures largely intact. Within this model, power does not work only by suppressing facts. It works by reorganizing the conditions under which facts can acquire institutional weight at all.

This framework matters because several contemporary developments intensify the upstream struggle over plausibility and consequence. One is the normalization of conspiratorial interpretation as an inferential style. Conspiracy frames matter here not simply because they are factually dubious or politically extreme, but because they function as shortcuts of adjudication. They collapse uncertainty into intention, convert skepticism into identity work, and can satisfy epistemic, existential, and social motives at once, which makes them especially attractive under stress and polarization (Douglas, Sutton and Cichocka, 2017: 539). Douglas, Sutton and Cichocka further emphasize that conspiracy theories are often simultaneously "speculative", "complex", and "resistant to falsification" (2017: 538). That combination is politically important because it helps explain why conspiracy does not only misdescribe events; it can also reorganize the standards by which later evidence will be judged. Another is the rise of file politics: leak cycles, dossier-like releases, scandal archives, and document dumps that circulate as if their publication should settle disputes. The problem is that files rarely arrive as self-interpreting truth. Their meaning is fought over through timing, classification, interpretive authorization, and procedural gating (Fenster, 2014: 309; Paquin, 2025: 2).

Platform tempo and generative AI intensify these dynamics further by altering the ratio between production and verification. Information disorder research has shown that contemporary democratic strain is not reducible to false content alone, but involves the entire chain of creation, production, and distribution, as well as the identities and incentives through which messages are taken up (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 12–13, 28–29). Generative AI amplifies these pressures by lowering the cost of producing fluent and persuasive artifacts while raising the burden of verification and complicating provenance (Fisher, Howard and Kira, 2024: 2–3). In such conditions, contested authenticity becomes routine. The crucial issue is no longer only whether information exists, but whether a polity can still decide what counts as settled enough for action.

These pressures are not confined to spectacular media events. They increasingly appear in automated and semi-automated administrative settings as well, where systems classify, rank, score, and route people before any final human decision is formally made. That development matters because it reveals a larger governance problem. Debates over meaningful human control often focus on whether a human remains somewhere in the loop. The harder question is whether the human or institution that remains is still given a field that is legible enough, timely enough, and challengeable enough for judgment to be more than ceremonial. Put differently, the problem is not only automation. It is the upstream organization of what a later human actor is allowed to see, interpret, and sanction.

A further micro-form of narrative control becomes especially salient in this setting. I code it here as apocalyptic compliance framing: a recurrent package of false precision, binary compression, personification of systems, and immunization against doubt. Its political effect is to convert uncertainty into urgency and urgency into deference. It narrows the perceived option set and reclassifies verification as harmful delay. This matters because democratic hollowing does not always present itself as indifference. It can also appear as excessive urgency, overconfident certainty, and moralized compression that makes procedural caution look illegitimate (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 28–29; Roberts, 2014: 13).

The paper does not claim that all controversy is narrative control, that all delay is hollowing, or that any presence of informational conflict is itself diagnostic. The mechanism family is asserted only where observable indicators converge and where relevant counterindicators are absent, such as shared evidentiary settlement, deterrent sanctions, or reforms that measurably reduce recurrence. The contribution is therefore conditional and diagnostic rather than totalizing. It specifies when democratic form persists while the binding conversion of evidence into consequence weakens.

The article proceeds in five moves. It first defines democratic hollowing as a transformation in the governance of public reality and specifies the coupled mechanism family. It then operationalizes narrative control, impunity signaling, and weak accountability loops through observable signatures, including conspiratorial inference governance, apocalyptic compliance framing, file-politics dynamics, and AI-related provenance stress. Next, it outlines a theory-driven comparative design using process tracing across the United States, Canada, and Europe, with Israel–Gaza included as a bounded stress test for tempo, classification, and evidentiary non-convergence under conflict conditions. The results section then reports the cross-case patterning of the mechanism family. The discussion closes by drawing out the paper’s implications for democratic repair, institutional design, and the problem of human judgment under conditions of plausibility overload.

Materials and Methods

This study adopts a theory-driven comparative design oriented toward causal process tracing rather than endpoint correlation. That methodological choice follows directly from the paper’s central claim. If democratic hollowing proceeds through upstream reconfiguration of plausibility, sanction, and consequence under intact legality, then it will often remain difficult to see through approaches that treat institutional survival, transparency presence, or regime-level democratic indices as sufficient evidence of constraint. Process tracing is therefore used to reconstruct how the proposed mechanism family operates over time, with particular attention to sequencing, institutional learning, and the conditions under which harms do or do not acquire enforceable weight (Beach and Pedersen, 2019: 11).

The analysis proceeds from a relational understanding of power in which constraint is produced through social relations and infrastructural arrangements rather than merely possessed by actors or offices. That starting point justifies treating plausibility environments and accountability routing as empirical objects rather than rhetorical afterthoughts (Barnett and Duvall, 2005: 39–42). Comparative leverage is generated through structured, focused comparison in which the same mechanism-guided questions are applied across settings and then read together to identify recurring patterns, meaningful variation, and plausible combinations sufficient for hollowing under intact legality (George and Bennett, 2005: 59). The United States, Canada, and Europe are selected not as representative regime types in the abstract, but as distinct plausibility environments. The United States offers high polarization and fast-moving credibility conflict. Canada offers high procedural density with a lower-spectacle political style and more routinized administrative absorption. Europe offers multi-level governance in which classification and administrative discretion are often distributed across national and supranational sites (Huysmans, 2000; Bigo, 2002). Huysmans captures the classificatory core of this terrain when he notes that migration was increasingly presented as "a danger to public order, cultural identity, and domestic and labour market stability" (Huysmans, 2000: 752).

That formulation matters beyond migration policy itself because it shows how a category can be rendered governable as a threat before any later adjudication of specific cases begins. Israel–Gaza is included as a bounded stress test rather than as an additional democratic case. Its role is to reveal how the same plausibility and accountability dynamics behave under conflict tempo, where emergency framing, classification struggles, and evidentiary non-convergence intensify quickly. The scope condition is explicit: the stress test is used to analyze information-environment dynamics, not to adjudicate contested substantive claims about the conflict itself. The evidentiary base is built from triangulated public materials, including institutional texts, legislation, court documents, oversight reports, formal statements, investigative journalism, platform and media artifacts, and benchmark infrastructures for contextual calibration. This triangulation is not treated as a generic “more sources is better” principle. It is methodologically necessary because the paper’s mechanisms operate precisely by governing how claims become credible, how enforcement expectations are taught, and how procedural routing converts harm into consequence or repetition.

For that reason, the analysis treats public claims not only as propositions but as circulating artifacts whose durability, mutability, timing, and uptake matter politically. The information disorder framework is used here as a sensitizing lens because it clarifies that contemporary democratic strain is not reducible to false content alone. It includes the phases of creation, production, and distribution, as well as the identities and incentives that govern uptake (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 12–13, 28). These premises justify the decision to analyze plausibility environments rather than treating misinformation as a standalone variable. They also help explain why the paper treats automated and semi-automated infrastructures as relevant even when a “human decision” formally remains present: the question is not only whether a human remains in the loop, but whether the evidentiary field presented to that human remains governable. Analytically, the paper distinguishes causal mechanisms from contextual amplifiers and from observable micro-signatures. The causal engine is the coupled operation of narrative control, impunity signaling, and weak accountability loops. Platform tempo, generative AI, and scandal-file circulation are treated as amplifying conditions that alter verification costs and attention dynamics, increasing the effectiveness of mechanism deployment without constituting mechanisms on their own.

Conspiratorial inference governance and apocalyptic compliance framing are treated as micro-signatures primarily within narrative control, meaning observable packages that shift inference norms and adjudicative standing rather than independent causal engines. The three mechanisms are operationalized as signatures of infrastructural power rather than as psychological states. The mechanisms are identified through a portable indicator scheme applied consistently across cases. What makes this comparison analytically difficult is precisely what makes it useful: the arenas are close enough in form and vocabulary to appear interchangeable, yet different enough in institutional routing to produce distinct pathways. The proximities and similitudes, shared democratic language, shared transparency rituals, shared platform infrastructures, make mechanism identification harder because the same surface signals can be generated by different underlying processes. This is why the paper does not code hollowing from outcomes alone, but from patterned indicator convergence: how inference rules are governed, how enforcement expectations are taught, and how procedural repetition absorbs consequence. The stress test value of the Israel–Gaza information environment is not that it is “like” the other cases, but that it compresses tempo and classification pressures so the upstream governance of plausibility becomes more observable.

The comparative payoff is therefore diagnostic rather than typological: the paper traces how similar democratic forms can host different hollowing dynamics. Narrative control is indicated by repeated moves that govern inference rather than merely messaging, including referee delegitimation, evidentiary reclassification, identity-sorting cues that convert factual dispute into allegiance conflict, and rhetorical packaging that makes doubt socially costly, including apocalyptic compliance framing (false precision, binary compression, personification of systems, immunization against doubt). Impunity signaling is indicated by patterned nonconsequence or selective sanction that recalibrates enforcement expectations, including strategic silence under reputational threat, procedural stalling that outlasts attention windows, and routinized ambiguity that shifts interpretive burden onto critics. Weak accountability loops are indicated by recurrent institutional processing that preserves legitimacy while leaving incentive structures intact, including repeat inquiries or reviews without measurable enforcement change, jurisdictional diffusion of responsibility, symbolic reforms that do not alter sanction capacity, and outcome latency that repeatedly severs visibility from consequence.

Counterindicators are tracked in parallel: dispute resolution through shared evidentiary standards tempers narrative control claims, reliable sanctions and deterrent effects temper impunity signaling claims, and reforms that measurably increase enforcement capacity or reduce recurrence temper weak-loop claims. Narrative control is operationalized as the governance of intelligibility, meaning patterned efforts to shape who counts as credible and what counts as evidence, including recurrent moves that delegitimate referees, reclassify evidentiary claims, and convert factual disputes into identity disputes (Benford and Snow, 2000: 614). In this paper, conspiratorial interpretation is treated as a specific mode of plausibility engineering within narrative control, because it collapses complex causal fields into agentic intention, distributes epistemic status through in-group loyalty, and converts uncertainty into moral urgency. This operational extension is grounded in the social psychological literature that identifies epistemic, existential, and social motives for conspiracy belief, including the desire to understand one’s environment, feel safe and in control, and maintain a positive image of the self and the social group (Douglas, Sutton and Cichocka, 2017: 539).

Methodologically, conspiratorial inference is therefore coded not primarily as a content category but as a shift in inference norms, especially when evidentiary standards are displaced by loyalty tests and skepticism is reframed as moral danger. A further micro-signature is coded as apocalyptic compliance framing, treated as a portable subset of narrative control that becomes visible across conspiratorial subcultures, crisis discourse, and certain techno-apocalyptic framings. It is identified through four observable features: false precision (specific dates or countdowns without testable mechanism), binary compression (compliance versus catastrophe), personification of systems (AI, “the state,” or “the planet” treated as intentional moral actors), and immunization against doubt (rhetorical conversion of evidentiary caution into moral failure). This signature is included because it governs inference norms upstream of factual dispute by transforming uncertainty into urgency and urgency into deference, intensifying the identity-sorting dynamics emphasized in information disorder research (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 27–28).

In practical terms, the analysis tracks whether such frames narrow perceived options, delegitimate verification, and convert procedural latency into interpretive certainty. Impunity signaling is operationalized by tracing repeated norm violations, legal breaches, or harmful practices that are met with delayed, partial, or absent sanction, with emphasis placed on the enforcement environment as a teacher of expectations rather than on any single event. This mechanism is coded when patterns of nonconsequence or selective enforcement plausibly recalibrate what actors believe they can do without penalty, including when institutions repeatedly fail to attach sanctions in ways that would ordinarily deter repetition. Strategic silence is incorporated here as an active tactic within impunity signaling. Maor defines strategic silence as an organization’s calculated decision to avoid internal and/or external communication in order to advance long-term goals, particularly under reputational threat and anticipated negative media coverage (Maor, 2016: 2). This supports coding nonresponse, ambiguity maintenance, and deflection as actions that can delay accountability traction, shift interpretive burdens onto critics, and exploit institutional tempo. Weak accountability loops are operationalized as repetitive institutional processing that preserves legitimacy while allowing recurrence. The empirical signature is procedural routing that produces hearings, reviews, reforms, or internal investigations without altering incentive structures, enforcement capacity, or jurisdictional authority in ways that would reduce recurrence. The analysis codes loop dynamics when institutional response becomes routinized as performance, when responsibility disperses through jurisdictional boundaries, and when each cycle closes without changing the probability of repetition.

This paper treats contemporary “file politics” as a high-salience observation site where all three mechanisms can become unusually legible. “Files” refers to leak cycles, document dumps, dossier-like releases, and scandal archives whose circulation is often treated publicly as an epistemic endpoint. Methodologically, the study does not assume that the existence of an archive settles disputes or guarantees consequence. Instead, it tracks how archives are metabolized within plausibility environments: who is authorized to interpret, what classificatory moves determine what counts as proof, what procedural gates are invoked, and how long consequence can be deferred without reputational collapse. Fenster’s analysis supports this stance by demonstrating that government secrecy frequently fails and high-profile disclosures recur, yet the circulation of information does not automatically produce democratic accountability or institutional consequence (Fenster, 2014: 309). Paquin’s analysis further supports the mechanism link between transparency, scandal amplification, and democratic trust erosion by showing how transparency and press-freedom mechanisms can be leveraged by radical-right populist strategies to expose alleged misconduct, selectively amplify scandals, and thereby strengthen anti-establishment narratives that corrode political trust (Paquin, 2025: 2).

Together, these sources justify treating file circulation not as resolution but as an arena in which narrative control, impunity signaling, and accountability looping can interact. Generative AI is treated as a contextual capacity shift that intensifies narrative competition and complicates evidentiary stabilization, not as a separate mechanism. Fisher, Howard and Kira argue that contemporary generative systems facilitate the production of persuasive synthetic media and fluent text at scale, such that "ordinary people now have unprecedented power to produce content" for benign or malicious purposes (Fisher, Howard and Kira, 2024: 2). Just as importantly, they argue that the governance challenge is not best understood as an entirely novel content category requiring wholly separate moderation logics. Rather, synthetic content magnifies an already familiar problem of harmful circulation while exposing the limits of bans, labels, and detection-first governance.

Their practical recommendation is that platforms should "double down on improving and enforcing their existing rules" rather than imagine that a new media ontology will solve an old enforcement problem (Fisher, Howard and Kira, 2024: 4). That insight is directly relevant here because the paper’s concern is not novelty for its own sake, but the weakening of institutions able to sort, verify, and sanction under conditions of abundance. Because reliable provenance detection cannot be assumed at scale, the analysis here treats generative AI less as a sui generis mechanism than as a force multiplier that raises the stakes of rule enforcement, platform responsibility, and adjudicative delay. In this study, AI is therefore incorporated as a background condition that inflates verification costs, increases volume and velocity, and intensifies the incentive for heuristic sorting under overload. Roberts’s framework helps formalize overload as governable rather than accidental: she theorizes online information control through small changes in the costs of access and attention, including information friction and information flooding, as mechanisms that shape spread and impact under high-information conditions (Roberts, 2014: 13). This supports treating “cognitive overload” not merely as a psychological byproduct of modernity but as a structural condition that can be exploited by actors seeking to reroute public reasoning from adjudication toward allegiance. Analytically, each case is examined manually using the same mechanism-guided prompts, tracking recurring moves that (1) govern credibility and evidentiary standards, (2) signal nonconsequence, and (3) route accountability through procedures that preserve legitimacy while allowing repetition. The same prompts are extended to track conspiratorial inference governance, apocalyptic compliance framing, file-politics dynamics, and AI-related provenance stress, treated as empirically traceable intensifiers within the same mechanism family (Douglas, Sutton and Cichocka, 2017: 539; Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 12–13, 28; Fisher, Howard and Kira, 2024: 2–3; Roberts, 2014: 13; Fenster, 2014: 309; Paquin, 2025: 2; Maor, 2016: 2). Validity is pursued through triangulation and negative-case attention. Mechanism presence is asserted only when multiple source types converge or when the same pattern is observable across distinct arenas, and counterindicators are actively tracked: narrative control claims are tempered when disputes resolve through shared evidentiary standards; impunity signaling claims are tempered when sanctions reliably attach and deter repetition; and weak accountability loop claims are tempered when reforms measurably alter enforcement capacity or incentive structures. This architecture preserves the paper’s comparative design while integrating the added material as mechanism-consistent extensions rather than as topic drift.

Results

The findings below report the cross-case patterning of the indicator scheme specified in the Methods, showing how the same mechanism family appears across arenas while varying by institutional pathway and tempo. Across the four arenas examined (United States, Canada, Europe, and the Israel–Gaza information environment), the analysis shows that democratic hollowing can proceed through public visibility rather than institutional collapse. Elections, courts, inquiries, and press infrastructures remain formally active, yet the binding force of accountability is repeatedly weakened because the struggle shifts upstream, from adjudicating facts to governing what can count as credible, actionable, and sanctionable in the first place. This is the practical meaning of governance of public reality in the paper’s conceptual framework: the durable capacity to shape plausibility conditions so that institutions appear to process conflict while consequences remain optional, delayed, or selectively applied.

To avoid definitional overreach, the analysis tracks counterindicators case by case. In the United States, counterevidence would include stable cross-partisan referee legitimacy and routinized sanction that produces deterrent effects even under polarization. In Canada, counterevidence would include closure that reliably converts inquiries and oversight into enforceable remedies within attention-relevant tempo windows, rather than diffusing into normal non-closure. In European settings, counterevidence would include transparency and press-freedom infrastructures that increase accountability without being repurposed into systematic trust-destruction, alongside classification regimes that do not pre-sort credibility and enforceability by threat categories. In the Israel–Gaza stress test, counterevidence would include rapid cross-network evidentiary convergence and verification practices that remain institutionally authoritative despite conflict tempo and moral sorting pressures.

A first consistent finding concerns narrative control as plausibility engineering, not spin in the usual sense. In each arena, actors compete to set the rules of inference: who is authorized to interpret, what evidentiary thresholds are considered legitimate, and which interpretive frames become socially safe to adopt. Where narrative control is strongest, contestation does not end because counterevidence is unavailable. Contestation becomes nonresolving because evidence is continually reclassified, and because audiences are routed into identity-aligned plausibility rather than shared verification. This is where conspiratorial uptake becomes methodologically relevant. Conspiracy is not treated here as eccentric belief content but as a shift in adjudication norms that substitutes loyalty tests for evidentiary standards.

This shift is reinforced by the fact that conspiracy frames can satisfy epistemic motives (certainty), existential motives (control), and social motives (positive group image). Under stress or polarization, these motives increase the attractiveness of interpretive packages that promise total explanation and moral clarity. The comparative variation concerns the style and locus of this plausibility engineering: in the United States it tends to surface as explicit credibility warfare and referee delegitimation; in Canada it more often travels through institutional tempo and a quieter normalization of non-closure; in European settings it is frequently intensified by classificatory pre-sorting and multi-level administrative discretion; and in the Israel–Gaza information environment it becomes most visible as accelerated inference conflict under high-tempo conditions where verification lateness is itself politicized.

Across cases, referee delegitimation, evidentiary reclassification, and identity-sorting cues function as recurring narrative-control indicators, because they shift contestation from what is true to who is authorized to decide what can count as true. A second finding is that conspiratorial inference does not merely misinform. It can stabilize an otherwise unstable accountability environment by providing a cognitively efficient explanation for why institutions fail to attach consequences. That stabilization is purchased through compression: complex causal fields are collapsed into agentic intention, and ambiguity is retrofitted into narrative certainty. This dynamic becomes most visible in what the paper codes as apocalyptic compliance framing: false precision, binary compression (compliance versus catastrophe), personification of systems (AI as enforcer, society as host), and immunization against doubt through “better safe than sorry” logic. The output is not simply a false claim. It is an interpretive technology that converts uncertainty into urgency, and urgency into deference, thereby governing inference norms upstream of factual dispute.

In practical terms, these frames narrow the perceived option set, delegitimate verification as delay, and convert hesitation into moral failure. Comparatively, the mechanism’s function is constant while its triggers vary: in the U.S. and Europe, urgency is often routed through polarized credibility contests and scandal logics; in Canada, it is more likely to attach to procedural latency and institutional “waiting rooms”; and in the Israel–Gaza information environment, urgency and moral sorting are intensified by conflict tempo and asymmetric recognition conditions, making binary compression and immunization against doubt especially politically effective. The third, and most structurally revealing, result is that file politics operates as a high-salience mechanism amplifier rather than an epistemic endpoint. In the U.S. “Epstein files” imaginary, but also in other leak cycles and dossier-like releases across contexts, document circulation is commonly treated as if it should settle disputes. The analysis finds the opposite pattern. Files routinely become contested objects whose political life is governed by classification, timing, interpretive authorization, and procedural gating. Analytically, file politics is treated as an observation site where narrative control (interpretive authorization and evidentiary reclassification), impunity signaling (nonconsequence despite visibility), and weak accountability loops (procedural absorption of scandal) become simultaneously observable.

The central empirical move is not that secrecy prevents truth. It is that recurring leak cycles and partial disclosure can coexist with deferred consequence. That coexistence is the precise space where the paper’s three mechanisms couple together: narrative control governs how the archive is read, impunity signaling teaches that consequence is optional, and weak accountability loops routinize institutional processing without reducing recurrence. Comparatively, this is where arenas differ most sharply in how the archive is metabolized: in the United States, file politics tends to be absorbed into polarized plausibility battles that convert evidentiary disputes into identity disputes; in Canada, file politics more often produces a procedural absorption pattern in which legitimacy is preserved through “proper process” while enforcement expectations thin; in Europe, transparency and scandal infrastructures can be repurposed into trust-destruction strategies that treat exposure as an end in itself; and in the Israel–Gaza information environment, the file logic is intensified by tempo and contestation over classification, making evidentiary non-convergence a durable outcome rather than a temporary phase. File politics also clarifies why redaction and incompleteness matter sociopolitically. Redaction does not only remove content. It can produce meaning by revealing the machinery of withholding, intensifying public speculation, and inviting plausibility contests over what the gaps signify.

In scandal environments, incompleteness is rarely experienced as a limit on knowledge. It is often experienced as evidence of concealed intent, which invites interpretive escalation. This dynamic helps explain how “the files” can become an endlessly renewable political object: every absence becomes another prompt for narrative capture, and every delay becomes another proof claim. The result is a perverse accountability inversion. Archives can increase attention while decreasing the probability that attention produces enforceable consequence, because plausibility fragmentation outruns institutional processing capacity and because procedural time becomes politically weaponizable. The comparative difference is primarily one of tempo and institutional routing: some settings convert incompleteness into rapid plausibility capture, while others convert it into slow procedural absorption, but the effect converges when consequence fails to attach within a realistic attention window. A fourth result concerns impunity signaling as a teachable environment.

Across arenas, repeated norm violations, legal breaches, or harmful practices that meet delayed, partial, or absent sanction recalibrate expectations about what can be done without penalty. The mechanism is not the violation itself but the enforcement pattern that follows. These signals are amplified when sanction is inconsistent, when investigations remain open without closure, or when procedural responses substitute for binding remedy. Strategic silence functions here as an active tactic rather than an absence. Under reputational threat, noncommunication can delay traction, shift interpretive burden onto critics, and exploit institutional tempo, particularly when media cycles move faster than oversight capacity. When strategic silence co-occurs with procedural routing (reviews, inquiries, “lessons learned” reports), publics can struggle to distinguish genuine enforcement from institutional theater, even while legality remains intact. Comparatively, the U.S. pattern tends to be visible as selective enforcement narratives and overt sanction conflict; the Canadian pathway tends to normalize non-closure and diffusion as “due process”; European settings often express impunity through classification and administrative discretion that pre-sorts enforcement priorities; and the Israel–Gaza stress test makes sanction asymmetries and recognition struggles more immediately visible, clarifying how impunity signaling can be accelerated by emergency framing. A fifth result extends the framework into contemporary media capacity conditions by showing that generative AI functions as a verification-cost inflator that increases the payoff of plausibility engineering. The analysis does not treat AI as a single causal driver. It treats AI as a capacity shift that increases the volume, velocity, and stylistic persuasiveness of narrative artifacts, thereby increasing incentives for heuristic sorting under overload. Accordingly, AI is treated here as an amplifier of plausibility competition and verification costs that strengthens the effectiveness of narrative control and the durability of weak loops, rather than as an independent mechanism of hollowing.

Under these conditions, provenance disputes become easier to trigger and harder to settle quickly, which strengthens narrative control strategies that keep publics inside suspension long enough for consequence to dissipate. The relevant point is not simply that synthetic content exists. It is that the credibility environment is increasingly organized around contested authenticity, and contestation itself can become the goal because it delays adjudication and weakens the attachment between evidence and enforcement.

Comparatively, the effect is mediated by institutional tempo and public trust baselines: where verification infrastructures are politicized or slow relative to circulation, contested authenticity more readily becomes a stable equilibrium; where administrative routing is complex, provenance disputes are easier to proceduralize; and under conflict tempo, authenticity contestation is folded into moral sorting, intensifying non-convergence. A sixth result is that transparency itself can be weaponized as a trust-destruction toolkit, particularly in European contexts where radical-right populist strategies reframe transparency and press freedom not as accountability mechanisms but as resources for scandal amplification and institutional delegitimation. Here, visibility becomes ambivalent. It can expose wrongdoing, but it can also be mobilized to portray institutions as irredeemably corrupt, thereby reducing willingness to accept institutional adjudication even when it is procedurally sound. This is a direct instance of narrative control and weak accountability loops coupling together: public visibility increases while collective capacity to translate visibility into enforceable sanction decreases, and procedural outcomes are more easily dismissed as either rigged or irrelevant.

The comparative implication is that visibility does not travel with consequence: across cases, transparency may increase informational supply while simultaneously degrading the shared adjudicative ground needed for sanction to bind. Finally, the comparative stress test across the Israel–Gaza information environment and the North American and European arenas underscores the centrality of tempo. High-intensity conflict conditions make plausibility engineering unusually salient because verification is structurally late, and lateness can be reframed as proof of bad faith. Under these conditions, the mechanisms converge. Narrative control sets inference rules and defines who is credible. Impunity signaling teaches that consequence is optional or asymmetrical. Weak accountability loops routinize response without reducing recurrence. The comparative pattern is not that the cases are identical. It is that they exhibit the same underlying mechanism family operating at different speeds and through different institutional pathways, which is precisely what the paper’s framework was built to identify.

Queer, disability, and postcolonial analytics are mobilized here as a refusal of stabilizing narrative and a diagnostic of institutionalized power, foregrounding how “individual failure” is produced when power stays silent through administrative deferral and credibility denial, while episodes of excessive loudness are read as reactive performances of authority under perceived loss rather than as evidence of renewed democratic constraint. Viewed through the title axes, the United States case most clearly foregrounds legibility struggle, as referee legitimacy and evidentiary standing become objects of direct political contestation. Canada foregrounds capacity, as procedural density and administrative burden shape whether oversight can attach consequence within meaningful tempo windows. The European settings foreground classification, as risk and belonging categories pre-sort credibility and enforceability and enable transparency to be repurposed into distrust.

Empirically, classification appears as the administrative sorting of persons and claims into threat, risk, belonging, and credibility categories that pre-structure what counts as admissible proof, what triggers exceptional handling, and whose harms are treated as presumptively actionable. The Israel–Gaza stress test compresses all three, revealing how tempo and classificatory asymmetries accelerate evidentiary non-convergence and sanction asymmetry.

Discussion

The results sharpen the paper’s core claim: democratic hollowing is less a story of institutional disappearance than a story of democratic constraint being relocated upstream into the governance of public reality. Across the United States, Canada, and Europe, and under the bounded stress test of the Israel–Gaza information environment, formal democratic practices remain visible. Elections continue, courts sit, oversight bodies operate, journalists publish, and transparency artifacts circulate. Yet the binding force of those practices thins when credibility, sanction, and denial are reorganized so that institutions can continue to process conflict without reliably attaching consequence.

What changes, in other words, is not the existence of democratic form but the dependable conversion of evidence into enforceable response within a tempo that still matters politically. This is why the paper’s mechanism family matters. Narrative control, impunity signaling, and weak accountability loops do not operate as detachable explanations stacked beside one another. They operate as a coupled infrastructure. Narrative control governs intelligibility by shaping inference rules: what counts as evidence, who counts as a credible interpreter, and which interpretive moves remain socially or institutionally legible without penalty. Impunity signaling recalibrates expectations of enforcement by teaching publics and institutions which violations will be absorbed, delayed, or selectively pursued, including through strategic silence under reputational threat (Maor, 2016: 2). Weak accountability loops stabilize the arrangement by routing harm through procedural repetition that preserves legitimacy while leaving incentive structures substantially intact. The combined effect is a plausibility environment in which accountability is formally available yet practically unreliable.

The comparative contribution is not that the four arenas are equivalent. It is that the same upstream mechanism family appears across them, while expressing itself through different institutional pathways and narrative styles. In the United States, narrative control is most visible as explicit credibility warfare. Referees are not merely contested, they are actively delegitimated, and adjudication is recoded as partisan aggression. This is the environment in which conspiracy frames become politically effective not because they “replace facts” but because they rewire the rules of settlement, turning skepticism into a loyalty failure and verification into complicity.

The motivational literature helps explain why this style takes hold under stress and polarization: conspiracy narratives can satisfy epistemic motives (certainty), existential motives (control), and social motives (positive group image), which makes identity-aligned plausibility a resilient equilibrium (Douglas, Sutton and Cichocka, 2017: 539). The U.S. implication is not that institutions vanish. It is that the credibility environment becomes structured so that institutional action, including enforcement, can be narratively framed as illegitimate regardless of evidentiary quality. In Canada, the same mechanism family is less spectacular but no less relevant. The pathway is often routed through capacity, tempo, and procedural density. Hollowing can accumulate when complex administrative routing, jurisdictional diffusion, and high transaction costs normalize non-closure while preserving the appearance of responsiveness. This does not require overt referee war. It can proceed through “proper process” that repeatedly outlasts attention windows, teaching publics that resolution is improbable even when institutions appear to be working. The effect is a quiet form of impunity signaling: not necessarily the dramatic message that “nothing will happen,” but the slower lesson that consequence is unlikely to attach in time to matter. Here, strategic silence and procedural repetition can interlock: silence buys time, and loops consume time, producing an accountability experience that is formally lawful yet practically exhaustible. European settings clarify a third pathway that hinges more heavily on classification and administrative discretion within multi-level governance.

In these environments, plausibility is often pre-organized by categories of risk, threat, legality, and belonging, which shapes whose claims are treated as presumptively credible, whose harms are routed into bureaucratic recognition, and whose speech is treated as noise. That classificatory pre-sorting matters because it makes narrative control less dependent on overt propaganda. Narrative control can be exercised by governing the standing conditions under which speech counts and by structuring the administrative legibility of harm. These are precisely the conditions under which transparency can become ambivalent. Visibility can expose wrongdoing, but it can also be weaponized into scandal amplification and institutional delegitimation strategies that erode trust while leaving governance machinery intact (Paquin, 2025: 2–3). In the paper’s terms, this is a tight coupling between narrative control and weak accountability loops: scandal can proliferate while institutional consequence remains optional, particularly when adjudication itself becomes a contested political object. The Israel–Gaza information environment functions as a bounded stress test that compresses these dynamics and makes tempo decisive.

Under conflict conditions, verification is structurally late relative to circulation, and lateness is easily reframed as proof of bad faith. Information disorder research helps specify why this matters: interpretation is shaped by identity and group affiliation, and accepting information that challenges one’s sense of self can be jarring, making resistance to dissonant evidence easier even when the message appears persuasive to a neutral observer (Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017: 28–29). In conflict tempo, this identity-sorting becomes more intense and more morally saturated, making evidentiary non-convergence durable. What the stress test clarifies is not a unique “conflict mechanism,” but the acceleration of the same upstream family: narrative control sets inference rules, impunity signaling becomes asymmetrical in recognition and enforcement, and weak loops routinize response without reducing recurrence. The stress test therefore protects the paper from overclaiming: it shows how quickly the infrastructure can collapse into churn when tempo outruns institutional adjudication capacity. Seen comparatively, the paper’s added concepts are best understood as intensifiers inside the same mechanism family rather than as separate causal engines.

Conspiratorial inference governance functions as a style of inference management that turns adjudication into allegiance. File politics functions as a high-salience arena in which all three mechanisms become legible at once: archives circulate as if they should settle disputes, yet they often become contested objects whose meaning is fought over through authorization, classification, timing, and procedural gates. Generative AI functions as a verification-cost inflator. The crucial point is not simply synthetic content, but the shifting ratio between production and verification. Generative systems lower the cost of producing persuasive artifacts while raising verification burdens and destabilizing provenance cues (Fisher, Howard and Kira, 2024: 2–3). Fisher and colleagues are especially useful here because they resist treating synthetic content as a wholly distinct governance object. Their argument suggests that the central problem is not the existence of a radically new kind of harm, but the scaling of already damaging content forms under conditions where platforms still struggle to enforce even their existing rules consistently.

Under these conditions, contested authenticity becomes a routine political condition, and suspension itself becomes strategically valuable. Roberts’s account of information friction and flooding gives language to this structural feature: small changes in the cost of access and attention can shape what spreads and what stabilizes, making overload a governable environment rather than a mere byproduct (Roberts, 2014: 13). This aligns with the paper’s broader claim that public reality is governed upstream, in the conditions under which claims can become actionable.

The same logic increasingly travels through apparently neutral governance instruments. Ethical AI assessment frameworks, interpretability checklists, roadmap gap analyses, and strategic alignment matrices often present themselves as technical aids for responsible decision making. Yet they also preformat what can count as a risk, a gap, a valid explanation, or a remediable concern. In that sense, they are not external to the paper’s argument but contemporary examples of it. They show how legibility, capacity, and classification can be operationalized inside managerial and regulatory templates long before any public controversy reaches a court, a newsroom, or a parliamentary chamber. Governance by denial can therefore operate through the quiet normalization of formatted judgment as much as through spectacular disinformation or overt institutional breakdown.

This point has implications beyond public controversy in the narrow sense. As administrative, workplace, and semi-autonomous decision systems proliferate, the problem of hollowing increasingly appears inside ordinary governance interfaces rather than only at the level of spectacular scandal. When automated systems sort workers, flag risk, prioritize cases, or structure what counts as relevant information for later review, they do not merely assist decision making. They help organize the evidentiary field within which later human control is supposed to operate. This is why debates over meaningful human control cannot be confined to the question of whether a human formally remains in the loop. The prior question is whether that human is still being handed a legible, timely, and challengeable field of action. If legibility is already warped, capacity already overloaded, and classification already pre-sorting credibility upstream, then the language of retained human control may function as reassurance rather than as a real account of democratic constraint.

The same point can be stated more concretely. A reviewer who receives an AI-generated summary, a pre-ranked queue, and a small set of model-selected anomalies does not inherit the full field of contestability. They inherit a pre-governed evidentiary environment whose omissions, orderings, and relevance cues have already structured judgment in advance. Under those conditions, a formal human sign-off can coexist with substantive hollowing, because what remains "human" is the final gesture of endorsement rather than meaningful control over classification, timing, and challengeability. This is precisely why the paper treats human control as an accountability question, not merely as a staffing question.

This also helps clarify why governance should not be imagined as a purely formal relation between abstract institutions and abstract publics. It is mediated through voice, documentation, interface design, presentational norms, and the aesthetic codes that govern who or what appears composed enough to count. The politics of accountability therefore include not only the visible routing of files, sanctions, and hearings, but also the quieter governance of tone, fluency, and presentational legitimacy. These aesthetic dimensions are not secondary. They are among the practical means by which some claims become easy to absorb while others are coded as noise, excess, or non-governable disturbance. The apocalyptic compliance script clarifies one especially potent form of narrative control in this environment. Its four markers, false precision, binary compression, personification of systems, and immunization against doubt, should be read as an observable package that governs agency by narrowing perceived options and delegitimating verification as delay. As a package, it converts uncertainty into urgency and urgency into deference. Comparatively, its uptake is most likely where plausibility environments are already saturated: when identity-sorting is strong, when trust baselines are low, when verification is structurally late, and when institutions cannot attach consequence within a tempo that publics still experience as meaningful. A further contribution of the comparative framing is to return the paper’s title terms, legibility, capacity, and classification, from abstraction to mechanism terrain. Legibility refers to the conditions under which harms and claims become institutionally actionable, including evidentiary thresholds, standing, and recognized forms of proof. Capacity refers to what institutions can operationally do in time, including the tempo of investigation, enforcement bandwidth, administrative burden, and the practical costs of converting visibility into remedy. Classification refers to how categories of risk, threat, belonging, and credibility pre-sort subjects and claims, shaping who is presumptively trusted, who is presumptively suspect, and what kinds of harms are recognized as sanctionable. Read this way, the cases show different emphases without changing the engine: the U.S. pathway foregrounds legibility war and referee conflict; Canada foregrounds capacity, tempo, and the normality of procedural absorption; Europe foregrounds classification and the weaponization of transparency into distrust; Israel–Gaza foregrounds the acceleration of all three under conflict tempo and asymmetric recognition. These findings also sharpen what is at stake in the paper’s policy implication without overpromising quick fixes. Common remedies can misfire because they often add information without rebuilding adjudication capacity, or add procedures without restoring enforceable consequence. Transparency initiatives can increase informational supply while weakening shared standards of settlement. Procedural reforms can proliferate oversight artifacts while leaving sanction capacity unchanged. Platform interventions can remove some harmful content while leaving provenance contestation and identity sorting intact. The paper’s implication is therefore infrastructural: democratic repair requires governance with teeth, meaning the redesign of credibility and enforcement infrastructure so that evidence can once again attach to consequence within a realistic tempo, and so that legibility, capacity, and classification do not silently route some harms into permanent nonconsequence. Taken together, the comparative analysis supports a hard conclusion. A polity can learn to live inside degraded evidentiary conditions. It can normalize permanent suspicion toward some claims and permanent indulgence toward others. It can treat accountability as ritual, verification as delay, and truth as one more strategic resource among others. When that happens, democratic vocabulary remains available, but it no longer binds. The paper’s contribution is to show how that learning occurs, mechanism by mechanism, and why contemporary conspiratorial cultures, file politics, generative AI, and automated classificatory infrastructures intensify rather than replace the older dynamics of narrative control, impunity, and procedural deferral.

The forward-facing question that follows is therefore not whether democracy still exists as institutional form. It is whether the credibility environment has been rebuilt so that democracy can still bind as force. That requires more than visibility, more than transparency, and more than additional procedural layers. It requires institutions able to preserve legibility, maintain capacity under tempo pressure, and refuse classificatory arrangements that route some harms into permanent nonconsequence. Without those conditions, democratic systems may continue to look alive while losing the ability to compel accountability where it matters most.
