Here are generally adopted literature references in common English, categorized by their meaning:
1. For Misfortune, Struggle, or Hardship"A sea of troubles" (Shakespeare, Hamlet): Used to describe an overwhelming amount of problems."The best-laid schemes o' mice an' men / Gang aft agley" (Robert Burns): Used to express that even the best plans often go wrong."Whips and scorns of time" (Shakespeare, Hamlet): Refers to the injustices, mockery, and hardships one suffers simply by living."The Winter of our discontent" (Shakespeare, Richard III): Used to describe a time of unhappiness or dissatisfaction.

2. For Unavoidable Fate or Choices"To be, or not to be" (Shakespeare, Hamlet): Frequently used to introduce a question of existence, survival, or a critical life decision."A fork in the road" (Robert Frost/Common Idiom): Refers to a point where a decision must be made between two paths."The die is cast" (Julius Caesar/Suetonius): Used when a decision has been made and cannot be undone.

3. For Life’s Journey and Paradoxes"It was the best of times, it was the worst of times" (Charles Dickens, A Tale of Two Cities): Used to describe a paradoxical, turbulent period."All the world's a stage" (Shakespeare, As You Like It): References the idea that life is a performance and people are merely playing roles."A tale told by an idiot" (Shakespeare, Macbeth): Often quoted to describe life as meaningless or absurd.

4. For Knowledge or Curiosity"Pandora’s box" (Greek Mythology): Used to describe a process that, once started, will cause many unforeseen problems."To go down the rabbit hole" (Lewis Carroll, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland): Describes entering a bizarre, confusing, or complex situation.

"Catch-22" (Joseph Heller, Catch-22): A paradoxical situation where you cannot escape because of contradictory rules. For example, needing experience to get a job, but needing a job to get experience."Big Brother" (George Orwell, Nineteen Eighty-Four): Used to describe an all-seeing, invasive authority or government surveillance that monitors citizens' lives."Tilting at windmills" (Miguel de Cervantes, Don Quixote): Attacking imaginary enemies or pursuing futile, idealistic causes."Green-eyed monster" (William Shakespeare, Othello): A personification of jealousy."Wear my heart upon my sleeve" (William Shakespeare, Othello): To express one's feelings openly rather than hiding them."Method in my madness" (William Shakespeare, Hamlet): Suggests there is a specific, logical reason behind someone’s seemingly mysterious or irrational behavior."Brave New World" (Aldous Huxley, Brave New World): Used to describe a new era, often ironically, when it is technologically advanced but morally or spiritually empty."Pot calling the kettle black" (Miguel de Cervantes, Don Quixote): When one person criticizes another for a fault that they both share."The world is my oyster" (William Shakespeare, The Merry Wives of Windsor): Refers to a world full of opportunities and possibilities."Kafkaesque" (Franz Kafka, The Trial/The Metamorphosis): Describes a nightmarishly complex, bizarre, or illogical bureaucratic situation where the individual feels powerless.

"A pound of flesh" (Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice): Demanding something that is owed to you, even if it is cruel or will cause great suffering to the other person."Double, double toil and trouble" (Shakespeare, Macbeth): Often used to describe a situation that is becoming chaotic, difficult, or "brewing" into a mess."Moby Dick" (Herman Melville, Moby-Dick): A metaphor for an obsession or a goal that is nearly impossible to achieve and might eventually destroy you."Sour grapes" (Aesop, Aesop's Fables): To act like you didn't want something anyway, just because you couldn't have it."The lady doth protest too much" (Shakespeare, Hamlet): Used when someone’s overly emotional or frequent denials make you suspect they are actually guilty or lying."Sound and fury" (Shakespeare, Macbeth): Describes a huge, noisy commotion or great passion that ultimately signifies nothing or has no real impact."Sherlock" (Arthur Conan Doyle, Sherlock Holmes): A sarcastic nickname for someone who states the obvious ("No ship, Sherlock")."Stepford Wife" (Ira Levin, The Stepford Wives): Used to describe someone (usually a woman) who seems unnervingly perfect, submissive, or robotic."Scrooge" (Charles Dickens, A Christmas Carol): A universal term for someone who is stingy, grumpy, or hates holiday cheer."By the skin of my teeth" (The Bible, Book of Job): Escaping a disaster or succeeding at a task by the narrowest possible margin.

Here are ten of the most important references from pop culture that have become part of daily conversation:"Jump the shark" (Happy Days): Used to describe the moment when a long-running show or brand loses its original appeal and starts using desperate, ridiculous gimmicks to stay relevant."Gaslighting" (Gaslight): A term for psychological manipulation where a person is made to question their own sanity or perception of reality. It originated from a 1944 film of the same name."Bucket list" (The Bucket List): A list of things someone wants to do before they die ("kick the bucket"). Interestingly, the term didn't exist until the 2007 movie of the same name popularized it."Drinking the Kool-Aid" (1978 Jonestown Massacre): Refers to blindly following a person, ideology, or trend without question. (Note: This is a darker reference originating from the tragic real-life events often depicted in documentaries and media)."May the Force be with you" (Star Wars): While specific to the films, it is commonly used in everyday life to wish someone good luck, especially before a difficult task."We're not in Kansas anymore" (The Wizard of Oz): Used to describe a situation that has suddenly become strange, unfamiliar, or much more complicated than expected."YOLO" (Drake, "The Motto"): Standing for "You Only Live Once," this became a universal shorthand for justifying risky behavior or making the most of a moment."Catfish" (Catfish documentary/MTV show): Used as a verb to describe someone who creates a fake online identity to deceive others, usually in a romantic context."Show me the money!" (Jerry Maguire): A common way of demanding proof or tangible results, typically regarding payment or a job offer."The Truman Show" (The Truman Show): Used to describe the feeling that your life is being watched, staged, or that you are the unintended center of everyone else's attention.

"The first rule of Fight Club is: you do not talk about Fight Club" (Fight Club): Used to describe a secret or unspoken agreement that everyone in a group is expected to follow strictly."Oh, you sweet summer child" (Game of Thrones): A patronizing way to tell someone they are being naive or have no idea how difficult a situation is actually going to be."Debbie Downer" (Saturday Night Live): A nickname for someone who constantly shares negative news or brings down the mood of a group."Friend zone" (Friends): Describes a situation where one person in a relationship wants to be romantic while the other only views them as a platonic friend."Spam" (Monty Python’s Flying Circus): While originally a brand of canned meat, the use of "spam" to describe repetitive, unwanted electronic messages was inspired by a 1970 comedy sketch where the word is repeated endlessly."Toast" (Ghostbusters): Used to say someone or something is finished, defunct, or in serious trouble (e.g., "If we don't finish this project, we're toast")."Core memory" (Inside Out): Used to describe a specific, highly influential moment from your past that you believe shaped who you are today."Red pill / Blue pill" (The Matrix): A metaphor for choosing between learning a painful, life-changing truth (red pill) or remaining in blissful, contented ignorance (blue pill)."Sophie’s Choice" (Sophie’s Choice): Refers to a situation where someone is forced to make a tragic decision between two equally balanced or beloved options."It’s all Gucci" (Hip-hop culture/Social Media): A popular slang term used to say that everything is fine, cool, or going well.

"Break the internet" (Kim Kardashian/Social Media): Used when something—usually a piece of news or an image—is so viral that it seems to overwhelm the world's attention or digital servers."Bring a knife to a gunfight" (The Untouchables): A metaphor for being severely outmatched or completely unprepared for a specific challenge or conflict."Round up the usual suspects" (Casablanca): Used to describe a predictable, often ineffective response to a problem where you blame the same people or use the same old solutions."A perfect storm" (The Perfect Storm): Describes a rare combination of circumstances that aggravate a situation drastically, leading to a disastrous outcome."Main character energy" (TikTok/Social Media): Refers to someone who carries themselves with confidence or drama as if they are the lead role in a movie, often used to describe someone being self-centered or highly intentional about their life."My bad" (Clueless): A casual way to accept blame for a mistake. While it sounds like ancient slang, it was significantly popularized by the 1995 film Clueless."Regifting" (Seinfeld): The act of giving a gift you received to someone else as if it were new. This social concept became a standard term thanks to a famous 1995 episode of Seinfeld."I feel like I'm taking crazy pills" (Zoolander): A humorous way to express frustration when you feel like everyone around you is ignoring a glaringly obvious fact or truth."Ride off into the sunset" (Western Films): Originally the standard ending for cowboys in old movies, it now refers to any person reaching a happy, peaceful conclusion to a chapter in their life or career."A glitch in the Matrix" (The Matrix): Used to describe a strange coincidence or an unnerving moment that feels like a mistake in reality itself.

1. Classic Literature & DramaThese are phrases that feel so natural we often forget they were written by specific authors centuries ago:"Break the ice" (William Shakespeare, The Taming of the Shrew): To initiate a conversation or relieve tension in a social setting."Wild goose chase" (William Shakespeare, Romeo and Juliet): A hopeless or pointless search for something that doesn't exist or is impossible to find."Dead as a doornail" (William Shakespeare / Charles Dickens): Used to emphasize that something is completely dead or no longer functional."Love is blind" (Geoffrey Chaucer, The Canterbury Tales): Suggests that being in love makes you overlook a person's flaws.2. Modern TV & Film CatchphrasesThese more recent additions have fundamentally changed how we describe social dynamics:"The dark side" (Star Wars): Refers to the evil or negative part of someone's personality or a specific organization."Dealbreaker" (30 Rock): A specific factor or issue that makes a situation or relationship unacceptable."You had me at hello" (Jerry Maguire): Often used (sometimes jokingly) to mean you were convinced or fell in love the very first moment something happened."Jump the gun" (Sports/Early 20th Century): To start something too early or before you are supposed to.3. Historical & Nautical OriginsMany "common" phrases actually come from specific historical events or old seafaring terms:"Read the riot act" (1714 British Riot Act): To give someone a stern warning or reprimand to stop their bad behaviour."Turn a blind eye" (Admiral Horatio Nelson): To knowingly ignore a fact or situation. Nelson literally held a telescope to his blind eye to pretend he didn't see a "retreat" signal."Bite the bullet" (19th Century Surgery): To endure a painful or difficult situation with courage, referencing soldiers who bit bullets during surgery without anesthesia."Under the weather" (Nautical origin): To feel unwell. Sick sailors would go below deck to get "under" the harsh weather.4. Visual Shorthand"Tip of the iceberg" (Idiom): A small, visible part of a much larger, hidden problem."Burning bridges" (Idiom): Irreversibly ending a relationship or path so that you can never return to it.

"Swan song" (Ancient Greek Legend): A final performance or activity before retirement or death (based on the myth that swans sing a beautiful song right before they die)."Crossing the Rubicon" (Julius Caesar): Passing a point of no return. When Caesar crossed the Rubicon river with his army, he knew he was starting a civil war he couldn't stop."White elephant" (Legend of the King of Siam): A possession that is useless or troublesome, especially one that is expensive to maintain or difficult to get rid of."Move the needle" (Modern Business Speak): To make a noticeable or significant difference in a situation or a set of data."Ghosting" (Modern Dating/Social Media): Suddenly ending all communication with someone without any explanation."Hate-watching" (Modern Pop Culture): The activity of watching a television show or movie that you find bad or annoying, purely to mock or criticize it."Cold feet" (Stephen Crane, Maggie: A Girl of the Streets): To become nervous or lose your courage right before a big event, like a wedding or a major presentation."The elephant in the room" (Ivan Krylov's Fables): An obvious major problem or issue that people are avoiding discussing or acknowledging."Paint the town red" (19th Century Slang): To go out and enjoy yourself flamboyantly, usually by drinking and partying."Drinking from a hose" (Academic/Corporate Slang): Being overwhelmed by a massive amount of new information or tasks all at once.

From Greek Mythology"Achilles' heel": A person's one specific weakness or vulnerable point in an otherwise strong character."The Midas touch": The ability to make a lot of money or be successful in everything you do (though in the myth, it was actually a curse)."Opening Pandora’s box": Performing an action that seems small or innocent but ends up causing a huge, uncontrollable wave of problems."A Trojan Horse": Something that looks like a gift or a helpful addition but is actually intended to undermine or destroy from the inside."Between Scylla and Charybdis": The original version of "between a rock and a hard place"—being forced to choose between two equally dangerous options."A Herculean task": A job that requires enormous strength, effort, or endurance to complete.From Aesop’s Fables"Cry wolf": To give a false alarm so many times that when a real danger finally happens, no one believes you or comes to help."Lion’s share": The largest or best part of something when it is divided up."Dog in the manger": Someone who prevents others from having or using something they don't even want or need themselves."Slow and steady wins the race": From The Tortoise and the Hare, this is used to remind people that consistency is often better than being fast but unfocused.

Secularized Religious Idioms"The writing on the wall" (Book of Daniel): A clear sign that something is going to fail or come to an end soon."Good Samaritan" (New Testament): Someone who helps a stranger in need without expecting anything in return."Turn the other cheek" (New Testament): Choosing not to retaliate or seek revenge when someone mistreats you."Wash your hands of it" (Pontius Pilate): To refuse to take responsibility for a situation or to distance yourself from it entirely."At your wits' end" (Book of Psalms): To be so worried or confused that you don't know what to do next."Forbidden fruit" (Book of Genesis): Something that is desired specifically because it is not allowed.More Pop Culture "Shorthand""The Golden Ticket" (Charlie and the Chocolate Factory): A rare opportunity that guarantees success or entry into something exclusive."Kryptonite" (Superman comics): A specific thing that acts as a person’s ultimate weakness (similar to Achilles' heel, but often used for things like "Chocolate is my kryptonite")."Catnip" (Real world, but a common metaphor): Used to describe something that is irresistible to a specific group of people (e.g., "That new tech is catnip for investors")."Double-edged sword" (Ancient, but used constantly in media): A situation or decision that has both favorable and unfavorable consequences.

1. Nautical (Sea) TermsThese come from the age of sailing ships, where "social shorthand" was often a matter of life or death:"Loose cannon": Someone who is unpredictable and likely to cause damage. In the old days, if a heavy cannon broke free from its ropes during a storm, it would roll around the deck smashing everything in its path."Show your true colours": To reveal your real character or intentions. Ships used to carry flags (colours) from many nations to deceive enemies; they only showed their "true" flag when they were ready to fight."Taken aback": To be surprised or shocked. This originally described a ship's sails being flattened against the mast by a sudden change in wind, stopping the boat dead."Feeling blue": To be sad. When a ship’s captain died at sea, the crew would fly a blue flag and paint a blue stripe along the hull when returning to port."Smooth sailing": When a situation is going perfectly and easily without any obstacles.2. Sporting MetaphorsThese are the go-to phrases in business and politics to describe progress and competition:"Ballpark figure": A rough estimate. It refers to being "within the stadium," even if you aren't exactly on the right base."Hail Mary": A desperate, last-ditch effort with very little chance of success. It comes from American football, describing a long, risky pass thrown at the very end of a game."Drop the ball": To make a mistake or fail to handle a responsibility."Level playing field": A situation where everyone has the same opportunities or advantages."On the home stretch": The final part of a long or difficult task. This comes from horse racing, referring to the final straight part of the track leading to the finish line.3. Combat & Weaponry"Bite the bullet": To endure a painful situation bravely. Before anesthesia, soldiers would literally bite down on a lead bullet to keep from screaming during surgery."Flash in the pan": Something that shows great promise but fails almost immediately. It refers to early flintlock guns where the gunpowder would sometimes flare up (flash) in the pan without actually firing the bullet.
