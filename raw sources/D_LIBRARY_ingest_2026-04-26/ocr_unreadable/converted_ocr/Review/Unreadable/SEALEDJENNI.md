# SEALEDJENNI

## Extracted Text

Introduction

The glitch is the tell: the moment someone senses the machine in a work, flinches, and turns
that feeling into suspicion toward the maker. When meaning feels unstable, audiences reach
for tests of legitimacy. Is it real. Is it legitimate. Is it worth trusting.
The Sealed Card Protocol begins from that flinch and treats it as data rather than confusion.
The current controversy around generative AI is often framed as a dispute about capability.
Can the system produce plausible texts, plausible images, plausible voices. Yet the sharper
problem is institutional and cultural, not technical. The struggle concerns responsibility and
legitimacy: who can be held accountable for what a hybrid pipeline produces, how
accountability is evidenced, and how recognition is stabilized into durable credit when
mediation becomes perceptible and nameable. In the Sealed Card Protocol, the glitch marks
the precise moment when evaluation shifts from artifact to pathway.
This article refuses a familiar shortcut. It does not ask whether AI corrupts authenticity, as if
authenticity were the absence of mediation or the presence of a pure interior voice. Instead,
it treats authenticity and legitimacy as negotiated readings of mediation, stabilized or
destabilized at interfaces: peer review, platform formatting, citation regimes, aesthetic
conventions, disclosure rules. Under these conditions, the same artifact can be praised for
clarity while the process that produced that clarity is treated as contamination. The same
work can be recognized as compelling while its maker is rendered suspect. The glitch is the
governance event in which that conversion is decided.
To name what is being decided, the paper works with charging as an action rather than
charge as a substance. Charging names the public allocation of credibility, not the private
intensity of experience. It is the social work by which meaning adheres so that an object,
text, or practice can survive circulation as real enough to trust. Charging can accumulate
through repetition, marks of use, narrative framing, aesthetic coherence, and procedural
traceability. It can also spike into sudden authority, implode into delegitimation, or dissolve
until the artifact remains visible but socially weightless. The analysis therefore tracks where
charging stabilizes, where it fractures, and how institutions and publics manage that fracture.
Three arms are coupled to observe how legitimacy is manufactured across domains often
discussed separately. The first arm is artistic and appears first because this is where the
glitch is most rapidly perceived. It treats two practices as two sides of one instrument.
Wheels of Will is an Instagram-based visual practice that re-photographs the same ritual
tools across the Pagan year, testing what reads as anchored versus staged when objects
circulate under platform conditions. HEXA is a recursive writing pipeline that runs
long-duration material through cycles of revision, re-ingestion, and re-circulation, making
authorship legible as procedure rather than origin.
The second arm is academic and tracks legitimacy as infrastructure. Knowledge-making is
treated as genre-governed labor embedded in institutional tempo. This arm uses procedural
autoethnography as evidence, including the decision to provide a markdown trace of the
human and AI interaction chain as an accountability artifact. The trace is treated analytically
rather than confessionally. It exposes distributed labor rather than performing innocence.

The third arm is ritual and closes the analysis because it forces the strongest evidence
question. Witch bottles serve as the crystallizing case, moving from archaeological
archetype to contemporary practice and digital circulation. The point is not to resolve
whether ritual “works” under institutional criteria. The point is to examine how credibility is
redistributed when ritual objects are translated into galleries, feeds, and academic prose,
and how evaluative grammars change across those translations.
This article sits at the intersection of media theory, science and technology studies,
disability-informed critique, and practice-based research. It contributes to AI debates not by
adjudicating tool permissibility but by modeling legitimacy conversion as an observable
social mechanism.

Contemporary scholarship

Recent research on generative AI increasingly converges on a structural observation that
aligns with the glitch-as-tell: the public controversy is mischaracterized when framed as a
question of technical capability. The decisive shift occurs not at the level of plausibility but at
the level of adjudication. Once mediation becomes perceptible, evaluation migrates from
artifact to apparatus. The object no longer stands alone; it drags its conditions of production
into the scene of judgment. What follows is not simply skepticism about quality but a demand
for procedural accounting. The critical question becomes: who is authorized to answer for
this artifact, under what regime of evidence, and according to which model of responsibility?
In distributed production environments, accountability must be reconstructed rather than
presumed, and legitimacy becomes a problem of institutional design rather than expressive
authenticity.
Across editorial guidelines, copyright doctrines, and policy statements, authorship functions
less as romantic attribution than as infrastructural anchor. It is the juridical and reputational
node through which consequences circulate. Denying AI the status of author is therefore not
merely symbolic; it preserves a stable attachment point for sanction, correction, and credit
allocation. The insistence on human authorship operates as a boundary-maintenance device
within liability architectures. Disclosure, in this light, is not neutral transparency but the
visible edge of an epistemic sorting mechanism. It does not simply clarify who did what; it
determines what kinds of labor are recognized as labor, what kinds of assistance are treated
as contamination, and which hybrid configurations can be stabilized without destabilizing
institutional trust. The author persists as seal not because solitary production remains
descriptively accurate, but because institutions require a locus where responsibility can
crystallize.
Within art scholarship and creative industries research, the analytic emphasis shifts from
liability to authenticity, aura, and distributed agency. Here, generative systems are rarely
described as replacing artists; they are more often understood as reorganizing the ecology
of intention, affordance, and historical sedimentation embedded in training data. Yet even
this more generous framing reveals a fault line between process-value and artifact-value.
Historically, artistic authenticity has been anchored to legible traces of effort: apprenticeship,
technique, the visible mark of the hand, the temporal narrative of becoming. These traces

function as condensed proof that an artifact is not merely produced but earned. High-output
generators disrupt this evidentiary economy by compressing visible labor at the surface. The
problem is not that labor disappears; it is that its temporality becomes opaque. Conceptual,
curatorial, and iterative work may intensify upstream, but the artifact no longer reliably
signals the cost of its own production. This opacity unsettles evaluative grammars that
equate visible difficulty with deserved legitimacy.
Disability studies and queer disability scholarship introduce a further destabilization by
interrogating the normative assumptions that structure these grammars. Institutional
judgments of rigor, originality, and seriousness often presuppose stable bodies capable of
sustained cognitive endurance, predictable tempo, and consistent output. Within such
regimes, fluency becomes moralized: smoothness stands in for discipline; coherence stands
in for intellectual virtue. Reframing time as infrastructural rather than individual reveals how
“rigor” frequently encodes a particular bodily and temporal norm. Generative AI, in this
context, can operate as a legibility prosthesis—redistributing drafting, restructuring, and
revision labor in ways that enable participation under conditions previously marked as
deficiency. Yet this redistribution does not neutralize hierarchy. It recalibrates it. Institutions
may accept the final artifact while intensifying scrutiny of the pathway, interpreting
redistributed labor not as accommodation but as shortcut. In this sense, AI reconfigures the
politics of compliance: it can widen access to legible form while simultaneously tightening
surveillance over the means by which legibility is achieved.
Scholarship on digital religion adds another dimension by foregrounding how authority and
evidence mutate under conditions of mediation. Ritual practice, when translated into tutorial,
image, or testimony, is not simply documented; it is reformatted into genres that carry their
own credibility cues. Authority shifts from lineage to visibility, from embodied repetition to
demonstrable competence. This translation does not render ritual less real; it subjects it to
new proof regimes that privilege narratability and reproducibility. The relevance to generative
AI debates lies in the shared problem of legitimacy conversion. Across these domains,
recognition alone is insufficient. What matters is whether recognition can be stabilized into
credit under prevailing evaluative grammars, and whether mediation is interpreted as
technique or as epistemic threat.
What distinguishes generative conditions from prior forms of mediation is not the existence
of distributed production but the acceleration and inferability of that distribution. Generative
systems compress temporal distance between archive and artifact, making translation
events more frequent and more visible. This acceleration increases the density of
recognition moments while simultaneously heightening the probability of pathway audit. The
result is not simply more output; it is a proliferation of evaluative thresholds. As mediation
becomes easier to sense—even when imperfectly inferred—the likelihood of legitimacy
withdrawal intensifies. The controversy surrounding AI, then, is less about synthetic
capability than about the reconfiguration of institutional trust under conditions where the
seam is no longer guaranteed to remain invisible.

The Sealed Card Protocol takes its title as analytic model rather than decorative allegory.
The invocation of Sakura Cardcaptor is not an appeal to nostalgia, whimsy, or symbolic

2

flourish. It is a structural extraction. In the narrative, a child becomes custodian of forces she
did not originate, did not design, and does not fully comprehend. These forces are
elemental, volatile, and consequential. They are not inert tools awaiting mastery; they are
dynamic systems that exceed intention while still demanding containment. The dramatic arc
concerns not heroic sovereignty but iterative stabilization: release, recognition, containment,
re-sealing. Each act of containment does not erase power but reorganizes it. Amplification
does not dissolve responsibility; it concentrates it at the level of the bearer.
This structure provides a precise analytic entry point into contemporary generative
infrastructures. In the series, the small body becomes the locus where asymmetry
condenses. The cards are diffuse, powerful, ambient; the custodian is singular, accountable,
and visible. The asymmetry is not resolved by control but managed through ongoing
negotiation. This asymmetry mirrors a core feature of generative AI systems: distributed
computational capacity paired with localized responsibility. Models ingest vast corpora,
synthesize across histories, and output at scale. Yet when legitimacy is questioned,
consequences are routed to the human operator, author, or institutional representative. The
amplification is systemic; the accountability is individual.
The Sealed Card therefore functions as a governance diagram. It visualizes a regime in
which power scales outward through mediation while liability collapses inward toward the
person. The more expansive the generative infrastructure, the more intensely the institution
seeks a node where consequences can crystallize. This is not incidental; it is structural.
Institutions depend upon identifiable sites of sanction. Diffuse agency threatens enforcement
architectures because sanction requires attachment. The author function, in this context,
hardens not as romantic ideal but as infrastructural necessity. It becomes the seal that holds
distributed production within manageable bounds of responsibility.
What appears, at first glance, as “AI anxiety” is thus not merely technophobic sentiment. It is
a governance reflex. The anxiety indexes the strain placed upon legitimacy regimes when
mediation becomes newly visible and newly nameable. Under prior media conditions,
distributed production could remain partially opaque. Editorial processes, collaborative
drafting, and invisible labor were normalized because they did not destabilize the myth of
singular authorship. Generative systems disrupt this equilibrium by rendering mediation
inferable at the level of output characteristics. Even imperfect inference—mere
suspicion—can trigger pathway audit. The anxiety is less about machine capability than
about institutional vulnerability: if authorship becomes obviously hybrid, how are
responsibility and credit stabilized?
The Sealed Card model clarifies that containment is not about purity but about consequence
management. In the narrative, each card must be resealed not because it is evil but because
uncontained power disrupts the environment. Containment is a mode of governance, not
moral condemnation. Translating this to generative infrastructures, the insistence on
disclosure norms, authorship rules, and provenance tracking operates as contemporary
re-sealing. These practices do not eliminate generativity; they attempt to domesticate it
within recognizable accountability pathways.
However, the analogy is not celebratory. It exposes a tension. In Sakura Cardcaptor, the
custodian gradually forms relational knowledge of each card; power becomes intelligible

3

through encounter. In institutional AI governance, relational understanding is secondary to
liability assignment. The system does not ask whether the operator comprehends the
distributed mediation; it asks whether the operator can be held responsible for its effects.
The person becomes the convergence point where distributed agency is retrospectively
simplified into singular accountability.
This condensation has consequences. When amplification concentrates responsibility,
legitimacy becomes hyper-fragile at the level of the individual. A perceived glitch—an output
that feels “too smooth,” “too optimized,” “too machine”—can trigger a cascade in which
institutional trust withdraws not from the artifact alone but from the person associated with it.
The pathway becomes suspect; suspicion attaches to the bearer; containment reasserts
itself through exclusion or sanction. The glitch functions here as threshold event: the
moment perceptibility of mediation reorganizes evaluation from aesthetic appraisal to
governance procedure.
It is crucial to emphasize that the Sealed Card is not an analogy for creative content. It is a
model of asymmetrical power distribution. The cards exceed the child; the model exceeds
the author. Yet the child must answer. Likewise, generative systems exceed any single
operator’s comprehension, but institutions require that someone answer. The gap between
systemic scale and individual liability produces the core tension this paper tracks.
Amplification without redistributed responsibility generates intensified scrutiny of the human
node.
This model also clarifies why the author function persists despite repeated theoretical
proclamations of its death. The author survives because governance requires a seal.
Without a seal, legitimacy cannot be anchored; without anchoring, sanction cannot land.
Generative AI threatens to displace the author from origin point to coordinator of mediation.
But coordination is less symbolically stable than origin. Institutions prefer origin because
origin naturalizes responsibility. Coordination reveals distributed agency, and distributed
agency destabilizes consequence assignment.
The Sealed Card Protocol therefore names a practice-based inquiry into whether mediated
work can survive evaluation without collapsing into purity tests or liability panic. It asks
whether accountable procedure can replace fantasies of unmediated origin as the criterion of
legitimacy. Yet it remains attentive to the risk that the seam—once sensed—will be
interpreted not as evidence of procedural accountability but as contamination.
Finally, the model underscores a structural asymmetry that extends beyond AI. In any
regime where power scales through infrastructure while accountability localizes in persons,
governance tension intensifies. Generative AI merely makes this asymmetry acute and
visible. The glitch becomes diagnostic because it reveals the moment when institutional trust
shifts from engagement with artifact to interrogation of pathway. The seal tightens; the audit
begins.
In this framework, the Sealed Card operates as an analytic architecture for understanding
contemporary legitimacy regimes: power expands outward through layers of mediation and
infrastructure, yet responsibility collapses inward onto the individual who becomes the visible
bearer of distributed effects. Legitimacy, in turn, is not inherent to artifacts but allocated
through the authorization—or rejection—of the pathways that produced them. The glitch
4

marks the decisive threshold in this process: the moment when aesthetic engagement gives
way to procedural scrutiny, when evaluation shifts from what the work does to how it was
made, and governance replaces appreciation as the dominant mode of judgment.
The critical wager of the paper is that this conversion is neither accidental nor temporary. It is
the emerging structure of cultural production under generative conditions. The question is
not whether we can eliminate the glitch. The question is whether institutions can reorganize
legitimacy around accountable mediation rather than nostalgic purity, and whether
responsibility can be distributed proportionally to infrastructural power rather than reflexively
localized at the most visible node.

Conceptual framework and Methods
This article begins from the proposition that legibility is not a neutral descriptive property of
texts, images, or practices but a regulatory achievement produced within power-saturated
environments. What becomes readable as evidence, credible as work, or authoritative as
voice is not determined by intrinsic qualities of artifacts but by institutional grammars that
pre-structure perception. Universities, platforms, editorial boards, algorithmic feeds, and
subcultural publics all participate in calibrating what counts as seriousness, effort,
coherence, and authenticity. Legibility is therefore a disciplinary filter: it sorts, stratifies, and
stabilizes cultural production according to historically sedimented expectations about tempo,
authorship, fluency, and visible labor. To treat legibility as a power relation is to insist that
readability itself is governed—that smoothness, clarity, and coherence are not simply
aesthetic virtues but compliance signals within institutional infrastructures.
Within this framework, authenticity and legitimacy cannot be understood as interior states
residing in a subject or an object. They are negotiated outcomes at evaluative
interfaces—moments where mediation becomes perceptible and is either normalized or
pathologized. Authenticity is not the absence of mediation; it is the successful stabilization of
mediation under a regime that refuses to call it contamination. Legitimacy is authenticity’s
institutional ratification. Both are effects of recognition structured by power. When mediation
remains invisible or culturally sanctioned, legitimacy flows almost automatically. When
mediation becomes newly inferable—particularly under generative conditions—recognition
hesitates, and that hesitation opens a governance space.
Charging names the public allocation of credibility that allows an artifact to endure circulation
without collapsing under suspicion. It is not equivalent to affect, aura, or persuasion, though
it intersects with each. Charging is the social adhesion that enables a work to accumulate
trust across exposures, to survive scrutiny long enough to be cited, shared, endorsed, or
canonized. It is the conversion of recognition into stabilized credit. Yet charging is not
guaranteed; it is contingent and reversible. It depends upon whether the pathways that
produced the artifact are interpreted as acceptable technique or disqualifying shortcut. This
is why the analytic hinge of the protocol is the seam: the moment when evaluation shifts
from artifact to pathway. At that threshold, recognition and legitimacy can diverge. A work
may be admired while its method is condemned; it may be compelling while its producer is
rendered suspect. The seam is therefore not a stylistic crack but a governance trigger.

Methodologically, the protocol embraces triangulated practice-based inquiry in order to
render this seam observable across domains rather than merely theorized. Artifacts are
selected longitudinally to capture shifts in reception under altered circulation regimes. This
longitudinal emphasis is crucial: legitimacy is not static; it mutates as evaluative contexts
change. Selection prioritizes moments where mediation becomes perceptible or contested,
because it is in contestation that governance logics become visible. The method does not
seek representativeness in a statistical sense; it seeks analytic precision in lived procedure.
It treats singular trajectories not as universal models but as diagnostic instruments capable
of exposing structural tensions.
The primary textual artifacts—selected HEXA texts including the 2008 Ovid and Pygmalion
piece and its later refitting—function as controlled experiments in legibility recalibration. The
earlier text foregrounds affiliative rhythm and atmospheric accumulation; the refitted version
foregrounds segmentation and scaffolding. The shift is not merely stylistic but infrastructural:
it responds to platform-conditioned readability norms. By staging this longitudinal refitting,
the protocol isolates how the same intensity of attachment can be recoded as indulgent
under one regime and strategic under another. The artifact does not change its core
investment; the evaluative grammar changes its interpretation.
The primary visual artifacts—the Wheels of Will bottle photographs—perform an analogous
test in material form. Controlled aesthetic variation is introduced while object continuity is
preserved. Marks of use, residue, binding, and containment operate as condensed pathway
cues. By holding the object-family constant while altering lighting, framing, and stylistic
registers, the sequence exposes how charging migrates—or fails to migrate—across
circulation environments. The same bottle can accrue devotional authority, aesthetic
legitimacy, lifestyle branding, or dismissal as prop depending on the interpretive apparatus
through which it is encountered. The artifact remains materially identical; the regime of
reading transforms its credibility.
Crucially, procedural traces—drafts, revision histories, prompts, sequencing decisions,
metadata—are retained as chain-of-production objects. This retention is not transparency
theater; it is an analytic strategy. By making distributed labor inspectable, the protocol tests
whether visibility of mediation increases trust or intensifies suspicion. Institutional feedback
is treated as empirical data about legitimacy allocation. Comments, refusals, endorsements,
and framing requests are not interpreted as neutral assessments but as signals indicating
which mediations are sanctioned and which trigger withdrawal. In this sense, the institution
becomes an object of analysis rather than the arbiter of truth.
The analysis combines close reading, interface analysis, and procedural autoethnography
because legitimacy conversion operates simultaneously at textual, visual, infrastructural, and
experiential levels. Close reading isolates rhetorical recalibration; interface analysis reveals
platform-conditioned evaluation; procedural autoethnography exposes how distributed labor
is reorganized under scrutiny. The refusal of representativeness is deliberate: the protocol
does not claim that these trajectories exhaust possible configurations. Instead, it argues that
by tracing legitimacy at the level of lived procedure—where reward, refusal, and suspicion
are concretely experienced—structural patterns become visible that abstract survey methods
might obscure.

Critically, this framework risks inflation if not disciplined. To claim that legibility is power, that
authenticity is negotiated, and that charging governs circulation could slide into totalizing
explanation if every evaluative act is subsumed under the same analytic vocabulary. The
protocol therefore demands restraint: charging must not become a synonym for all credibility;
the seam must remain empirically identifiable rather than metaphorically ubiquitous. The
strength of the model lies in its specificity—the moment of evaluative shift—not in its
expansion into universal diagnosis.
What emerges from this methodological architecture is not a celebration of mediation nor a
lament for purity. It is a structural critique of how institutions convert perception into sanction.
By situating legibility as a regulated interface, by identifying the seam where recognition
mutates into audit, and by treating procedural visibility as data rather than confession, the
Sealed Card Protocol attempts to move beyond moral panic and toward analytic clarity. The
wager is that under generative conditions, legitimacy will not collapse—but it will increasingly
hinge on how institutions recalibrate their tolerance for visible mediation. The critical
question is whether they can evaluate accountable procedure without defaulting to nostalgia
for solitary origin, and whether distributed power can be matched by distributed responsibility
rather than reflexively localized blame.
Analysis and Results
The artistic arm functions as the protocol’s most immediate testing ground because this is
where evaluation tends to occur fastest and with the least explicit procedural framing. Under
platform conditions—particularly Instagram—assessment precedes deliberation. The object
appears; inference begins. Wheels of Will isolates how credibility is not located within the
bottle as a metaphysical property but inferred from material cues that condense a narrative
of production. Seals, bindings, residue, sediment, density of contents, signs of handling, and
containment architecture operate as evidentiary shortcuts. They do not prove ritual efficacy;
they imply duration, contact, repetition, intention. These cues function as compressed
pathways: they suggest how the object came to be without narrating its entire history.
Charging, in this context, does not reside inside the glass container. It emerges in the
interpretive relation between viewer and object. A bottle that appears worn signals continuity;
a bottle that appears overly composed signals staging. The same wax drip can be read as
devotional residue or aesthetic styling depending on the threshold of suspicion operative in
the viewer. The platform intensifies this ambiguity because it compresses attention into
scroll-time. Under such temporal compression, inference outruns verification. Credibility
becomes probabilistic and fragile.
Yet this very fragility exposes a tension. Wheels of Will attempts to stabilize interpretation by
preserving anchor images across seasonal variation. The object-family remains constant;
aesthetic register shifts. This controlled variation tests whether continuity of object can
sustain continuity of credibility. What emerges is not a stable gradient but a volatility curve.
Slight increases in stylization—sharper lighting, cleaner framing, more deliberate
composition—can flip inference from “devotional continuity” to “strategic aestheticization.”
The protocol thus reveals that credibility under platform conditions is not linearly correlated
with care or craft. It is correlated with perceived compliance to an unspoken visibility
threshold. Too little mediation risks invisibility; too much risks suspicion.

7

This suggests a deeper structural issue: platform aesthetics reward legibility, but institutional
suspicion punishes visible optimization. The artistic arm exposes the paradox that credibility
often depends upon appearing insufficiently optimized. The bottle must look intentional but
not too intentional. The mediation must be present but not foregrounded. Visibility must be
achieved without displaying the labor of achieving visibility. This is not an aesthetic standard;
it is a governance grammar.
HEXA performs the same experiment in language rather than object. The 2008 Ovid and
Pygmalion piece moves through associative drift, affiliative cadence, and atmospheric
accumulation. Its architecture is rhythmic rather than propositional. It generates intensity
through proximity and invocation rather than argumentative scaffolding. In certain evaluative
regimes, this produces depth; in others, it produces suspicion. The absence of overt framing
cues—clear thesis statements, explicit stakes, segmented argumentation—can trigger
coding as indulgent or insufficiently rigorous. Yet this coding reveals less about the text’s
internal structure than about institutional expectations for visible spine.
The later refitting re-engineers legibility. Segmentation sharpens. Cadence tightens. Hinge
lines signal argumentative progression. The text becomes scan-readable, quotable,
platform-compatible. This transformation is not reducible to editing for clarity. It is
infrastructural recalibration—an adaptation to circulation environments that privilege
modularity and explicit orientation cues. However, this recalibration introduces a new
vulnerability. When scaffolding becomes visible, the pathway of optimization becomes
inferable. The text risks being coded as strategic, even manipulative, because it displays
awareness of circulation mechanics.
The comparison between versions does not stage a simple authenticity-versus-artifice
dichotomy. Instead, it reveals a structural double bind. A text that refuses visible optimization
may be dismissed for lacking discipline; a text that performs optimization may be dismissed
for instrumental calculation. Legitimacy hinges not on the intensity or coherence of the
language itself but on how mediation is inferred. The decisive variable is not expressive
force but the perceived legitimacy of the pathway.
This leads to the arm’s central diagnostic claim: mediation becomes controversial not when it
exists—because all production is mediated—but when it is judged to have exceeded
acceptable visibility. Acceptable visibility is not codified; it is ambient. It is a tacit threshold
that shifts across audiences and platforms. The artistic arm demonstrates that the seam is
perceptual before it is theoretical. It is felt as excess or deficiency before it is articulated as
critique.
However, the arm must also confront its own limitations. Because the analysis centers on a
single longitudinal trajectory, it risks overgeneralizing from a particular aesthetic practice.
The volatility observed in Wheels of Will may not replicate across other subcultures or media
ecologies. The model’s strength lies in analytic precision, not universality. Its claims remain
diagnostic rather than representative.
The academic arm translates this volatility into formal rule. Academic legitimacy is
genre-governed and tempo-bound. It rewards specific performances of coherence: explicit
argumentation, citation density, structured progression, disciplined tone. These signals
operate as compliance markers within institutional infrastructures. Rigor is not merely
8

intellectual thoroughness; it is adherence to legibility conventions that align with historically
sedimented models of solitary authorship.
Generative AI destabilizes this infrastructure by redistributing visibility within the labor chain.
Drafting, rephrasing, structural outlining, and stylistic smoothing can be externally scaffolded.
What had previously required prolonged visible effort may now appear as rapid coherence.
This temporal compression produces suspicion not because the output is inferior but
because it decouples visible strain from visible result. Institutions accustomed to equating
rigor with endurance may interpret efficiency as shortcut.
A paper can therefore satisfy formal expectations—argument clarity, citation integration,
structural coherence—and still trigger authorship anxiety if the pathway is deemed
illegitimate. The anxiety is not reducible to plagiarism concern; it is ontological. It asks
whether the producer still qualifies as author in a meaningful sense. The author functions as
liability anchor, and AI threatens to redistribute cognitive labor beyond that anchor.
The provision of a markdown trace of the human-AI interaction chain attempts to intervene in
this logic. By rendering distributed labor inspectable, the trace reframes authorship from
origin myth to procedural accountability. The move relocates legitimacy from purity toward
traceability. However, this relocation is fragile. Disclosure can regulate process without
transforming the deeper evaluative grammar. If institutions continue to equate rigor with
solitary origin or visible strain, traceability becomes supplementary rather than constitutive of
legitimacy. The seam persists.
Moreover, disclosure itself can trigger suspicion. Visible traces of AI assistance may be
interpreted as evidence of dependency rather than evidence of procedural honesty. The
academic arm thus exposes a paradox parallel to the artistic arm: transparency can stabilize
credibility in one register while destabilizing it in another. Legitimacy remains contingent
upon institutional tolerance for visible mediation.
Critically, the arm must also acknowledge that AI does introduce epistemic risks—fabrication,
hallucination, shallow synthesis. The argument does not deny these risks. Rather, it
contends that institutional response often exceeds technical critique and enters moralized
pathway policing. The question is not whether AI introduces error but whether legitimacy
judgments are proportionate to demonstrable epistemic harm or are instead animated by
nostalgia for unassisted cognition.
The ritual arm pushes the framework to its evidentiary edge. Ritual credibility does not
emerge from institutional certification but from recurrence, embodied uptake, and relational
continuity. Witch bottles crystallize the evidentiary paradox. Archaeologically, they are
identified through patterned deposition and material recurrence. Their existence is verifiable;
their efficacy—often framed as harm prevented—cannot be experimentally recovered.
Negative outcomes leave no positive trace. The absence of harm cannot be empirically
archived.
This creates a structural asymmetry between material artifact and epistemic claim. The
bottle persists; the efficacy claim evaporates under institutional proof regimes. Digital
circulation intensifies this asymmetry. Objects become images; practices become tutorials;

9

efficacy becomes narrative testimony. Authority migrates toward visibility. What cannot be
visually demonstrated risks being reclassified as aesthetic residue.
Galleries can elevate the container while bracketing the claim. Platforms can aestheticize the
ritual while flattening its epistemic authority into lifestyle performance. The artifact survives
translation; the practice is reframed as content. Charging, once anchored in recurrence and
embodied relation, becomes contingent upon algorithmic circulation.
Yet the ritual arm also exposes a limit in the protocol itself. By analyzing charging as
credibility allocation, the framework risks assimilating ritual efficacy into institutional
legitimacy grammars. The critique must remain attentive to this danger. Not all credibility is
institutional; not all efficacy seeks ratification. Ritual practice may operate within alternative
epistemic economies that do not aim for academic validation. The arm therefore serves not
to domesticate ritual into institutional proof but to show how translation across regimes
reorganizes what counts as credible.
Across artistic, academic, and ritual domains, a shared feedback loop emerges. Translation
renders practice legible beyond its original environment. Recognition produces visibility.
Visibility triggers audit. Audit either stabilizes credibility or withdraws it. Withdrawal demands
recomposition—stylistic adjustment, disclosure modification, recalibration of visibility.
Generative AI intensifies the translation phase. It accelerates the movement from archive to
legible artifact. This acceleration increases recognition events and multiplies audit triggers.
The seam becomes more frequent because mediation becomes more inferable. What was
once ambient becomes suspect.
However, this intensification does not mean AI singularly creates legitimacy anxiety. Rather,
it amplifies preexisting evaluative grammars. The protocol’s critical claim is therefore not that
AI corrupts authenticity but that it exposes the fragility of institutional tolerance for visible
mediation. The glitch is not technological error; it is governance reaction.
The analysis ultimately reveals that legitimacy is less a verdict on artifacts than a policing of
pathways. Under generative conditions, the policing intensifies because the seam becomes
easier to sense—even when incorrectly inferred. The risk is not merely false positives of AI
detection; it is the normalization of suspicion as default evaluative posture.
The arms collectively demonstrate that visible optimization, distributed labor, and
infrastructural recalibration are not anomalies but structural features of contemporary
production. The question that remains is whether institutions can recalibrate legitimacy
around accountable mediation without collapsing into pathway punishment—or whether the
seal will continue to tighten each time amplification exceeds nostalgia for solitary origin.

Conclusion
This paper began from a small, almost bodily event: the glitch as perceptual tell. Not a
technical failure, but the moment someone senses mediation, pauses, and turns that pause
into an evaluation of trust. The Sealed Card Protocol treated that pause as a legitimacy
event and followed its consequences across three coupled domains where work is made,

10

circulated, and judged. By naming the glitch as a governance event and charging as a social
allocation, the protocol shifts AI debates from tool permissibility toward the legitimacy
machinery that decides whose mediated work is allowed to count.
The analysis showed that the decisive struggle is not whether generative AI can produce
plausible artifacts. The struggle is how recognition is converted into legitimacy when
production is distributed. In the artistic arm, Wheels of Will demonstrated that charging is
performed through anchors rather than claims. Seals, bindings, residues, and containment
cues operate as condensed pathways that invite inference about how an object came to be
and whether it deserves trust. The same cues can also be reclassified as theatrics the
moment an audience decides the labor should have remained invisible. HEXA showed the
parallel dynamic in language. A text refitted for circulation can preserve attachment while
changing its risk profile. Drift can be punished as indulgence. Scaffolding can be punished
as construction. In both cases, suspicion adheres to the seam where the artifact reveals its
conditions of making.
In the academic arm, the same flinch becomes rule. Institutions can accept an artifact and
still refuse the producer’s claim to legitimate authorship because authorship functions as a
seal for liability, credit, and sanction. Under these conditions, AI intensifies pre-existing
gatekeeping. It lowers the cost of genre compliance while sharpening the policing of
pathways, especially when clarity appears without the visible struggle that institutions quietly
treat as evidence of rigor. The decision to provide a markdown trace of the human and AI
interaction chain was introduced as a methodological counter-move against purity
metaphysics, relocating legitimacy from interior authenticity toward accountable procedure.
Yet the analysis also clarified the limit of that move: traceability can satisfy disclosure norms
without guaranteeing recognition if the deeper hierarchy remains intact, the expectation that
legitimate work must look like solitary origin and bear a recognizable cost.
The ritual arm pushed the argument to its evidentiary edge. Ritual practice was treated as an
epistemic technology of agency rather than as belief content. In digital ecologies, authority
and proof are repeatedly rebuilt as visibility, tutorialization, testimony, and aesthetic cueing,
and that rebuilding makes practice vulnerable to mischarging through translation. Witch
bottles crystallized the point because they survive as objects while their original scenes of
efficacy cannot be recovered through institutional verification. The outcome that would
matter most is often negative, a harm prevented. The bottle persists anyway as a material
claim that protection could be enacted by means not reducible to proof. Contemporary
circulation repeats the conversion problem under new conditions. Display regimes and
platforms can elevate the container while editing the claim, accepting the artifact while
withdrawing legitimacy from the pathway and the epistemic authority it asserts.
Taken together, the three arms support a single claim. Generative AI does not simply
increase output. It reorganizes where legitimacy is assumed to reside and sharpens the
conditions under which recognition is allowed to become durable credit. By amplifying
translation from lived archive to legible form, AI increases recognition events and multiplies
opportunities for legitimacy withdrawal at the seam where mediation becomes audible. The
glitch is therefore not marginal. It is governance: an audience, an institution, or a platform
decides whether techniques are mediation or disqualifying contamination, and if those who
wield them are allowed to count as a creator of knowledge. The recurring finding is that

Acceleration multiplies recognition events. More recognition events produce more
opportunities for audit. The seam becomes more frequent, not because mediation is new,
but because its inferability is amplified.
This insight reframes the public debate. The core controversy is not whether generative
systems can produce plausible output. That question has largely been settled at the level of
surface competence. The controversy concerns how institutions recalibrate trust when
distributed production becomes visible enough to threaten the myth of solitary origin.
Legitimacy becomes a question of governance architecture rather than aesthetic
authenticity.
The paper therefore rejects a purity model of authenticity. Authenticity is not unmediated
interior truth. It is a socially stabilized reading of mediation that survives scrutiny. Legitimacy
is authenticity’s institutional ratification. Charging names the social work through which that
stabilization is achieved—through repetition, continuity, narrative framing, procedural
traceability. Glitching names the moment that stabilization fractures, spikes, implodes, or
dissolves under pathway suspicion.
However, the argument also resists celebratory techno-optimism. Generative AI does not
automatically democratize production. It redistributes compliance pressures. It can widen
access to legible form while intensifying scrutiny of the means by which legibility is achieved.
It can expose infrastructural biases while also enabling new forms of surveillance and
sanction. The seam is not inherently emancipatory. It is diagnostic.
The political stakes emerge at scale.
When charging spikes, credibility can outrun explanation. Artifacts can accumulate rapid
legitimacy because they align with platform logics of virality, aesthetic familiarity, or affective
immediacy. Under such conditions, institutional verification lags behind recognition. Authority
becomes decoupled from deliberation.
When charging implodes, legitimacy can collapse around a single detected seam. A hint of
AI assistance, a visible edit, an optimization cue can trigger disproportionate withdrawal. The
artifact remains intact; its epistemic weight evaporates. The pathway contaminates the
product.
When charging dissolves, work persists as visible but weightless. It circulates without
accumulating durable credit. It is consumed but not cited, shared but not ratified,
acknowledged but not remembered. Dissolution is perhaps the most insidious outcome: not
scandal but quiet irrelevance.
These are not aesthetic footnotes. They are structural conditions of knowledge-making,
art-making, and ritual meaning-making under generative infrastructures.
The Sealed Card Protocol ultimately argues that legitimacy under generative conditions
cannot be secured by new purity tests. Prohibitions, blanket bans, or nostalgic returns to
solitary origin do not resolve the asymmetry between infrastructural amplification and
localized responsibility. Instead, the paper proposes a politics of mediation with teeth. Such a
politics would require institutions to evaluate accountable procedure rather than inferred

22

purity. It would mean distributing responsibility proportionally to infrastructural power rather
than reflexively localizing blame at the most visible node. It would entail recognizing that
visible optimization is not inherently contamination and that distributed cognition does not
automatically nullify authorship.
This is not a call to dissolve responsibility. On the contrary, it insists upon responsibility that
is traceable, inspectable, and proportionate. But it refuses the moralization of mediation as
such.
The open question the paper leaves deliberately unresolved concerns institutional
adaptability. Can legitimacy regimes evolve to accommodate distributed production without
collapsing into suspicion? Can authorship be reconceptualized as coordination without
erasing accountability? Can charging stabilize under conditions where seams are more
frequently sensed?
The glitch will not disappear. Generative infrastructures ensure that mediation remains
perceptible. The question is whether the seam becomes a site of perpetual policing or a site
of procedural recalibration.
The Sealed Card Protocol does not claim to solve this tension. It names it. It renders it
observable. It argues that the decisive struggle of the generative era is not over whether
machines can write, draw, or synthesize. It is over how institutions decide whose mediated
work is allowed to count—and whether pathway visibility will remain grounds for punishment
or become grounds for accountable recognition.
Legitimacy has always been a governance project. Generative AI makes that project visible.
