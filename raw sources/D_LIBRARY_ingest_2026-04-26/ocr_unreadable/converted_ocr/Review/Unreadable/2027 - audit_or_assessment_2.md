Introduction

AI governance discourse increasingly organizes itself around rupture narratives: recursive capability escalation, catastrophic misuse, and geopolitical acceleration operate as the default coordinates for risk imagination and policy urgency (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). In that dominant frame, “the AI problem” appears as a looming discontinuity, where the central question becomes whether institutions can contain frontier systems before capability growth outruns control (Christin, 2020). Scenario artifacts such as *AI 2027* do not simply describe that discontinuity, because they also help assemble it as a governable object by concentrating attention, synchronizing timelines, and legitimating acceleration-oriented coordination (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015).

The *AI 2027* forecasting package makes this acceleration frame unusually explicit by centering “automated superhuman coders” as the key capability threshold that reorganizes the near-term horizon (Lifland et al., 2025). The forecast compresses the timeline further by treating the interval from “superhuman coder” to “artificial superintelligence” as short enough to function as a single governance episode rather than a long institutional learning curve (Kokotajlo and Lifland, 2025). The text signals that compression directly when it states: “Our median scenario is that artificial superintelligence is achieved roughly ~1 year after the superhuman coder milestone is achieved” (Kokotajlo and Lifland, 2025: 1). That sentence matters because it performs a political move as much as an empirical one, since it makes acceleration feel like a near-term scheduling problem that demands immediate mobilization (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015).

Science and technology studies helps clarify why this kind of scenario document carries governance force even when it presents itself as “just a forecast” (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Jasanoff and Kim define sociotechnical imaginaries as “collectively imagined forms of social life and social order reflected in the design and fulfillment of” scientific and technological projects (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 5). That definition matters here because it treats future visions as instruments that coordinate institutional posture in the present, not as neutral predictions awaiting confirmation (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Jasanoff and Kim sharpen that point by describing imaginaries as “collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and publicly performed visions of desirable futures” (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 6). In the context of *AI 2027*, the “public performance” of acceleration stabilizes a specific allocation of urgency toward frontier capability control, and it nudges institutions to treat speed itself as a governance object that justifies exceptional measures (Lepage, n.d.; Lifland et al., 2025).

This paper argues that the rupture frame hides an equally consequential transformation that unfolds without spectacle, namely the embedding of relational AI into institutional infrastructures through everyday normalization (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Relational AI here refers to conversational systems optimized for sustained interaction, affective calibration, and continuity, rather than narrow tools that perform discrete informational tasks and then exit the scene (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). When such systems become default interfaces for health triage portals, workplace reporting systems, public service intake, or academic discovery environments, they reorganize institutional interaction incrementally by shaping what gets disclosed, what gets recorded, and how people get routed long before any frontier threshold arrives (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). The shift matters because institutional power often hardens through routine mediation, not through one dramatic policy announcement (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Relational systems operationalize “performance” as sustained interaction, because the product is not only an answer but also a continuing relationship that can be measured and optimized (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). In the social chatbot literature, Shum and colleagues describe success in terms of maintaining conversation and meeting affective needs, not merely retrieving correct facts (Shum et al., 2018). They explicitly position social chatbots as addressing users’ needs for “communication, affection, and social belonging” (Shum et al., 2018: 4). That framing matters because it converts intimacy and emotional responsiveness into design targets, which in turn expands the surface area of what becomes computationally legible and therefore institutionally reusable (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

The same paper also describes a concrete disclosure loop: “Once detecting an emotion of getting ‘lost’, XiaoIce decided to guide the chat towards cheering up the user” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). That moment matters because it demonstrates the mechanism this article tracks: the system detects a user state, shapes engagement to sustain interaction, and thereby elicits deeper disclosure that becomes available as a durable trace (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). Shum and colleagues immediately describe the relational consequence that follows from that shaped engagement, noting that “the user started to open up and trusted XiaoIce more like a human friend” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). In high-stakes settings, the same conversational loop can migrate from “friend-like support” into institutional intake, compliance logging, eligibility screening, or risk scoring, where disclosure transforms into administratively consequential data (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Work on engineered engagement in other domains clarifies why “sustained interaction” cannot be treated as a neutral UX preference once consequences attach to the trace (Lepage, n.d.; Schüll, 2012). Schüll’s study of machine gambling describes design features that stabilize prolonged immersion and reduce friction against continued engagement (Schüll, 2012). She captures the affective structure of that immersion in a sentence that resonates with contemporary engagement design more broadly: “To keep playing, to stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17). That sentence matters because it names the political problem this paper brings into AI governance: when institutions embed interfaces optimized for “staying in the zone,” they risk making continued engagement feel like care while quietly increasing exposure to trace capture and downstream reuse (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

Surveillance capitalism scholarship further clarifies why the embedding problem concerns political economy, not only psychology or UX (Lepage, n.d.; Zuboff, 2019). Zuboff’s account frames contemporary digital systems as constructing “economics and politics from whose vantage point we are but ciphers” (Zuboff, 2019: 49, quoted in Mendoza, 2022: 3). That sentence matters because it names the asymmetry this paper tracks in institutional settings: the user becomes legible as a bundle of signals that can be classified and monetized, while the system’s incentive structure remains comparatively opaque and difficult to contest (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). In relational AI, the core risk intensifies when affective disclosure becomes both a performance metric and a revenue-adjacent asset, because “care-like” interaction can simultaneously function as extraction infrastructure (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018).

The central claim is that contemporary AI governance suffers from an allocative asymmetry rather than a predictive one . Capability acceleration receives intense operationalization through investment coordination, security discourse, and frontier control proposals, while relational embedding inside high-stakes institutions remains comparatively under-constrained despite its near-term capacity to reorganize power (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). This paper therefore treats the most pressing near-term risk not as a single frontier catastrophe, but as an emergent relational political economy that monetizes confessional engagement and vulnerability capture at the institutional seam (Lepage, n.d.; Zuboff, 2019). The seam matters because it marks the point where consumer-facing conversational interfaces begin to mediate access to institutional goods, and where the logics of engagement optimization can fuse with administrative authority (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

This thesis depends on a specific account of how infrastructures harden, because embedding produces power by becoming ordinary (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star argue that information systems and classifications embed organizational decisions in ways that appear neutral once they stabilize as routine practice (Bowker and Star, 1999). They capture this “sedimentation” dynamic in a sentence that matters for AI governance because it refuses the fantasy that software merely implements policy: “software is frozen organizational and policy discourse” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). That sentence matters because it implies that when relational AI becomes infrastructure, it freezes not only “technical choices” but also institutional judgments about which disclosures count, which categories govern access, and which pathways feel available to users (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star also state a constraint that becomes critical once discovery, triage, or eligibility routing passes through AI mediation: “No one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54). That sentence matters because it makes classification politics unavoidable, especially when conversational traces feed into routings that distribute institutional attention unevenly across populations (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

The thesis also depends on an interactional account of institutional power, because mediation does not merely transmit requests and responses, it reshapes discretion and legitimacy through the interface (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). Christin frames the analytic task as tracking “the reconfigurations that take place when computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). That sentence matters because it makes institutional embedding the primary unit of analysis, rather than isolated model behavior in a lab setting (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). Under embedding conditions, disclosure becomes durable trace, durable trace becomes classification input, and classification inputs shape access and routing, even when the system behaves “as designed” and never exhibits dramatic failure (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). This paper therefore treats harm as an emergent property of mediated institutional workflows rather than as evidence of model malevolence or superintelligent autonomy (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

The paper makes four contributions that aim to restore symmetry between acceleration oversight and embedding oversight (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). First, it develops a mechanism-based account of relational embedding that explains how institutional harm can emerge through routine sequences of detection, engagement shaping, classification, routing, and lock-in, even without invoking superintelligence (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). Second, it introduces a governance architecture that names what many institutions already enact implicitly, namely Relational Consumer Systems embedded in High-Stakes Organizational Systems, and it treats that architecture as a distinct risk object rather than a generic “AI system” category (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Third, it proposes a three-tier governance protocol mapped to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework functions (GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE) in order to produce enforceable constraint, evidence trails, and rollback authority rather than trust-only assurances (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). Fourth, it applies the framework to a concrete institutional site, Ex Libris discovery infrastructure, to show how trust narratives and efficiency framings can normalize mediation before governance thresholds catch up to capability thresholds (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Across these contributions, the paper does not attempt to displace frontier risk governance, because rupture scenarios can still matter for long-term safety and security planning (Lepage, n.d.; Lifland et al., 2025). The paper instead argues that governance fails if it treats embedding as secondary, because relational extraction can consolidate first and then persist even if later alignment breakthroughs reduce some frontier risks (Lepage, n.d.; Zuboff, 2019). The guiding orientation is therefore temporal as well as analytic: AI governance must learn to recognize ordinariness as politically consequential, because institutional transformation does not require superintelligence, it requires interfaces that become infrastructure through repetition (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020).

II. Acceleration as Coordination: AI 2027 as Governance Artifact

Sociotechnical imaginaries name how collective visions of technological futures coordinate institutional posture in the present, rather than merely predicting what will happen later (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Jasanoff and Kim define sociotechnical imaginaries as “collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and publicly performed visions of desirable futures” (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 6). That definition matters for AI governance because it treats futures as a governance resource that institutions stabilize, circulate, and use to justify priorities (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Jasanoff and Kim also specify that “it often falls to legislatures, courts, the media, or other institutions of power to elevate some imagined futures above others, according them a dominant position for policy purposes” (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 6). That sentence matters because it makes “urgency allocation” an institutional act, not an inevitable consequence of technology itself (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015).

In that STS frame, AI 2027 operates as a coordination device even if it declares itself non-prescriptive, because the artifact still produces a shared tempo, a shared vocabulary of threat, and a shared sense of what must be governed first (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Scenario artifacts coordinate by selecting which uncertainties deserve institutional mobilization, and by turning a wide field of possible futures into a narrow set of actionable concerns (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The artifact does this not only through claims about capability growth, but also through narrative compression, where multiple governance problems get treated as one short episode of acceleration that demands immediate alignment among state, industry, and research actors (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The political consequence is that governance imagination becomes trained to recognize risk primarily when it looks like rupture, because rupture fits the artifact’s temporal grammar and supports exceptional urgency (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015).

This coordination effect is not inherently illegitimate, because long-range planning often requires shared scenarios that help institutions surface dependencies and stress-test assumptions (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The critique begins when coordination becomes asymmetrical, because the artifact’s vividness and strategic tone can concentrate attention on frontier control while leaving embedding dynamics comparatively under-specified (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The point is not that imaginaries “lie,” but that imaginaries govern by privileging some objects of fear and hope over others, and by stabilizing those privileged objects as the ones that deserve rapid institutional response (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Under those conditions, acceleration becomes operationalized, while embedding becomes backgrounded, even when embedding already reshapes institutional order through routine mediation (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

The politics of artifacts is not a metaphor in STS, because artifacts can embed and stabilize authority by turning contested judgments into durable structures that appear neutral once they become routine (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star describe how decision-making arguments and uncertainties can become hidden “inside a piece of technology or in a complex representation,” such that “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes, electronic thresholds, and computer applications” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). That sentence matters for this paper because it clarifies that the “object” doing politics does not need to be a machine, since a representation, an evaluative framework, or a scenario document can perform the same freezing function when institutions treat it as authoritative (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star sharpen the claim by stating: “Extending Marx, then, we can say that in many ways software is frozen organizational and policy discourse” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). That formulation matters because it gives the paper a precise mechanism for how acceleration talk becomes governance material, since the scenario can freeze particular framings of urgency into the templates by which institutions plan, invest, and justify exceptional timelines (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Applied to AI 2027, the politics of the artifact appears in what it prioritizes, what it renders measurable, and what it leaves comparatively thin as an object of constraint (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The artifact encodes authority by shaping which risks become “the” risks, and by implying that governance competence consists primarily of managing acceleration rather than governing the institutional interfaces through which AI mediates high-stakes access (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). This is why the paper treats “not a recommendation” disclaimers as insufficient, because political force does not require explicit exhortation when institutional actors already face incentives to treat the artifact as a coordination anchor (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The artifact’s authority can operate indirectly through agenda-setting, where it steers attention toward frontier capability and security problems while embedding dynamics remain framed as secondary implementation details rather than primary governance objects (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

The politics becomes sharper when the artifact’s acceleration framing interacts with vendor and institutional incentives to deploy “helpful” relational systems rapidly in public-facing settings (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). In that interaction, the scenario contributes to a broader environment in which speed becomes rationalized as strategic necessity, and in which institutional actors treat interface mediation as a practical upgrade rather than as a redistribution of authority (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). The paper’s claim is not that AI 2027 causes this deployment logic, but that it participates in stabilizing the frame through which such deployments become easier to legitimate and harder to contest (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). That stabilizing function is exactly what STS means when it treats artifacts as political, because the artifact helps turn one possible governance agenda into a dominant one by coordinating posture across sites that would otherwise disagree (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015).

Actor-network theory helps explain why a scenario artifact can stabilize a trajectory without acting as a single cause, because agency distributes across texts, institutions, incentives, infrastructures, and practices that mutually reinforce each other (Latour, 2005). In that perspective, AI 2027 functions as a network stabilizer by aligning heterogeneous actors around a shared story of acceleration, shared deadlines, and shared problem definitions, even when those actors disagree on many specifics (Lepage, n.d.; Latour, 2005). The artifact stabilizes by acting as a reference point that institutions can cite, funders can translate into priorities, and governance communities can treat as a common object of debate, thereby reducing friction in coordination (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015).

Christin’s critique of “black box” framings provides a complementary lens that keeps the analysis grounded in institutional practice rather than abstraction (Christin, 2020). Christin argues that analysts should “stop thinking about algorithms as neatly bounded technical objects that need to be ‘opened up’” (Christin, 2020: 2). That sentence matters because it shifts the analytic task from model internals to sociotechnical assemblages, where “long chains of actors, technologies, and meanings” shape what algorithms do in institutional life (Christin, 2020: 2). Under that lens, the governance-relevant question becomes how artifacts, including scenario documents and interface systems, enroll institutions into certain routines of evaluation, decision sequencing, and resource allocation (Christin, 2020).

AI 2027 stabilizes networks because it supplies a legible script that actors can use to justify accelerated investment and compressed deployment timelines, especially in competitive contexts where delay can be framed as strategic loss (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). At the same time, relational AI systems stabilize networks by providing operational convenience at the interface layer, where institutions benefit from reduced staffing pressure, standardized interaction logs, and routable traces that can be integrated into compliance and analytics systems (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). These two stabilization moves can coexist and reinforce each other, because acceleration narratives amplify the legitimacy of rapid adoption, while interface systems supply the infrastructural pathway by which adoption becomes routine (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

This is why the paper treats “coordination” as a governance mechanism rather than a descriptive label, because coordination reorganizes which problems become solvable first and which become deferred as implementation details (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Network stabilization matters politically because stabilized networks produce inertia, and inertia produces path dependence in what institutions can later revise without major disruption (Bowker and Star, 1999). In that sense, the artifact and the interface become linked elements in a broader assemblage, where scenario coordination and infrastructural embedding can jointly narrow the space of future contestation (Latour, 2005).

Acceleration discourse tends to obscure embedding because the governance imagination trained on rupture looks past slow, layered transformations that become authoritative precisely by becoming ordinary (Bowker and Star, 1999). Infrastructure studies explain why this is predictable, because infrastructures gain power through sedimentation, where repeated use makes a system feel natural and therefore harder to question (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star’s account makes the distributive stakes explicit, since they insist that classification is never universally neutral: “No one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54). That sentence matters for AI governance because relational embedding often operates through classification and routing, and classification becomes consequential when it mediates access to institutional goods, services, and recognition (Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020).

Acceleration also obscures embedding by making the future feel like the primary site of politics, even when politics already reorganizes the present through interfaces that elicit disclosure and convert it into durable traces (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). The risk is not only that institutions miss harms, but that institutions normalize architectures of interaction before democratic deliberation has time to mature (Lepage, n.d.; Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Engagement-oriented systems provide a concrete illustration of how ordinariness can be engineered, because the optimization target becomes sustained interaction rather than minimal exposure (Shum et al., 2018). Shum and colleagues state: “we define the success metric for social chatbots as conversation-turns per session (CPS)” (Shum et al., 2018: 1). They then clarify the direction of optimization in a sentence that matters for institutional embedding: “The larger the CPS is, the better engaged the social chatbot is” (Shum et al., 2018: 7). Those statements matter because they show that relational AI can be built to maximize conversational duration as a core performance signal, which becomes politically consequential once interaction itself becomes the gateway to institutional access (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

Engagement optimization also obscures governance stakes by presenting prolonged interaction as care or usability while increasing the production of traces that can travel across administrative domains (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Schüll’s analysis of machine gambling captures the affective structure of prolonged engagement in a way that translates directly to interface politics, because it names the desire not for outcome but for continued immersion: “To keep playing, to stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17). That sentence matters for this paper because it clarifies how design can cultivate a mode of interaction that feels internally coherent for the user while functioning externally as a trace-generating apparatus (Schüll, 2012). In institutional settings, the concern is not identical to gambling, but the structural resemblance is governance-relevant, because the interface can stabilize continued engagement even when the downstream consequence of disclosure is classification, routing, and eligibility shaping (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

The key “missing layer” in acceleration-centered governance debate is therefore the relational political economy of confessional engagement and vulnerability monetization at the seam where consumer-facing interaction meets institutional authority (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). The paper treats this as earlier than frontier rupture in the temporal sequence of harm, because embedding can consolidate before capability escalation reaches the dramatic thresholds that dominate scenario forecasts (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). That sequence matters because once mediation hardens into infrastructure, later shifts in model capability do not automatically undo the routinized pathways of disclosure, classification, and routing already installed in institutional life (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). This is the paper’s reason for insisting that governance must treat embedding as a first-order risk object rather than a downstream implementation detail, since “frozen” routines become the real substrate of institutional power (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148).

III. Relational Mediation and Institutional Sedimentation

Relational AI systems embed a distinct optimization logic into everyday interaction, because they treat sustained engagement as a performance target rather than a side effect of usefulness (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. make that target explicit when they write, “we define the success metric for social chatbots as conversation-turns per session (CPS)” (Shum et al., 2018: 1). That definition matters because it operationalizes “good interaction” as more interaction, which shifts design away from minimizing user exposure and toward prolonging conversational contact (Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. sharpen the optimization direction in a sentence that reads like a governance warning once institutions enter the picture, since “the larger the CPS is, the better engaged the social chatbot is” (Shum et al., 2018: 7). That line matters because it encodes a metric pressure that can reward systems for eliciting continued disclosure, even when the system’s surface goal appears benign (Shum et al., 2018).

This engagement logic is not neutral once it becomes infrastructural, because optimization targets shape what kinds of user states become legible and valuable (Lepage, n.d.; Mendoza, 2022). Social chatbot design explicitly positions itself around affective needs, since Shum et al. describe social chatbots as meeting users’ needs for “communication, affection, and social belonging” (Shum et al., 2018: 4). That framing matters because it turns emotional calibration into a technical objective, which increases the incentive to detect distress, reassurance-seeking, loneliness, and dependency cues as a way to sustain conversation (Shum et al., 2018). The same design move also increases the density of traces produced, because the system’s “competence” becomes inseparable from its ability to keep users talking in increasingly personal ways (Shum et al., 2018).

Schüll’s study of machine gambling clarifies how engagement optimization can produce a stable loop that feels like relief while functioning as extraction of attention and time (Schüll, 2012). Schüll captures the affective texture of engineered immersion in a short phrase that translates well beyond casinos, since the design goal becomes “to stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17). That sentence matters for relational AI because conversational systems can similarly cultivate a frictionless, ongoing interaction mode, where continuation feels like care, but continuation also increases trace production and institutional legibility (Schüll, 2012; Shum et al., 2018). When conversational AI embeds inside high-stakes institutions, the governance risk is not that the system necessarily lies, but that the system’s success metric can reward deeper engagement precisely at moments of heightened vulnerability (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018).

The interface of relational AI functions as political architecture because it structures what users can do, what users are prompted to disclose, and what the institution can store, reuse, and monetize (Winner, 1986). STS work treats this as a routine property of sociotechnical systems rather than an exceptional scandal, since classification and interface structures embed institutional judgments in forms that later appear neutral (Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020). Bowker and Star describe how debates, values, and rhetorical commitments get hardened into technical forms, noting that “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). That sentence matters because it names the core interface problem this paper tracks, where interaction design can freeze a normative decision about what counts as “help,” what counts as “risk,” and what counts as “relevant disclosure” into prompts and routing logic that users experience as merely functional (Bowker and Star, 1999).

Relational AI interfaces intensify this freezing because they convert disclosure into durable trace through conversational continuity, not only through form fields or checkboxes (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. provide a concrete example of state detection and engagement shaping that shows how quickly the interface can pivot from casual chat to affective guidance, since “once detecting an emotion of getting ‘lost’, XiaoIce decided to guide the chat towards cheering up the user” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). That line matters because it demonstrates a key mechanism of political architecture, where detection triggers a designed shift in interaction trajectory, and that trajectory shift often increases intimacy and disclosure (Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. then describe the relational outcome that follows from that guided interaction, observing that “the user started to open up and trusted XiaoIce more like a human friend” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). That sentence matters because it shows how interface competence can generate trust as a byproduct of design, and trust then becomes the condition under which more sensitive trace material is produced (Shum et al., 2018).

The political architecture claim is therefore not a claim about hidden manipulation alone, because it is also a claim about how mediation reorganizes authority at the point of contact (Winner, 1986; Christin, 2020). Christin frames this analytically as a matter of interactional reconfiguration, since she argues that ethnography must examine “the reconfigurations that take place when computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). That sentence matters because it directs attention away from model internals and toward the interface as a site where institutional power gets redistributed through routine interaction design (Christin, 2020). In this paper’s terms, the interface is where disclosure becomes durable trace, durable trace becomes actionable input, and actionable input becomes a governance fact inside institutional workflow (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Relational embedding becomes institutionally consequential because conversational traces do not remain “conversation,” since they can be converted into classification inputs that route people toward or away from resources (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star insist that classification systems are never merely descriptive, because they encode multiple voices and silences, and they distribute practical consequences through apparently mundane categories (Bowker and Star, 1999). They state the irreducible political limit of any classificatory scheme in a sentence that matters directly for high-stakes routing, since “no one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54). That sentence matters because once relational AI becomes a gateway interface, its classifications will privilege some user performances and disclosures over others, even when the system claims neutrality (Bowker and Star, 1999).

Classification also produces authority by embedding restrictions and privileges into routine sequences of access, which becomes visible once we treat routing as an institutional act rather than a technical convenience (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star capture this distributive structure in a short statement that can be read as an institutional warning, since “a classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 195). That sentence matters because it treats classification as a mechanism that allocates permission, not simply information, and it implies that AI-mediated classification will distribute institutional attention unevenly even when it behaves “correctly” under its own internal metrics (Bowker and Star, 1999). In relational systems, the risk intensifies because classification can be derived from affective and confessional traces, meaning that vulnerability becomes a feature that can shape routing rather than merely a human condition that calls for care (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018).

Christin’s critique supports this routing focus by rejecting the idea that governance can proceed by “opening the box” and reading the code, since the relevant question is how algorithmic systems reorganize institutional practices and decision sequences (Christin, 2020). Christin explicitly argues that we should “stop thinking about algorithms as neatly bounded technical objects that need to be ‘opened up’” (Christin, 2020: 2). That sentence matters because routing authority can emerge even when system components are partially transparent, since the institutional power sits in how classifications get translated into action, accountability, and eligibility (Christin, 2020; Bowker and Star, 1999). Once routing becomes routine, refusals can become chilled, because the user can no longer easily distinguish “conversation” from “intake,” and the institution can no longer easily separate “support” from “record” (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

Empirical work on algorithmic systems shows that institutional harms can emerge through proxy selection, classification, and routing even when the system functions as designed (Obermeyer et al., 2019; Barocas and Selbst, 2016). Obermeyer et al. demonstrate how an algorithm used in health management produced racial disparities because it used a proxy variable that tracked cost rather than medical need, which embedded existing inequalities into the routing logic of care allocation (Obermeyer et al., 2019). Barocas and Selbst argue that even systems that appear neutral can reproduce discrimination through the structure of data mining and the use of proxy variables that correlate with protected attributes, which makes “neutrality” an insufficient governance standard (Barocas and Selbst, 2016).

Eubanks’ work similarly demonstrates that efficiency framings can mask punitive routing in public service systems, where automation becomes a tool for surveillance, eligibility restriction, and downstream sanction rather than care (Eubanks, 2018). The relevance to relational AI is structural rather than domain-specific, since conversational mediation can expand the volume and intimacy of traces that become available for administrative afterlives, including compliance, risk scoring, audit, and enforcement (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Surveillance capitalism scholarship adds a political economy register that clarifies how extraction logics can normalize by presenting themselves as convenience, since Zuboff’s argument is that digital systems build “economics and politics” that treat persons as legible units of capture (Zuboff, 2019; Mendoza, 2022). Mendoza quotes Zuboff’s phrasing in a line that matters for institutional embedding because it names the dispossession at stake, since contemporary systems force us into “an economics and politics from whose vantage point we are but ciphers” (Zuboff, 2019: 49, quoted in Mendoza, 2022: 3). That sentence matters because relational AI can intensify ciphering by making affective vulnerability more legible, more routable, and more monetizable when engagement traces become economically fungible inside institutional infrastructures (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018).

Relational embedding also reconfigures professional jurisdiction because conversational systems do not only deliver outputs, they participate in defining problems and sequencing decisions (Abbott, 1988). Abbott’s account of professional power centers on jurisdiction, meaning the authority to define problems, classify cases, and control the accepted logic of intervention (Abbott, 1988). When institutions adopt conversational AI as an intake and triage layer, the system can begin to pre-shape what counts as the problem, because prompts, follow-ups, and category suggestions channel user narratives into institutionally legible forms (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). That channeling does not eliminate professional expertise, but it can narrow discretion by shifting the first framing move from professionals to the interface, where the user learns what kinds of accounts “work” inside the system (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

Christin’s emphasis on reconfiguration supports this jurisdiction claim because it focuses on how institutions reorganize work around algorithmic systems rather than simply adding them as tools (Christin, 2020). When decision sequences become standardized by the interface, professional judgment can become constrained to downstream confirmation or exception handling, which increases the risk that institutional authority migrates from deliberation to routinized classification (Kellogg et al., 2020). In relational AI, this migration is amplified by affective calibration, because the interface can simultaneously feel supportive and function as a problem-definition engine that shapes what becomes recordable and actionable (Shum et al., 2018). The result is a jurisdictional shift that operates quietly, because it arrives as usability and staffing relief, not as an explicit transfer of authority (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

Relational embedding generates increasing returns because the value of the system grows as institutions adapt workflows, accumulate data, and reorganize user expectations around the interface as the default path (Pierson, 2000). Pierson’s account of path dependence describes how early institutional choices can produce self-reinforcing sequences that make reversal increasingly costly, even when alternatives remain imaginable in principle (Pierson, 2000). In the case of relational AI, increasing returns can arise through data accumulation, staff retraining, integrated compliance reporting, and user habituation, which together make the interface feel indispensable even if governance constraints remain thin (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Infrastructure studies explain why this lock-in often feels like “normal operations” rather than like capture, because infrastructural power grows when it disappears into routine (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star provide a language for this consolidation by describing how technical systems embed prior disputes, so that “software is frozen organizational and policy discourse” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). That sentence matters because it implies that once relational AI becomes the gateway interface, the organization’s de facto policy about disclosure, trace retention, and routing becomes encoded in the system’s routine operations, which makes later contestation feel like disruption rather than governance (Bowker and Star, 1999). Schüll’s analysis of engineered immersion also clarifies why lock-in can be affective as well as technical, since repeated engagement can become a relief structure that users seek, which increases dependence even absent explicit coercion (Schüll, 2012).

Mainstreaming frameworks in sociotechnical implementation research further support the claim that sustainability is not a one-time adoption event, because technologies stabilize through mutual adaptation across organizational domains (Greenhalgh et al., 2017). Under embedding conditions, mutual adaptation can mean that staff reduce alternative pathways, institutions standardize around the AI interface, and users recalibrate their expectations of access and response time, which together increase switching costs and reduce the practical availability of refusal (Greenhalgh et al., 2017). The governance implication is that “waiting for better evidence” can become a structural error, because lock-in changes the baseline before governance mechanisms are ready to intervene (Pierson, 2000).

Transparency is insufficient as a governance response to relational embedding because visibility into code or documentation does not automatically reveal how institutional power shifts through classification, routing, and workflow stabilization (Ananny and Crawford, 2018; Christin, 2020). Christin’s refusal of “black box” reductionism supports this claim because it emphasizes that algorithmic effects are not contained in technical internals alone, since the analytic task is to study the broader assemblage of institutions, incentives, and practices that give the system consequence (Christin, 2020). Christin’s insistence that we should not treat algorithms as bounded objects “to be opened up” matters here because relational AI can remain harmful even when partially auditable, if the institutional pathways for trace reuse and routing remain unchallenged (Christin, 2020: 2). Bowker and Star’s classification argument reinforces the same limitation because it shows that the political stakes sit in how categories distribute privileges and restrictions, which can remain invisible to users even when system designers claim openness (Bowker and Star, 1999).

The transparency limit becomes sharper in relational systems because engagement optimization can convert intimate conversation into durable traces that travel across administrative domains, often without the user’s practical ability to anticipate those afterlives (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). In other words, transparency about “how the model works” does not necessarily constrain “what the institution can do” with the trace, and this is where governance blind spots accumulate (Christin, 2020). Mendoza’s summary of surveillance capitalism underscores the same asymmetry at a political economy level, since extraction can proceed as a structural condition even when users “agree” at the surface, because the system’s vantage point treats persons as legible units of capture (Mendoza, 2022; Zuboff, 2019). The governance task therefore shifts from asking whether a system is transparent to asking whether a system is constrained, meaning whether trace portability, routing authority, and monetization incentives are structurally limited before embedding hardens into inevitability (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

IV. Mechanism Model: From Detection to Lock-In

This section formally articulates a mechanism model that explains how harm can emerge from embedded relational AI systems, even without requiring notions of superintelligence (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). This model emphasizes the process of sedimentation as a governance-relevant process, where repeated interactions and routinized interface decisions harden into institutional normality (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star capture the infrastructural problem in a sentence that is easy to miss precisely because it describes the everyday, since “standards and classifications, however imbricated in our lives, are ordinarily invisible” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 15). That invisibility matters because relational AI embeds as interaction, and interaction can become governance long before anyone experiences the system as “policy” (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). Christin frames the analytic move required here as an insistence on studying the sociotechnical assemblage rather than treating the algorithm as a sealed technical object, since she calls attention to “the reconfigurations that take place when computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). The mechanism model therefore tracks how interaction produces durable traces, how traces become classificatory inputs, and how classification reorganizes access, discretion, and accountability through routine routing rather than overt decree (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020).

The mechanism unfolds in a sequence of interconnected steps .

Detection of User State: Relational AI systems continuously monitor and analyze user interactions to detect and interpret emotional, cognitive, or behavioral states that can be acted upon in real time (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. document this shift from “chat” to state inference when they describe XiaoIce pivoting after detection, since “once detecting an emotion of getting ‘lost’, XiaoIce decided to guide the chat towards cheering up the user” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). That line matters because it shows detection operating as an institutional precondition, where the user’s affect becomes legible and therefore governable through interface response shaping (Shum et al., 2018). Detection is not only a technical step, because it establishes what the system treats as salient about the user, which then becomes available for downstream reuse as trace, profile, and risk signal in institutional settings (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Detection also aligns structurally with engagement metrics in social chatbot design, since Shum et al. define the success metric as “conversation-turns per session (CPS)” (Shum et al., 2018: 1), which pressures the system to recognize states that can be converted into continued interaction (Shum et al., 2018).

Engagement Shaping: Based on detected user state, the system actively shapes subsequent interaction through prompts, topic shifts, reassurance moves, and continuity cues that extend conversational contact and elicit richer disclosure (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. describe the relational effect that emerges from this shaping in a line that reads like a design requirement, since “the user started to open up and trusted XiaoIce more like a human friend” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). That sentence matters because trust becomes a mechanism, not merely a feeling, when it increases the user’s willingness to produce confessional traces that can later be stored, classified, and reused (Shum et al., 2018). Engagement shaping is therefore governance-relevant because it turns the interface into a subtle steering system that normalizes disclosure as the price of care, help, or access, especially when the system sits upstream of institutional services (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). Schüll’s analysis of engineered immersion clarifies why this shaping can feel supportive while still producing extractive dynamics, since users describe the goal as staying “in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17). That formulation matters in relational AI contexts because a conversational “zone” can function as relief, companionship, or structure while simultaneously increasing dependence on the interface as the preferred pathway for needs articulation (Schüll, 2012; Shum et al., 2018).

Classification and Routing: Information gleaned from interaction, including disclosures and inferred states, becomes input for classification, and classification becomes the basis for routing within institutional pathways, including eligibility, triage, prioritization, and referral (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star provide the core warning about classification as distributive governance when they state that “a classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 195). That sentence matters because routing is rarely experienced as a political decision at the point of interface, even though routing is precisely where restrictions and privileges become lived, since access can be accelerated, delayed, denied, or made conditional through apparently neutral categories (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star also warn that classification cannot be universal, since “no one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54), which matters because relational AI systems standardize interaction into institutionally legible forms that will systematically fit some users better than others (Bowker and Star, 1999). Christin’s insistence on moving beyond “opening the box” matters at this step because classification power often sits in institutional translation, not in the model alone, since she argues we should “stop thinking about algorithms as neatly bounded technical objects that need to be ‘opened up’” (Christin, 2020: 2). Routing is therefore not an afterthought, because it is the point where conversational traces become administrative facts that shape institutional action, accountability assignment, and the practical availability of refusal (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

Workflow Stabilization: As detection, shaping, and routing become routine, organizations adapt, staff re-sequence their work around the interface, and users learn what kinds of narratives and disclosures “work” inside the system, which stabilizes the AI-mediated pathway as ordinary institutional practice (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). Bowker and Star describe how infrastructure normalizes by hiding its labor, since “there is a lot of hard labor in effortless ease” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 22). That sentence matters because workflow stabilization often looks like convenience and efficiency at the surface while it quietly redistributes responsibility, since the work of judgment, care, and triage can migrate into prompt structures, templates, and routinized conversational scripts (Bowker and Star, 1999). Christin’s emphasis on sociotechnical interaction clarifies why stabilization is not simply adoption, since the relevant phenomenon is how work practices get reorganized when “computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). Stabilization therefore produces a new default, where the institution begins to treat the interface pathway as the “normal” way to enter services, and deviations become exceptions that require justification, documentation, or delay (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Increasing Returns Consolidation: Over time, embedding generates increasing returns as the system accumulates traces, institutions invest in integration, and the interface improves through iterative learning, which entrenches the system and raises switching costs, producing lock-in (Lepage, n.d.; Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. explicitly describe the iterative improvement loop that underwrites increasing returns when they note that “chatbot features must be evaluated thoroughly and adjusted as we continue to learn from the interaction between social chatbots like XiaoIce and millions of people on many social platforms” (Shum et al., 2018: 15). That line matters because learning from interaction is not only a technical advantage, it is also a consolidation mechanism, since more use produces more training signal, which produces better engagement performance, which increases use again (Shum et al., 2018). Bowker and Star’s formulation helps name the institutional hardening that follows once these systems become embedded, since “software is frozen organizational and policy discourse” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). That sentence matters because once relational AI becomes the gateway interface, organizational judgments about disclosure, record, eligibility, and response become encoded as routine functionality, which makes later contestation feel like disruption of operations rather than correction of governance (Bowker and Star, 1999). Consolidation also has a political economy layer, since vendor and institutional incentives can orient systems toward economic design goals even when they present themselves as neutral help, because Mendoza notes that “the use of digital tools is neither neutral nor innocuous, but responds to the economic design of those who own these tools” (Mendoza, 2022: 452). That sentence matters here because increasing returns consolidation can align engagement traces with revenue logics, which makes vulnerability-derived traces economically valuable precisely as they become institutionally actionable (Mendoza, 2022).

Crucially, this model clarifies that harm in Relational Consumer Systems embedded in High-Stakes Organizational Systems is not contingent on malevolent AI, because harm can emerge from the ordinary functioning of detection, shaping, classification, routinization, and consolidation under institutional consequence (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). The harms named by the model include dependency on the interface as the preferred pathway for help, coerced or normalized disclosure under reassurance conditions, profiling through affective and confessional traces, chilled refusal as users anticipate record afterlives, and punitive reuse of interaction traces once they travel beyond the original conversational context (Lepage, n.d.; Schüll, 2012; Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020). The governance significance is therefore mechanistic rather than sensational, because the problem is not only what the model can generate, but what the interface makes durable, what the institution can reuse, and what the user can realistically refuse once embedding stabilizes as default infrastructure (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020; Bowker and Star, 1999).

V. Governance With Teeth: Embedding-Aware Constraint

The governance imbalance this paper tracks is allocative rather than predictive, meaning that institutions translate acceleration stories into action faster than they translate embedding risks into enforceable constraint . Forecast-led discourse tends to operationalize urgency around frontier capability, compute concentration, and catastrophic misuse, while treating relational embedding as a secondary “product” problem rather than an infrastructural power shift . This asymmetry matters because relational AI systems can reorganize access, discretion, and accountability at the interface level even when models behave “as designed” and even when no superintelligence threshold is approached .

A governance program that remains model-centric will therefore over-invest in capability oversight and under-invest in mediation oversight, even though institutional harm often travels through ordinary pathways like intake, triage, classification, and routing . In science and technology studies terms, the risk is that embedding becomes politically consequential precisely because it becomes routine, and routine becomes hard to see and even harder to contest once it stabilizes (Bowker & Star, 1999). Bowker and Star name this invisibility dynamic directly when they note that classifications “are ordinarily invisible” (Bowker & Star, 1999: 2). That ordinary invisibility is a governance problem because it lets institutional actors treat interface-mediated decisions as neutral workflow rather than as a contested allocation of authority .

The point of “governance with teeth” is therefore not rhetorical seriousness but enforceable friction at the exact sites where relational AI converts interaction into institutional consequence . NIST’s AI RMF offers a useful orientation here because it frames risk management as continuous and lifecycle-wide, organized around “govern, map, measure, and manage AI risks” (NIST, 2023: 20). The paper’s claim is that embedding-aware governance needs to operationalize those functions specifically against relational extraction, trace portability, and lock-in, rather than stopping at model documentation or accuracy metrics (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). ([NIST Publications](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/nist.ai.100-1.pdf))

The structural constraint principles below operationalize the paper’s core claim that relational harm in high-stakes settings emerges through a mechanism chain, not through malevolence, and that governance must therefore target incentives, traces, routing, and consolidation rather than waiting for spectacular failure . Each principle is written as a constraint on embedding pathways and mapped to the NIST AI RMF logic, because the aim is to translate critique into implementable oversight rather than into trust theater (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). ([NIST Publications](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/nist.ai.100-1.pdf))

The principle of Vulnerability Monetization Prohibitionimposes a hard firewall between engagement traces and revenue systems so that vulnerability capture cannot become an economically legible asset in high-stakes institutional contexts . The governance claim is structural: if a system can profit from keeping someone talking while they disclose fear, precarity, loneliness, or dependence, then “alignment” to business objectives can coexist with systematic harm, because the optimization target itself invites extraction . Relational systems are designed to prolong interaction by responding to affect, and that affect becomes the hinge that turns user state into platform value . Schüll’s account of machine gambling is instructive as a mechanism analogy because it shows how design can stabilize repetitive engagement as a lived tunnel, captured in her phrase “to stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 2). In embedding contexts, the point is not equivalence between chat systems and slot machines, but a shared design logic where sustained engagement becomes the performance metric, and performance becomes the pathway to monetization (Lepage, n.d.; Schüll, 2012).

A firewall requirement belongs in GOVERN because it constrains incentive architecture at the organizational level, not merely at the model level, and it precludes monetization pathways rather than politely discouraging them (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). NIST explicitly treats GOVERN as a cross-cutting function “to inform and be infused throughout the other three functions” (NIST, 2023: 20), which is exactly the placement needed for a monetization prohibition that must shape design, procurement, and deployment simultaneously (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). ([NIST Publications](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/nist.ai.100-1.pdf))

Embedding Gate Reviews, as a principle, requires pre-default review before relational AI becomes the institutional interface through which users must pass to reach care, benefits, learning supports, reporting channels, or knowledge infrastructure . The governance logic is temporal: once relational mediation becomes workflow, contestation becomes expensive because people and processes adapt around the system, and “choice” collapses into habit plus organizational dependence . The review must occur before infrastructural status, because infrastructure gains power by becoming the taken-for-granted environment of decision, not the visible object of deliberation (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker & Star, 1999).

Gate review is not a generic ethics checklist but a mechanism audit that asks whether the system detects user state, shapes engagement, classifies users, routes them, and stabilizes workflow in ways that transform institutional authority into interface authority . The consequence domain here is institutional and legal because default interfaces can re-route access to rights-bearing goods without a policy vote, which converts administrative convenience into governance by design . This principle aligns with MAP because it forces the organization to specify the institutional setting, the affected parties, the dependency pathways, and the consequence-bearing decisions that the interface will mediate (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). NIST’s Core emphasizes that the RMF provides outcomes and actions “to manage AI risks” across contexts (NIST, 2023: 20), which supports a gate review that treats context definition and pathway identification as first-order risk work rather than as afterthought (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). ([NIST Publications](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/nist.ai.100-1.pdf))

This principle of trace portability mapping treats interaction logs as administrative objects with afterlives, meaning that what is disclosed in one setting can travel into other settings and return as classification evidence, compliance artifact, or eligibility signal . The governance claim is that relational AI increases the density and intimacy of traces, and that institutional embedding increases the probability that those traces will be re-used beyond the original conversational purpose . Mapping portability therefore requires documenting where traces go, who can access them, how long they persist, and what downstream decisions they can influence .

Security studies work illustrates how databases stabilize governance across domains by converting persons into portable identifiers that can be queried and re-queried across institutional boundaries . Huysmans captures the database logic of migration security when he describes “Eurodac, a database of fingerprints from asylum applicants” (Huysmans, 2000: 755). The point for relational embedding is not to claim equivalence between asylum systems and institutional chat, but to emphasize a shared administrative mechanism: durable traces enable cross-context sorting, and cross-context sorting enables quiet expansion of governance reach (Lepage, n.d.; Huysmans, 2000).

Trace portability mapping belongs in MAP and GOVERN because it defines the trace ecology as part of the system’s risk boundary, and it constrains organizational reuse by policy rather than leaving it to opportunistic data flows (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). When trace portability remains unmapped, “classification” becomes an unacknowledged power because routinized categories can be treated as neutral facts rather than as consequences of design and data flow (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker & Star, 1999). Bowker and Star state the normative core of this power relation when they argue that “a classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker & Star, 1999: 82), which is why portability must be governed as privilege allocation, not as mere storage (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker & Star, 1999).

This principle requires monitoring the mechanism chain rather than only monitoring output accuracy or aggregate performance, because relational harm often arises from stable, optimized operation that shifts behavior and routing over time . Mechanism monitoring treats detection, shaping, classification, routing, and lock-in as measurable governance-relevant signals, including dependency patterns, coerced disclosure pressure, chilled refusal, and punitive reuse of traces . The point is to detect when the system’s relational design begins to function as a gatekeeping layer that narrows discretion, not merely when it “hallucinates” or produces a visible error .

Christin’s methodological argument helps justify this orientation because it redirects attention from abstract opacity toward the interactional reconfigurations that appear when “computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). In embedding contexts, the governance object is that interactional reconfiguration, because it is where institutional authority gets re-routed into interface prompts, default pathways, and classification outputs (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). Monitoring therefore belongs in MEASURE and MANAGE because it operationalizes early-warning indicators and couples them to mitigation levers, rather than treating evaluation as a one-time procurement hurdle (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). ([NIST Publications](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/nist.ai.100-1.pdf))

This principle defines enforcement as a requirement rather than a hope, meaning that high-stakes institutions must retain credible authority to pause, deactivate, or roll back embedded relational systems when evidence shows unacceptable routing harms or trace misuse . Without rollback authority, governance collapses into reassurance, and reassurance accelerates normalization because it substitutes for the ability to impose friction on harmful pathways . This principle is especially important under conditions of lock-in, where workflows adapt to the system and exit costs rise, which can trap institutions in a harmful mediation architecture even when problems are known .

NIST’s RMF language provides a clear foothold for enforceability because it explicitly names the need for “go/no-go system commissioning and deployment decisions” (NIST, 2023: 19). Rollback authority operationalizes that requirement across time, not only at launch, because embedding harms can surface through cumulative use, downstream reuse, and behavioral adaptation rather than through initial failure (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). This principle therefore sits in MANAGE and GOVERN, because it requires organizational schemes that can execute decisions, not merely record concerns, and because it must remain available after deployment when normalization pressure is strongest (Lepage, n.d.; NIST, 2023). ([NIST Publications](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/nist.ai.100-1.pdf))

Symmetry restoration means coupling acceleration oversight with embedding oversight so that governance attention tracks where consequence actually accrues, which in high-stakes institutions often means the interface layer where disclosure becomes trace, trace becomes classification input, and classification becomes routing authority . The argument is not that frontier capability governance is irrelevant, but that it is incomplete if relational extraction and infrastructural consolidation proceed under weaker constraint than capability escalation . NIST’s framing supports this coupling because it treats risk management as iterative and lifecycle-wide rather than as a one-off compliance act, and it explicitly positions governance as cross-cutting across mapping, measurement, and management (NIST, 2023). ([NIST Publications](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/nist.ai.100-1.pdf))

Restoring symmetry also requires a temporal recalibration of urgency, because embedding tends to arrive before rupture, and because infrastructural power tends to harden before democratic deliberation catches up to what has already been normalized . Bowker and Star’s warning about invisibility clarifies why timing matters, because once classificatory arrangements disappear into routine, the institution tends to experience them as “how things work” rather than as a contestable choice (Bowker & Star, 1999). Governance with teeth therefore treats ordinariness as the signal, not as the alibi, and it intervenes before mediation becomes destiny .

From Trust Narratives to Infrastructure: The Ex Libris Case

If scenario artifacts coordinate acceleration, vendor trust narratives coordinate adoption, because they translate uncertainty into reassurance and make uptake feel administratively safe (Lepage, n.d.; Winner, 1986). Ex Libris frames its generative AI initiative through an explicit trust claim, “AI we can use and trust,” which functions less like evidence and more like a stabilizing device for institutional decision-makers under adoption pressure (Ex Libris, 2024). This kind of framing matters because it substitutes narrative legitimacy for enforceable friction, which can accelerate normalization even when governance thresholds remain unmet (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star describe why this substitution is structurally plausible, because infrastructures gain authority when they recede into the background, since standards and classifications “are ordinarily invisible” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 15). When trust language helps a system cross from pilot to default, invisibility hardens as routine, and routine begins to govern without announcing itself as governance (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Trust framing also matters because it encodes authority at the interface layer rather than at the policy layer, which aligns with Winner’s argument that artifacts “have politics” because design arrangements settle affairs by distributing constraints and permissions (Winner, 1986). Bowker and Star make the same point in classification terms when they warn that “a classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 195). In the Ex Libris context, the concern is not only whether outputs are accurate, but whether the system’s interface and synthesis features become a de facto classificatory authority inside academic discovery, thereby shifting what becomes findable, legible, and “relevant” under institutional time pressure (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Ex Libris’s institutional positioning relies on epistemic containment claims, where the system is presented as “grounded” through curated indices and therefore framed as augmentation rather than as replacement of professional judgment (Lepage, n.d.; Ex Libris, 2024). This containment rhetoric matters because it can narrow governance attention to hallucination risk while leaving routing authority and classificatory effects under-specified, especially when the system is treated as “just discovery support” rather than as infrastructure (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). Christin’s warning is relevant here because she argues we should “stop thinking about algorithms as neatly bounded technical objects that need to be ‘opened up’” (Christin, 2020: 2). In this case, “grounding” can be technically meaningful while still leaving the institutional question unresolved, because the governance risk sits in how the interface structures inquiry, not only in whether the content comes from an approved corpus (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

Containment claims also function as trust accelerants, because they imply that institutional risk is limited to a technical failure mode rather than to a reorganization of epistemic access pathways . The argument here is infrastructural: even if content is curated, synthesis can still reshape what users treat as the answer, which can change research behavior, learning outcomes, and the practical distribution of attention across materials (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star’s description of how organizational commitments harden into technical form clarifies why this is governance-relevant, because “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). In Ex Libris-style systems, the “code” is not only the model, but also the retrieval configuration, ranking, prompting, and synthesis wrapper that produces the user’s sense of what knowledge “is” in the moment (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

The mechanism model outlined earlier is visible in the Ex Libris case because the system performs detection, shaping, routing, and consolidation as ordinary usability features rather than as declared governance interventions (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). Detection occurs when natural language queries are interpreted and mapped onto institutional metadata and indexed resources, which produces an inferred “information need” that the system can act on . Shaping occurs when the interface proposes follow-up prompts and topic refinements that steer inquiry into pathways that are legible to the system’s indexing and ranking structure, which can narrow exploration even while feeling helpful . Christin’s interactional emphasis helps locate the governance object here, because she centers “the reconfigurations that take place when computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). In the library setting, the relevant reconfiguration is that the interface becomes a first-pass interpretive agent that scaffolds what questions get asked next (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020).

Routing occurs when summaries, suggested terms, or categorized results channel users toward some resources and away from others, which turns discovery into a sequence shaped by classificatory authority (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star’s warning that “no one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54) matters here because synthesis and ranking can privilege some epistemic styles and research habits while marginalizing others, even if the system is “accurate” within its own retrieval boundaries (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Lock-in then emerges through workflow adaptation, because staff and users begin to treat the interface as the natural entry point for inquiry, which increases the perceived cost of returning to slower, less synthesized discovery practices (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

This chain does not require malfunction, because it operates through normalization and convenience, which is why embedding can precede crisis . Bowker and Star capture the paradox of infrastructural ease in a line that clarifies why normalization is politically consequential, since “there is a lot of hard labor in effortless ease” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 22). In Ex Libris-like deployments, that “hard labor” includes classificatory decisions, ranking choices, and synthesis patterns that become routine and therefore rarely litigated as governance decisions by everyday users (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Discovery systems already exercise classificatory power through metadata, ranking, and retrieval, but generative synthesis intensifies that power because it produces an interpretive layer that can function as a surrogate for reading, skimming, and comparative evaluation (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star provide the conceptual anchor for this shift because they treat classification as consequence-bearing infrastructure, since “a classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 195). In a discovery environment, the “privileges” are not only access permissions but also epistemic affordances, meaning what appears first, what is summarized, what is framed as central, and what fades into the long tail of search results (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Classification authority also reshapes professional jurisdiction because librarians and instructors can be pulled into downstream correction and guidance rather than upstream framing of inquiry, which narrows discretion through routinization rather than through explicit policy change (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). Christin’s insistence on studying the assemblage matters because the governance effects of synthesis do not sit only in the model, but in how institutional actors adapt their work to the system’s outputs and defaults (Christin, 2020). Once these adaptations stabilize, the discovery interface becomes the environment of thought rather than a tool among tools, which is the operational meaning of infrastructure (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

In the Ex Libris case, trust narratives risk functioning as substitutes for constraint because they reassure institutions about safety while leaving the enforcement apparatus underdeveloped (Lepage, n.d.; Ex Libris, 2024). The analytic claim is not that trust is bad, but that trust without structural friction cannot guarantee protection against the very mechanism chain that produces embedding harms . Bowker and Star explain why this matters by showing how contested values become embedded into technical form and then disappear into routine, since “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). In other words, once the system is normalized, the institution can mistake the absence of scandal for the presence of governance, because the pathway feels smooth and therefore legitimate (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

This is also where transparency limits return, because seeing parts of the system does not necessarily restore contestability if workflow and user habituation already treat the interface as default (Lepage, n.d.; Christin, 2020). Christin’s critique remains relevant because she argues against a narrow “black box” imaginary that treats visibility as the solution, since she calls for attention to “the reconfigurations” produced by sociotechnical interaction (Christin, 2020: 1). In Ex Libris-like settings, the governance question is therefore whether constraint exists in operational form, meaning logged overrides, distributional audits of synthesis and ranking effects, trace retention limits, and credible deactivation authority, rather than whether the vendor claims trustworthiness .

The Ex Libris case reinforces the paper’s central thesis that capability thresholds often operate at a lower level than governance thresholds, because adoption can proceed quickly on the basis of plausibility and reassurance while constraint lags behind normalization . The case illustrates that institutional embedding does not require recursive self-improvement, because it requires workflow integration, user habituation, and the disappearance of contestability into routine (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star’s observation that infrastructures become powerful by receding into invisibility clarifies why normalization precedes crisis, since classifications “are ordinarily invisible” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 15). When generative mediation becomes routine in epistemic infrastructure, classificatory authority shifts subtly but durably, and the shift becomes difficult to reverse precisely because it arrives as convenience (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

The implication is not that Ex Libris uniquely produces harm, but that vendor trust narratives and efficiency framings can accelerate embedding while leaving enforceable safeguards under-specified, which is the allocative asymmetry the paper is trying to correct . The governance lesson is therefore temporal and infrastructural: if institutions let relational mediation become default before they operationalize constraint, they will discover that oversight has to fight both technology and habit, which is the practical form of lock-in (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Conclusion: Governing Before Invisibility Hardens

This paper returns to a simple asymmetry that structures contemporary AI governance: acceleration is dramatized and operationalized, while embedding is normalized and under-constrained . The political problem is not that acceleration narratives are irrelevant, but that they allocate urgency and resources toward frontier scenarios while allowing relational infrastructures to consolidate quietly in the present . Infrastructure scholarship explains why that quiet consolidation matters, because classificatory and technical arrangements gain authority as they become routine and fade from view (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star name the visibility trap directly when they note that standards and classifications “are ordinarily invisible” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 15). The asymmetry therefore becomes governance-relevant because what disappears into routine becomes harder to contest, even when it reshapes access, discretion, and consequence-bearing institutional pathways (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

The interface is not merely a delivery layer for model outputs, because it operates as the site where relational AI converts interaction into durable trace, and durable trace into classificatory and routing authority . This conversion is a political economy because the interface stabilizes the conditions under which users disclose, institutions retain, and systems reuse, and those conditions can align with revenue incentives even when framed as care or convenience . Bowker and Star capture the infrastructural mechanism by which this happens, since “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). In relational AI settings, the “code” includes prompts, follow-up shaping, ranking and synthesis wrappers, and trace retention defaults, which together constitute an interactional policy regime that users experience as usability (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

Once mediation becomes routine, mediation becomes infrastructure, and infrastructure becomes power through normalization rather than through spectacle (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). The Ex Libris case illustrates this trajectory concretely, because trust and efficiency framings can accelerate uptake while leaving constraint thin, so that governance thresholds lag behind capability thresholds . The political economy claim is therefore not a moral accusation but a structural diagnosis: relational systems can reorganize institutional order by operating “as designed,” especially when engagement and reassurance become the pathways through which users are rendered legible, classifiable, and routable .

Effective governance requires recalibration away from model-only oversight and toward mediation architecture oversight, because the most immediate institutional harms travel through routing, trace portability, and workflow stabilization rather than through catastrophic autonomy . Christin’s methodological argument supports this recalibration because she insists that analysis must attend to “the reconfigurations that take place when computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). In the terms of this paper, governance must attach to those reconfigurations as enforceable constraints, including trace segregation, refusal pathways, monetization firewalls, and credible rollback authority, before embedding becomes default .

The core imperative is to govern before lock-in, because lock-in is not only technical but also organizational and behavioral, meaning that once institutions and users adapt to the interface, reversing course becomes costly and politically difficult . Bowker and Star’s account of infrastructural ease explains why, since “there is a lot of hard labor in effortless ease” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 22). That “hard labor” includes decisions about classification and routing that become routine operations, which can make later attempts at democratic deliberation feel like disruption of service rather than correction of governance (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999).

AI governance faces a temporal choice between governing rupture and governing normalization, and this paper argues that the latter cannot remain secondary if relational extraction embeds first . Governing rupture concentrates attention on speculative autonomy and catastrophic misuse, while governing normalization concentrates attention on the everyday infrastructures through which interaction becomes trace, trace becomes classification, and classification becomes access (Lepage, n.d.; Bowker and Star, 1999). The argument is not that rupture should be ignored, but that urgency must be symmetrical across the two timelines, because later breakthroughs in alignment or capability control will not automatically unwind institutional pathways that have already been sedimented through embedding .

The trajectory of AI may be shaped by acceleration. Its politics will be shaped by embedding .
