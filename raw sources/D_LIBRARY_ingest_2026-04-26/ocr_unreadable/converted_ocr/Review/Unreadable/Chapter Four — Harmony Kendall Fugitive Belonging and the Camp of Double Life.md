# Chapter Four — Harmony Kendall: Fugitive Belonging and the Camp of Double Life

Chapter Four — Harmony Kendall: Fugitive Belonging and the Camp of Double Life

Opening Fieldwork Scene — Downtown Montreal, Pride Week
The café is a half‑converted ground floor of an old brick triplex — tall windows cracked open
to let in the August heat, ceiling fans pushing the warm air in lazy circles that never seem to
reach the back tables.​
It’s mid‑afternoon, too late for the lunchtime rush and too early for the after‑work wave.​
The espresso machine hisses every few minutes like a sigh; someone behind the counter
keeps refilling the pastry case even though no one is buying muffins.​
Outside, the sound of heels and bicycle bells drifts in between bursts of traffic.
The corner table is claimed territory.​
Three people have spread themselves out there in a kind of ritual sprawl: one bag tipped
sideways, spilling flyers for queer events across the table; a rainbow lanyard coiled like a
snake over a pile of drag‑brunch menus; two iced drinks sweating onto cork coasters.​
Between the flyers are tangles of bead bracelets, the kind you get for free from corporate
Pride sponsors, but here they look deliberately dumped — part of the clutter, part of the
tableau.
They’re plotting something for the weekend’s street fair: a roving “photo‑booth confessional”
where passers‑by can record a thirty‑second secret to be projected anonymously on the wall
of a downtown building after dark.​
The idea is part activism, part art, part dare.​
It’s also part spectacle, and everyone at the table knows it.

One of the three is wearing a headband with cat ears.​
Not as a costume — no one here treats it like that — but as if it’s simply what they wear.​
The headband sits on perfectly smooth hair that catches the light when they lean toward their
iced latte.​
Their lip gloss is fresh enough to leave a shimmer on the straw.​
The tank top says Drama Is My Cardio in glitter script, which somehow reads less like a joke
and more like a mission statement.
Their phone is in constant motion: quick bursts of texts, a flurry of emojis, half‑caps updates
to volunteers.​
They multitask without missing a beat in the conversation, switching from “We need a
second tripod for the projector” to “YES, bring the bubble machine” to “No, we are not
letting anyone touch the glitter cannon unsupervised.”​
When someone raises the question of having a formal “consent to broadcast” form for
participants, they wave the suggestion away with a flick of the wrist:​
“Please. No one’s gonna sue a cat.”​

It’s the kind of line that works because it’s both absurd and slightly true, and it derails the
practical discussion just long enough for everyone to laugh.

The dynamic at the table is collaborative but competitive — that specific Pride‑season energy
where friends egg each other on to be more outrageous, more quotable, more photogenic.​
The cat‑ear wearer keeps tossing in lines that tilt things toward camp:​
“If it’s not fabulous, it’s not worth our time.”​
“We can’t not have a glitter cannon. That would be a hate crime.”​
Every time the conversation starts to veer toward logistics alone, they pull it back toward
style and theatre, like the idea itself will wither if it gets too practical.
At one point they duck out for a smoke, leaving their phone — unlocked — face‑up on the
table.​
On the screen, the Notes app is open to a list that alternates between genuine tasks and
self‑branding reminders:
●​ check with DJ about generator hookup​
●​ find silver rhinestones for shoes​
●​ practice pout for photos​
●​ don’t get too earnest on mic​

It’s an unintentional confession: the logistics are real, but the image is equally part of the job.

When they come back, they bring the conversation around to what Pride “means” this year.​
The mood shifts — just enough to signal that we’re in a different register now.​
Someone talks about political backlash, about needing to make a stronger activist statement.​
Someone else counters with the importance of joy and visibility as resistance.​
The cat‑ear wearer listens, then shrugs with a small smile:​
“Honestly? I’m over having to pick a lane. Like, am I here for the community? Yes. Am I
here for the party? Also yes. Can’t it be both without me having to make a speech about it?”
No one argues.​
There’s a quiet acknowledgement in the air — the shared understanding that this is how most
of us actually live Pride: as a tangle of motives, allegiances, and pleasures that don’t add up
to a single coherent story.​
Some are here to march; some are here to dance.​
Some are here for the speeches; some are here for the glitter.​

Many of us — maybe most — are here for both, without any plan to resolve that
contradiction.

Watching them lean back in their chair, sunglasses pushed up on their head, chatting about
after‑parties while still troubleshooting the projection schedule, I feel it:​
Harmony‑energy.​
The unapologetic coexistence of sincerity and surface, activism and aesthetics, community
and self‑curation.​
Not as a compromise, not as a confused halfway point, but as a deliberate, sparkling
both/and.​
It’s camp, but it’s also a tactic.​
It’s the choice to keep multiple faces in play at once and never let anyone reduce you to just
one of them.
Harmony in the Buffyverse: Fugitive Belonging and the Camp of Double Life

From background player to undead socialite
Harmony Kendall begins as the sort of character you could almost miss.​
Introduced as one of Cordelia Chase’s satellite friends in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, she is a
fixture of the high‑school hallway: stylish, shallow, often giggling at the edges of someone
else’s put‑down.​
Her presence signals Cordelia’s social gravity — not because Harmony herself is powerful,
but because she orbits someone who is.​
In the early seasons, Harmony’s personality is legible mostly through cliché: the
glossy‑haired teen queen archetype distilled into a supporting role.
Then comes Graduation Day.​
In the chaos of the Mayor’s ascension, Harmony is bitten by a vampire and turned.​
When she reappears in The Harsh Light of Day (S4E3), she’s undead — but her personality
is startlingly intact.​
She is still bubbly, still fashion‑obsessed, still craving approval from those she admires.​
The transformation into a vampire does not rewrite her as a brooding predator; instead, it
renders her a camp contradiction: a blood‑drinking socialite with a glitter pen.

The double‑life as glitch
Most vampires in the Buffyverse embrace their new identity with theatrical flourish.​
They adopt darker aesthetics, deepen their voices, cultivate menace.​
Harmony, by contrast, keeps the pastel wardrobe and the peppy speech patterns.​

She wants to be taken seriously as a villain — but also wants to host wine‑and‑cheese nights,
keep her hair streaked just right, and maintain an active dating life.​
She doesn’t trade one life for the other; she tries to run both simultaneously.
This is her glitch: fugitive belonging.​
She is never fully at home in either world — too domesticated to be a convincing predator,
too monstrous to slip back into human society without someone noticing the fangs.​
Instead of resolving the contradiction, she performs it.​
Her existence becomes a running joke in the series — but it’s also a surprisingly coherent
survival tactic.

Key narrative moments: awkward crossings
Several episodes crystallise Harmony’s glitch:
●​ “The Harsh Light of Day” (S4E3) — Reuniting with Spike, she plays the role of
devoted vampire girlfriend with schoolgirl enthusiasm.​
She gushes over plans for mayhem but wants to be reassured about her outfit.​
Her villainy is a costume party in more ways than one.​
●​ “Disharmony” (Angel, S2E17) — Visiting Los Angeles, Harmony falls in with
Cordelia again, pretending she is still human.​
Her charade collapses, but she never seems particularly committed to keeping it
going.​
She drifts between human and vampire allegiances without real anguish — more
concerned about social fallout than moral crisis.​
●​ “Harm’s Way” (Angel, S5E9) — Now working as a secretary at Wolfram & Hart,
Harmony has a desk job in an evil law firm while trying to keep her vampiric urges
discreetly managed.​
She juggles corporate etiquette with the occasional murder, fretting over both in the
same tone.​
Her career trajectory reads like an undead version of the “working girl” sitcom plot.​
●​ Season 8 Comics — Harmony becomes a reality‑TV star, capitalising on her
vampirism as a brand while insisting on her relatability.​
She makes monstrosity marketable by reframing it as lifestyle content.​

Why Harmony is not a failed Faith or a failed Anya

It would be easy to read Harmony’s glitch as a diluted version of Faith’s outlaw stance or
Anya’s refusal of script.​
But Harmony is not resisting the moral order or breaking the ritual frame; she is blending
contradictory orders without trying to reconcile them.​
Faith says no to domestication.​
Anya says no to emotional choreography.​
Harmony says yes to everything — but in ways that keep her from being fully claimed by
any one allegiance.
Her refusal is not oppositional but diffusive.​
She doesn’t disrupt the system from within; she perforates it by living in multiple systems at
once, never quite convincingly but always persistently.​
Her camp is not about mocking seriousness — it’s about leaning so hard into every possible
role that sincerity and performance become indistinguishable.

Camp as method, not garnish
Harmony’s camp is often played for laughs in‑universe, but in queer reading, it can be seen as
a method of survival.​
Susan Sontag’s famous 1964 essay “Notes on Camp” positions camp as an embrace of style
over content, of artifice over authenticity.​
Harmony embodies this — but where camp is often read as ironic detachment, she uses it as
sincere self‑presentation.
Her vampire persona is not a mask she wears reluctantly; it’s one of several she keeps in
rotation.​
The Harmony who flirts with Spike, the Harmony who pitches herself as Cordelia’s bestie,
the Harmony who runs a vampire‑lifestyle reality show — all are equally “real” to her.​
Camp here becomes a way to hold contradictory selves without privileging one as the truer
version.

Fugitive belonging in queer/trans ritual life
In queer/trans ritual cultures, Harmony‑energy appears in practitioners who move fluidly
between overlapping but sometimes incompatible worlds: activist spaces and nightlife scenes,
spiritual circles and corporate day jobs, polyamorous networks and family holiday dinners.​
They don’t choose one identity as definitive; they build a repertoire of roles and move
among them depending on context.
In your fieldwork, I’ve seen this in:

●​ The activist‑DJ who DJs leather nights, organises for housing justice, and runs an
ancestor altar in their apartment.​
●​ The drag‑priestess who performs at charity balls and also leads grief rituals in
cemeteries, never toning down either side.​
●​ The corporate witch who shows up to Beltane in couture businesswear, takes a call
from a client mid‑circle, then jumps right back into drumming.​

This is not dilettantism; it’s a form of navigation.​
By keeping multiple selves active, they maintain access to multiple communities, resources,
and forms of legitimacy.

The risk of underestimation
In Buffy and Angel, Harmony is consistently underestimated — by allies and enemies alike —
because she never fully commits to a single script.​
She’s seen as harmless by humans and unserious by vampires.​
In ritual life, similar practitioners risk being seen as shallow, opportunistic, or unreliable.​
Their refusal to declare a singular allegiance can read as lack of commitment, even when it’s
a deliberate survival choice.
But Harmony’s adaptability is also her resilience.​
She can survive in hostile environments precisely because she’s never entirely in them.​
Her belonging is always partial, always provisional, always ready to pivot.

Harmony in the taxonomy of glitch
Placed alongside Willow, Anya, and Faith, Harmony’s fugitive belonging rounds out the
spectrum:
●​ Willow holds the work in duration.​
●​ Anya cracks the work open from within.​
●​ Faith guards the work from the threshold.​
●​ Harmony moves between multiple works without pledging herself entirely to any one
of them.​

Where the others stabilise or disrupt the circle, Harmony cross‑pollinates.​
She carries elements from one space to another, often without formal sanction, often in ways
that seem frivolous until they open unexpected lines of connection.
Fieldwork Parallels: Fugitive Belonging in Ritual Life

1. Mile End — Rooftop Beltane with After‑Party Energy
The circle is technically set for a Beltane rite.​
There are flowers twined into hoops, ribbons staked in a pattern that hints at a maypole, and
an altar covered in the usual seasonal suspects — a bowl of strawberries, jars of honey, cut
herbs in small glass jars.​
The facilitator calls people into a loose arc, explaining the invocation.​
The mood is soft, maybe too soft for the cool May air.
On the far side of the rooftop, two people are arranging a small speaker and a cooler.​
They’re in coordinated pastel windbreakers and mirrored sunglasses, each with a sprig of
lilac tucked behind one ear.​
When the invocation begins, they drift toward the circle — but only for a few minutes, long
enough to say their lines.​
Then they slide back toward the speaker and hit play on a playlist that is more
Pride‑afterparty than pagan‑folk: Robyn, Carly Rae Jepsen, early Madonna.
By the time the main body of the rite is over, their corner of the rooftop has become its own
gravitational field.​
Half the circle migrates toward it; people are dancing, laughing, and occasionally shouting
jokes back toward the altar crew.​
The rite dissolves into a hybrid zone: a ritual closing with disco beats, with offerings made
between choruses.
Analysis:​
This is pure Harmony‑energy — the afterparty bleeding into the rite, the rite bleeding
into the afterparty.​
The playlist‑keepers aren’t undermining the ritual; they’re reframing it so it can hold both the
seasonal devotion and the social exuberance in the same space.​
They refuse to choose which is “the real” Beltane, and by refusing, they drag everyone else
into that refusal.​
It’s camp as permeability: letting the disco ball spin over the altar without apology.

2. NDG — Tarot in the Club Bathroom

It’s a winter night, and the queer dance club is at full capacity.​
On the second floor, the bass from the main room rattles the walls even in the narrow
bathroom hallway.​
In the back stall, someone has set up a folding stool and a cloth bag spilling tarot decks.​
There’s a handwritten sign taped to the wall: QUEER TAROT / PAY WHAT YOU WANT.
People come in to touch up lipstick, adjust harnesses, and get three‑card spreads between
songs.​
The reader is in a sequined halter top and black lipstick, explaining the Fool card while
blotting sweat from their temples.​
Every reading is punctuated by someone laughing too loud, someone shouting from the line
outside the door, someone leaning over to borrow eyeliner.
When I ask how they ended up reading cards here instead of in a quieter space, they shrug:​
“This is where my people are tonight. You want magic? It’s happening in the bathroom line.”
Analysis:​
Here, the double‑life is ritual as nightlife — divination and drag sharing the same cramped,
noisy bathroom.​
The reader is not making a spectacle of contradiction; they’re normalising it.​
Tarot belongs here just as much as in a candlelit kitchen, and the sparkle of the halter top is
as much part of the magic as the cards.​
Like Harmony at Wolfram & Hart, they’re not leaving the corporate‑evil (or in this case,
capitalist club) structure; they’re using it as their stage.

3. Parc‑Extension — Community Potluck Meets Corporate Catering
The potluck is meant to be a grassroots, low‑budget celebration of Imbolc: casseroles in
Pyrex dishes, mason jars of soup, fresh‑baked bread cooling on the counter.​
But one attendee arrives with a collapsible cart stacked with catering trays — roasted
vegetables in foil, quinoa salad in plastic domes, mini cheesecakes in perfect rows.​
The trays are from their day job at a catering company that services corporate retreats.
They set up their food on the same table as everyone else’s, but the difference in presentation
is stark.​
They keep offering serving tongs, suggesting pairings — “The roasted squash goes really
well with the couscous” — while also explaining the origin of Brigid’s feast day in Irish
tradition.​
They float between the kitchen and the altar room, switching easily from professional
hospitality to seasonal storytelling.

By the time the candles are lit, everyone has eaten both the corporate quinoa and the
homemade bread.​
No one can quite remember which came from where.
Analysis:​
This is fugitive belonging as catering magic — moving between corporate kitchen culture
and community ritual kitchen without dropping either persona.​
It’s not subversion in the sense of sneaking in one to undermine the other; it’s coexistence
without merger.​
Like Harmony working as a secretary in an evil law firm while keeping her vampire identity
in play, this practitioner keeps both uniforms ready, and wears them interchangeably in the
same evening.

4. Online Samhain — The Zoom Ritual and the Side Chat
It’s an online Samhain, hosted by a collective that blends witchcraft, queer spirituality, and
activist grief work.​
The host has created a carefully structured ritual: grounding meditation, name‑reading for the
dead, guided trance.​
Dozens of cameras are on; candles flicker in pixelated squares.
In the chat, a cluster of participants are carrying on a parallel conversation: swapping recipes
for pumpkin bread, posting memes about Mercury retrograde, joking about who’s “haunting”
their Wi‑Fi connection.​
One participant is clearly moving between both spaces — on camera, they are solemn,
candle‑lit, eyes closed during the guided trance.​
In the chat, they are sending GIFs of Winona Ryder rolling her eyes.
After the ritual closes, they post a link to a virtual after‑party in another Zoom room.​
Half the participants click through.
Analysis:​
Here, fugitive belonging shows up as multi‑channel ritual presence — fully inhabiting the
solemn frame while also tending a parallel, irreverent, connective undercurrent.​
Like Harmony keeping her corporate job while also vamping on reality TV, this is a
conscious choice to maintain two registers at once.​
It allows for seriousness without rigidity, play without derailing the core intention.

Common mechanics: How fugitive belonging works
Across these vignettes, Harmony‑style fugitive belonging shares certain mechanics:

1.​ Maintain multiple allegiances — Keep visible ties to more than one social/ritual
world at the same time.​
2.​ Switch registers without apology — Move between seriousness and spectacle
without signalling that one is more authentic.​
3.​ Leverage style as connective tissue — Use camp, glamour, or curated
self‑presentation to make contradictory spaces feel continuous.​
4.​ Normalise contradiction — Treat the overlap of worlds as ordinary rather than
exceptional.​
5.​ Cross‑pollinate quietly — Bring forms, ideas, and aesthetics from one context into
another, often without announcement.​

This is not “failing” to commit; it’s committing to the ongoing act of crossing.​
It’s a way of being present in multiple circles without collapsing them into one or standing
entirely outside any of them.
Theoretical Framing: Fugitive Belonging as Ritual Technology

Harmony’s glitch: living without resolution
In the Buffyverse, Harmony’s transformation into a vampire does not rewrite her identity so
much as multiply it.​
She retains the speech patterns, social preoccupations, and affective style of her human life
while layering on the predatory appetites, vampiric power, and underworld connections of her
undead status.​
She doesn’t “rebrand” herself as purely one or the other; she lives in the collision space
between the two.
This is important: unlike Faith, who chooses to live at the threshold, Harmony doesn’t stand
at the doorway deciding which side to inhabit.​
She takes both inside with her, into every space.​
It’s not edge‑dwelling; it’s identity‑carrying across boundaries without ever off‑loading
either one.
This is what I’m calling fugitive belonging: belonging enough to move freely in a space, but
never in a way that severs ties to other, sometimes incompatible, spaces.​
It’s fugitive because it’s never fully captured; it’s belonging because it’s never fully
estranged.

Camp as method, not garnish
Susan Sontag’s Notes on Camp (1964) famously described camp as love of style over
content, of artifice over sincerity.​
But Sontag largely framed camp as apolitical — a taste sensibility.​
Later theorists like Moe Meyer and Fabio Cleto insist that camp is political, a queer
performance that exposes the constructedness of identity itself.
Harmony’s camp is exactly this.​
Her pink‑fluffy‑pen villainy doesn’t just “fail” to be serious; it shows that seriousness is
itself a style.​
Her refusal to switch to a darker aesthetic as a vampire isn’t naïveté — it’s a declaration that
she won’t translate her whole self into the idiom expected by her new identity category.
This makes her camp different from parody.​
She’s not mocking vampirism by staying in pastels; she’s inhabiting vampirism in pastels,
making that combination real through repetition.​
That’s the move that turns camp from a surface joke into a survival methodology:
performing both scripts until the contradiction becomes ordinary.

Muñoz’s disidentification: the art of inhabiting contradictions
José Esteban Muñoz’s Disidentifications (1999) offers the clearest theoretical entry point into
Harmony’s glitch.​
Disidentification is the tactic of neither fully assimilating into nor rejecting a dominant
identity category, but instead working within and against it at once.
Harmony disidentifies with the role of “vampire” as codified in the Buffyverse:
●​ She embraces the perks (immortality, nightlife, power).​
●​ She sidesteps the symbolic aesthetic (brooding menace, goth gravitas).​
●​ She rewrites the affective tone (from predator to party host).​

She also disidentifies with “human socialite”:
●​ She retains the wardrobe, the gossip, the parties.​
●​ She sidesteps the moral limitations (she still feeds on humans).​
●​ She rewrites the performance of innocence (from oblivious teen to knowing camp).​

This is the work of holding incompatible codes without collapsing into one.​
In queer/trans ritual life, disidentification looks like:
●​ Bringing club drag into a candlelit ancestor rite.​
●​ Quoting scripture ironically but still invoking it in spellwork.​
●​ Wearing the regalia of a tradition you were never fully initiated into, without claiming
full insider status.​

It’s a refusal to purify oneself for the comfort of any single audience.

Goffman and the collapsing of front stage/back stage
Erving Goffman’s The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1956) gives us the useful
language of front stage (the curated public self) and back stage (the less curated, more
“private” self).​
Many of Buffy’s characters keep these zones distinct — Buffy herself keeps Slayer life
hidden from most of Sunnydale; Willow has “witch Willow” and “student Willow”; Anya
keeps her vengeance‑demon past off the customer‑service floor at the Magic Box.
Harmony doesn’t.​
There is no back stage in her performance.​
She talks about her hair while plotting a murder; she gushes about hosting skills while
managing a minion.​
Her “private” style is indistinguishable from her “public” style, because she’s not managing
impression to fit context — she’s carrying context with her.
In ritual culture, this is the practitioner who is the same in the club, the kitchen, and the
circle:
●​ The glamour is real in all settings.​
●​ The irreverence is real in all settings.​
●​ The sincerity is real in all settings.​

It’s not that they can’t code‑switch; it’s that they choose not to.​
The refusal to compartmentalise is part of the magic.

[...]

●​ Harmony resists letting the circle forget it is one of many.​

This is why Harmony is not a “lesser” glitch — she is the fourth leg of the table.​
Without her, the ritual ecology risks turning in on itself, mistaking self‑containment for
strength.

Toward the shapeshifters
Harmony keeps multiple allegiances alive at once — but she still returns to each.​
The shapeshifters I turn to next do something else entirely:​
they don’t just belong to multiple worlds, they become different selves as they cross them.​
They don’t keep faces in rotation; they change faces entirely.​
They don’t bridge existing circles; they make circles that weren’t there before.
If Harmony’s magic is to say, You can keep all your selves, the shapeshifter’s magic is to say,
You can become someone new and still be you.​
That is where we go next: into the territory of unstable identities, transformative personae,
and the ritual arts of becoming otherwise.
The rhinestones still glitter.​
The doorways multiply.​
And the crossing is just beginning.
