Dear Professor Sautman,
I would like to be considered for a position as assistant professor in the Ph.D. Program in French
for the Graduate Center at City University of New York. I am convinced that my teaching
experience and research interests have adequately prepared me for this position. Furthermore, I
believe that my approach to religious and gender studies could bring a both unique and rigorous
contribution to your program. I have also explored many professional milieus, in both French
and English, and I am enthusiastically welcoming new challenges. My approach to teaching and
research, in accordance with the Graduate Center’s, is interdisciplinary, which allows me to
reach students in different programs in one class.
Although my primary area of research is religious studies, I have additional expertise in gender
and queer studies, and I am eager to teach the intersection of both areas. I also have a previous
background in French and Quebec literature, which relates to my interest for discourse and
narratives in general. Moreover, I have served as a teaching assistant in courses about religion
and sexuality, homosexuality and gender identities, as well as Quebec culture. I have also spoken
numerous times in many courses related to those topics. In addition, I have served as a research
assistant for projects on youth and sexuality in Quebec, on marginalized populations in France,
on religious diversity in Montreal, on school rites, and on the intersection of masculinities and
ageing. I also served as assistant editor and archive manager for the Religious Studies Project, an
online endeavor sponsored by the British Association for the Study of Religion (BASR), a
member of the American Academy of Religion (AAR), and the International Society for the
Sociology of Religion (ISSR). In the next few years, I hope to develop and give courses in

Statement of Teaching Interests
After completing a master’s degree in French and Quebec literature, I entered the field of
religious studies, with a queer feminist perspective, because I wanted to work closer to those
interested, at different degrees, in the complexity of the way identities, culture, and religion
intersect. Scholars and religiously involved people have different things to say about very diverse
kinds of social practices. In crossing over fields of studies, I needed to figure out, and very fast,
how students were taught fundamental concepts about those very omnipresent issues of life
without them thinking that these were religious truths. It is a very amazing thing to talk about
religion and identity as interconnected aspects of culture, and it is a tremendous challenge to
bring students to that place where they can detach themselves form their faith and collaboratively
think like mature students and even future scholars.
My passion for learning is doubled with the desire to tell others about what I have learned. It is
indeed a matter of pride for me to teach others, in a very short and effective fashion, what and
how I have learned. University courses and other speaking opportunities, where it was crucial to
communicate whole ideas about religions, identities, scholars, and theories in a short amount of
time, that have allowed me to put this into practice using interactive methods and lively
discussions with and among the audience.
In fact, something else came into this willful need to communicate knowledge while making sure
I was understood. Ever since I could talk, I always had a stutter. More pronounced in my
younger years, it has now become a very important element for me to deal with whenever I face
an audience, whether composed of students or scholars. It is important because it allows me to be
honest with the audience, as struggles may occur during communication. Was I
understood correctly? What can I do to make sure I was? I found out with experience that it is
always best to see students as part of the communicative process. Certainly they are the ones
being tested, but the exercise is also mine, and, as such, it is my duty to make sure that every
aspect of my classes engages everyone in a learning relationship based on reciprocity.
That being said, I cherish my ability to navigate between French and English. It is also part of
what allows me to understand and adjust to different kinds of audience and levels of expertise.

For all of those reasons, I am continuously interested in looking into and talking about issues of
normativity. The queer approach I adopted for my doctoral thesis led me to question whether or
not certain things deserved to be taught in a certain way, and others in other ways. Queer theory
is a constructivist approach to gender that questions one of the very first aspects of our identities
as individuals: how are we man or woman? In fact, my goal is to bring students to ask questions
like “How can we speak of something without creating a norm?”, “How have our scientific
categories influenced the way we talk about certain matters in our schools today?”, or “How do I
take part in the stereotypes surrounding these important matters?”. Culture is not something that
should only be seen as mere entertainment, but mostly as a reflection of our everyday “mundane”
life, as a lens with which scholars and students can understand the relationships between their
knowledge and the world outside academia.
In that sense, I often reflect on questions like “What does it mean to be a good teacher ?” or
“What is a good class ?”. I had the chance to meet great professors who modeled alternatives to
traditional lectures, and valued the teaching process as much as the learning process. There is a
great amount of tools available to help students be more involved in their learning (visual aids,
music, cinema, social media, performances, among many), and each class is a renewed
opportunity for me to use them properly, and at the right time, to allow the learning to flourish
and students to become more and more autonomous.
In sum, I always aim to develop pedagogical strategies dedicated to teaching several principles
from diverse fields of studies in a dynamic and concrete way. I am also convinced that these
strategies will equally help students in other areas of academia and in a very day-to-day manner,
inasmuch as they learn to encounter new experiences with open eyes and critical but respectful
words.
