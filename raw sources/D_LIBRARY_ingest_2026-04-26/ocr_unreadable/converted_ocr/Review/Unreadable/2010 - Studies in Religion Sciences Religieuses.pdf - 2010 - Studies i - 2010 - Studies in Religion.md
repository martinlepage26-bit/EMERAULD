Introduction
Contemporary Paganism,1 as a new religious movement, portrays gender in an array of
different ways and, as such, is very inclusive of sexual diversity. A number of studies
have already shown how gay and bisexual men, as well as lesbian and bisexual women,
negotiate gender definitions contained in Pagan religiosities (Griffin, 1999; Hasbrouck,
2004; Neitz, 2004; Harper, 2010; Oboler, 2010; Kraemer, 2012). Many of these negotiations happen through what Pagans call, sometimes meaning different things, witchcraft. The expression “Modern Witchcraft” is often used to refer to Wicca (Clifton,
2006), though Wicca is not the only form of Pagan witchcraft (nor is witchcraft the only
kind of Pagan practice). Pagans often understand witchcraft primarily as a magical
practice or “The Craft of the Witch,” no matter its cultural origins, and not in the
anthropological sense like that of Edward Evans-Pritchard or Jeanne Favret-Saada, for
instance.
But how does this general form of witchcraft help queer people take part in the Pagan
community? More precisely, what kind of changes does magic allow queer and transgender people to make so that they can relate to the Pagan community? To answer these
questions, this article will examine how queer and transgender people proceed to different kinds of negotiations regarding gender practices and representations, which drive
them to rely on witchcraft and magic, and either participate actively in the Pagan
community or distance themselves from it.
I have studied contemporary Paganism and witchcraft in the city of Montreal and the
Pagan “community” that surrounds this location. Through participant observation and
interviews (Paillé, 2006), I have worked to get a sense of the ways in which Pagan
LGBTQ (lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer) people define their relationship
to the Pagan community. This perspective highlights a certain phenomenon in which
LGBTQ Pagans partake in what I call “queer negotiations,” between their own perception of their gender and sexual identity and certain stereotypical gendered Pagan religiosities for which traditional binary Wicca is the main reference.
This article will take a look at contemporary Paganism and queer practices of witchcraft. First, I will give a brief definition of the Pagan movement as found in Montreal. I

Contemporary Paganism and Gender in Montreal
Wicca is the most influential Pagan tradition in the province of Québec and in the city of
Montreal, but it is certainly not the only one (Gagnon, 2003; Charbonneau, 2008;
Roberts, 2009). What I have witnessed in Montreal, however, is a far less structured
form of Wicca than that which can be seen in the United States or in other parts of
Canada such as, for example, the Wiccan Church of Canada in Toronto (Marron, 1989).
During my four years of studies in the field, I have heard of a maximum number of four
or five covens in the “official” hierarchical and initiatory sense, with a high priest and a
high priestess, and with three levels of initiation. In fact, I was only able to observe the
activities of one private (and quite secretive) Montreal coven, called The Silver Wheel.
Any member of such a Wiccan coven would probably consider most of the Wiccan
groups I know as simple circles. These circles are mostly composed of young people of
Western European background who practice with their group or in a solitary fashion
while sometimes joining a group they know from a friend or a family member.
People who call themselves Wiccans (or witches, sometimes interchangeably) often
do so in order to claim the practice of witchcraft as a spiritual or religious alternative to
their Christian, mostly Catholic, upbringings, or to recognize the sacred feminine and
masculine aspects of the immanent divinity that is Nature, our planet, or our entire
universe (Pike, 2004; Davy, 2007; Harvey, 1997).
Many Montreal Pagans never join a group, nor do they take part in public rituals, and
since they rarely come under the public light, their number is hard to estimate. Nevertheless, a large number of Pagan people who attend public rituals and form private
groups amongst themselves can also take part in Pagan traditions other than Wicca,
such as Goddess spirituality, Reclaiming Witchcraft, Druidry, Heathenism, Radical
Faeries, etc. In fact, all of these have influenced at one point or another the life trajectories of the majority of my informants. Even if there are some discrepancies between
different practices and ideologies, the Pagan community of Montreal is fairly harmonious and the mixing of different traditions often results in very inclusive rituals.
Many public rituals taking place during the eight sabbats of the year are organized by
a few active members of the community or any who wish to participate, no matter their
tradition.2 Very few public rituals are organized for the esbats, or the monthly full

These eight sabbats3 and thirteen esbats portray natural seasonal cycles as the interaction of the feminine and masculine and are celebrated by the majority in the Pagan
community of Montreal, sometimes under different names according to the group or
person’s specific tradition.
Not every member of the Pagan community of Montreal practices a strict structured form of Wicca, but many still observe the distinction between feminine and
masculine as the basis of their spirituality. For Wiccans, closer to the original Wicca
promoted by its founder Gerald Gardner,4 the symbolic efficacy of the magical ritual
is, for the most part, the result of the symbolic union of male and female polarities,
understood as having the traits of the Goddess (Mother-Goddess, Great Goddess,
Mother Nature) and the God (Horned God), who are given different names according to the mythological preference of the person or the group. Wicca, in this manner,
has a tendency to naturalize sexual duality, associating female with women, and
male with men (an association which equates gender with biological sex). This
distinction is realized during ritual performance, as men and women adopt certain
gestures and movements symbolizing the reproductive potential of their biological
sex. Wiccan covens are especially fond of this principle as they are the only ones I
have seen who practice the Great Rite, maybe the most binary ritual I have witnessed. With this rite, Wiccans invite the Goddess and the God to join them in a
fertility rite. The High Priest, representative of the God, dipping the ceremonial
dagger called the athame into the cup held by the High Priestess, is meant to
symbolize the (pro)creative union of both deities. Invoking the generative power
of the sacred masculine and feminine, Wiccans who perform this ritual believe that
the union of the two sexes can generate the energy and the power that “magic” or
the ritual tries to produce and channel.
Pagans who, for multiple reasons, tend to critique the gender binary, or fight prejudice
against LGBTQI (lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer and intersex) people, often
perceive the feminine/masculine duality in a different way, which is often expressed in
their beliefs and ritual practice. Their critique of heteronormativity and homophobia is
reflected in the way they perform their rituals, as they tend to leave behind the normative
Wiccan duality. What was thought of as complementarity between male and female is
instead thought to be, in traditions such as Reclaiming Witchcraft and the Radical
Faeries, mostly, a metaphor for the masculine and feminine parts in each of us. This
focuses less on the creative complementarity of the sexes and more on the awareness and
consciousness of these polarities within the psyche, on their acceptation and actualization in order to gain balance and wholeness.

However, in reality, these two interpretations of the gender binary, portrayed respectively and differently in Wicca and Reclaiming Witchcraft, can be found within the same
individual or coven. Much more blended in Montreal, compared with American Wiccan
or Reclaiming communities (Clifton, 2006), they either conflict, coexist or collide,
depending on the people and the context.
Much research in Pagan studies has shown how Pagans (LGBTQ or not) express their
identity through bodily means such as dancing, costuming, make-up, etc. all of which
can reflect their gender or sexual orientation (Pike, 2001; Hasbrouck, 2004; Neitz, 2004;
Harper, 2010). Some of it shows the challenges that oppose gender essentialism to
LGBTQ Pagans who still have to deal with stereotypes regarding their identity (Oboler,
2010; Kraemer, 2012; Harper, 2010). Research has also demonstrated that some rituals
can be specific to a gender, such as the women-only Red Tent, and they may separate
people of different genders before putting them together, during chants, for instance.
They also allow diversity on both sides, men with women and women with men (Adler,
1986; Davy, 2007; Harvey, 1997; Pike, 2001, 2004; Sloan, 2008; Charbonneau, 2002). I
have myself witnessed these phenomena at different Canadian festivals such as a WicCan Fest or Kaleidoscope Gathering. I will give an example of such rituals, done from a
queer perspective, in the next portion of this article.
Furthermore, some groups and traditions, as well as people within them, tend to
distance themselves from each other often because they cannot agree on their conception
of gender (Adler, 1986; Griffin, 1999; Althaus-Reid, 2005; Dufresne, 2004; Pike, 2001,
2004). Dianic witches, whose participants are mainly women and Goddess-focused
participants, certainly represent and enact gender for themselves in different ways than
Pagan men from the Radical Faeries movement, for example, who see male homosexuality as an expression of the Goddess or the divine within them.
As we have seen, an alternative (or complement) to Wicca like Reclaiming Witchcraft, with its critique of patriarchy and heteronormativity, would be ideal for LGBTQ
Pagans – who need to equate their spirituality with their gender or sexual identity – to
invest time and energy in. But, in reality, things are much more complex, and to take part
in one tradition or another has to do with more than gender or sexual orientation. Instead
we find that each person goes through a series of negotiations, significant in regards to
their life narratives, to the obstacles and hindrances they encounter, creating differentiated trajectories.
But how do Pagans who identify as LGBTQ reconcile their religiosity with their
identity? It is reasonable to think that the performance of rituals such as the Great Rite
especially, and ritual practice in general, demands for every LGBTQ person an active
work of negotiation in order to attune their religiosity with their gender or sexual identity
(Charbonneau, 2002; Harper, 2010; Sloan, 2008). Traditional Wiccan covens do encourage their high priestesses and high priests to be able to embody the Goddess as well as the
God, and solitary practitioners have the freedom they need to give different meanings to
highly sexual symbolic objects like the athame or the cup. There is every reason to
believe that the designation and the meaning of “feminine” and “masculine” become at
some point a very personal matter, in the name of a critique of normativity and instituted
authority (the Catholic Church and its authoritative regime being the exact opposite that
Pagans want to distance themselves from).

Let us now go deeper into the life story of three of my informants to see what kind of
negotiations the practices of witchcraft and magic allow them to operate in relation to
their gender and sexual identity. The goal is to determine what my informants’ experiences show in terms of negotiating gender norms, in Wicca and in society at large, and to
examine those negotiations in regards to power relationships as expressed through their
magical practices and their gender or sexual identity.

Callum
Callum, who is 24, is a preschool teacher, a conceptual and visual artist, an author, a
director and a diviner. He is single and lives with roommates in Montreal. Raised by a
Presbyterian family from Toronto, he came to Montreal, speaking both English and
French, after high school in order to study to become a teacher. Callum identifies as a
queer man, because he has no problem identifying with a masculine gender, but he
refuses to be categorized by his sexual orientation. He says: “I have an issue with
masculinity, but not with being masculine. [ . . . ] I feel like a man, like, I’m masculine,
but I’m my own masculine, which is not aggressive and domineering and all of those
things that identify with patriarchy.” By the age of four or five, he already suspected he
was queer, but it was only when he discovered Pagan religiosities that he was able to
express and fully assume his queer sexual identity. However, Callum, who describes
himself as a magician, as in a practitioner of magic, has only joined the Pagan movement
because it gives a more familiar voice to his solitary practice.
Callum was raised in a very liberal household, and with the coming of the internet, he
was able, at a young age, to satisfy his interest for other cultures from around the world,
like those of Egypt, China and Scotland, the country of his ancestors. In his teenage
years, he had already explored different spiritualities like Buddhism and Wicca, which
he quickly gave up for a much more chaotic spirituality inspired by the Radical Faeries
and LaVey’s Satanism. He then immersed himself in punk culture, alcohol and drugs,
leaving aside his interest for spirituality until he got to college and took anthropology
classes about magic. After the death of his grandfather, he started building small altars
dedicated to his deceased grandparents and to his ancestors in general. Very moved by
the symbolism surrounding death, the passing of time and the cycles of life, he also
maintains on these altars the image of the Mexican goddess of death, Santa Muerte,
which he sees as a multicultural archetype of healing and magic. He says: “She protects
queers, the sex workers, people who ride motorcycles, drug dealers, people who cross the
border, the things that institutions are against.” He also believes in respecting and
honoring the spirit of the place, in his case the spirits of the city who inhabit urban
spaces and fill them with life. Callum advocates thusly a spiritual or religious experience
that is rooted in the physical world and the material but also devoid of gendered or, at
least, heteronormative content.
In the same way, his job as a preschool teacher allows him to teach children some
general knowledge, also present in contemporary Paganism, which is seen as universal,
such as the changing of the seasons, the celebration of cultural diversity, basic astronomy, elements like fire and water, as well as energy-raising techniques. Outside of work,
Callum also started to gather, for Samhain or the summer solstice for example, a small

Simon
Simon is a 28-year-old transgender man. He is an author and he lives in Montreal. He is
also married to a transgender woman with whom he has a polyamorous relationship. He
identifies as pansexual, but often comes out as bisexual, for “simplicity’s sake.” Simon
comes from a very conservative Protestant adoptive family who never understood his
situation as a transgender man and he had to cut communications with them after they
refused to attend his wedding. Because they also enforced several stereotypes regarding
gender on the young girl that they thought he was before his transition about 10 years
ago, he had a very difficult time socializing in school and he was bullied until he went to
college. There, he opened his horizons on bisexuality and sex when he met his first
bisexual “female crush.” Moreover, he studied Japanese in Ottawa in order to eventually
teach English in Japan, but his transition represented a more pressing matter when he
finished his studies. His interest in Japan also led him to train in ninjutsu, which he sees
as a spiritual practice. He equally sees his work as an author as a spiritual calling.
When Simon started to pay attention to pre-Christian deities, his family met him with
silence and suspicion. Before going to college, Simon had trouble finding a spirituality
that suited him. In his words: “I kept on trying to find some sort of spiritual practice that
was natural and comfortable to me, but kept knocking against walls. It felt like I was sort
of trying to codify a personal code of ethics and could never settle on anything that
worked.” In fact, at the age of 18, Simon got baptized in order to reaffirm his faith, but it
did not take very well. When he and his wife got married, they had an unofficial
ceremony at which they officiated themselves, adapting every aspect of it (like the vows,
both wearing masks instead of a veil for the bride, and all of them sitting in a circle and

Today, Simon is part of a northern-tradition kindred in Montreal, but he has already
been in a similar group when he was still living in Ottawa. The chieftain of this kindred
of about eight friends of queer sensibility is a transgender woman and every member is
encouraged to have their own personal beliefs and practices. Additionally, the members
of the kindred also attend the Kaleidoscope Gathering together, a Pagan festival very
popular for its respect and acceptance of sexual diversity. The kindred used to meet often
at the time of the New Moon to do dumb suppers in honor of the Handmaidens of Freya
and the god Loki.5 At Samhain, for example, they normally make offerings to honor the
dead, which is very important to Simon who feels the need to be protected from dark and
negative energies. At Yule, they have an all-night vigil, and at Beltane, they sometimes
have what they call sacred orgies.
In fact, the kindred focuses especially on each person’s best abilities. Some of them
do medium work, others drumming or hypnosis, for example. Simon’s role is that of the
“Unholy spoon,” which jokingly means a priest. The story behind this title is that Simon
once volunteered to be the designated driver while he was cooking, using a “spoon
without holes,” the in-joke being that they had to give him that title while organizing
a sex magic ritual. For Simon, this shows the Lokian quality of the kindred, for which
humor and laughter are an important aspect of sociability and religiosity.
Simon identifies more specifically as a sex mage. As such, he practices magic by
raising sexual or bodily energy in order to cast spells. With his wife, he uses sex to raise
energy that will be directed towards a specific goal set by the kindred. He often uses this
magical practice for personal matters as well. He describes it as such:
My job is a spiritual calling, so when I feel low energy, like the atmosphere is gummy, not
good for creativity, I will put on music and dance because I feel that my spellcasting is best
done by kinetic energy, that’s why I make a good sex mage. I tend to use my body for
channeling energy. That’s kind of like a marriage of my job, physical health and spirituality.

He also maintains multiple relationships, which he calls alliances, with gods or
goddesses of the northern pantheon. In exchange for diverse blessings (energy,
protection, health, financial security, agility), Simon made oaths with gods and
goddesses like Tyr, Var, Girda, and others, or with his ancestors and passed teachers, to maintain different altars or shrines, to meditate each day, or to burn incense
regularly in their honor.
When Simon decided to transition and change his name, he felt he had found his real
self. He explains:
I just didn’t want the surname of the family that rejected me. Also as being raised as a
female, it had always been my expectation that if I ever were to get married, I would take on
the surname of the man I would be married to. So I thought it appropriate that, at long last,
finding this male side of myself that I wanted to live as, and accepting and loving this
person, that it was appropriate for me to take on the surname of this person. I saw it as a
marriage within myself, that I was finally coming together.

Nevertheless, he believes in a deep connection between the way he lives his lives, how

Sarabee
Sarabee is a 28-year-old bisexual woman who lives in Montreal. She is an independent
massage therapist who tends to people’s spiritual well-being. She identifies as a bisexual
woman, and is in a polyamorous relationship with a man.
She grew up in a small town in Ontario, Canada, in a family of farmers who instilled
her with values of work and devotion. Sarabee always felt different from her family, as
she was a very solitary and introverted child, even though she would later pretend to be
an extrovert for the sake of her studies. She was raised by a taciturn Presbyterian father
and a Catholic mother, for whom church attendance was not mandatory. In fact, when
Sarabee decided she did not want to attend anymore, her parents did not hold it against
her, allowing her to explore religions like Hinduism and Buddhism.
Sarabee describes herself as a very sensual and sexual person. She has always been
interested in sports and the nurture of the body, but after her studies, she hurt her back
and had to stop working. Then, she started traveling and lived in different parts of
Canada and in Australia for a short period of time. She identifies as a witch, a spiritualist,
an empath, and, most importantly, a healer and an “alltheist,” a word that she coined
herself to refer to the fact that she believes that every little bit of knowledge about God
and the divine describes one aspect or another of what the divine truly is. She also
describes her spirituality as exploration, and as reaching for enlightenment by “trying
to be the light.”
In her teenage years, she studied Wicca and contemporary witchcraft, but she was
always more interested in performing her own personal rituals to the full moon, for
example, “working on a natural 28 days cycle” or with a simple candle. Not very inclined
to do complicated rituals, she practices energy work (meditation and visualization) in
order to heal people, often from a distance, or change her perspective on a situation. She
also uses some divinatory tools such as astrology (Western, Chinese and Vedic), tarot,

In that sense, even though she never had any prolonged contact with any Pagan community, except at the Kaleidoscope Gathering, a Pagan festival where we met, she feels

Sarabee once had a serious relationship with another woman, whom she still calls
her wife. What she describes as a “sacred sisterhood connection” or a “soul-sister
connection” was even consecrated in a small ritual on the beach, back when they were
in Australia, which culminated in their getting the same tattoo, a symbol of eternity on
their wrists. They saw this as real wedding vows. Although very much in love, they
never engaged in a physical relationship. Sarabee thinks of her love for women as nonpossessive and being deeper than her love or sexual attraction for men. In fact, she and
her boyfriend have an open relationship and are currently learning more about polyamory. Sarabee specifies that she and he connected on “a very humanitarian level” for
that reason and that he completely understands her bisexuality as a spiritual part of her.
She says:

seems very one or the other.

Sarabee sees herself as being able to shift between masculine and feminine roles,
depending on the romantic or sexual nature of her relationships. When she speaks about
gender, religion and magic, she doesn’t seem very concerned by the oppositions between
them. In the same way, she never really came out as bisexual. She identifies as such, but
does not let her sexual identity define her lifestyle or her beliefs. In fact, she uses the term
“bisexual” with hesitation because it restricts her to a “one or the other” mentality,
leaving anything in-between unspoken for.

Witchcraft, Magic and Agency: An Open and Fluid Definition
The form of witchcraft I have studied in the Pagan community of Montreal is entirely
influenced by Wicca. Nevertheless, this research suggests that queer people often drift
away from Wiccan traditions and focus more on the practice of some type of magic as an
alternative to what has become nowadays a more and more mainstream religion. It is,
then, useful to understand Wicca as a gateway to what queer Pagans seek in terms of
spiritual and magical practices.
Just as some scholars have shown, between French and English anthropological
traditions (Keck, 2002), the social nature of witchcraft and magic, Wicca and contemporary witchcraft in Montreal are very much caught between such cultural differences.
Evans-Pritchard suggests, for instance, that witchcraft is, by essence, a very anti-social
act that needs to be revealed and overturned with divination and sorcery. For others, like
Bronisław Malinowski and Marcel Mauss, magic is rooted in the individual and pragmatic use of mana, the force inhabiting everything or every action having a socially
given meaning, and therefore sacredness, in a specific culture. Others, like Claude LéviStrauss and Favret-Saada, thought that magic had a logic of its own, that it was performative in itself and didn’t need a function in society or any culturally given meaning,
because it always refers to itself. This is what poststructuralist scholars would later use to
deconstruct power relationships, creating almost every type of hierarchy or binary model
there is: male and female, dominant and dominated, objective and subjective, etc.
The common thread of all these previous theories, which unfortunately cannot be used
as such to understand contemporary witchcraft,6 is that they imply a very technical yet
metaphorical nature of magic. Even if they suppose it is grounded in the physical and
concerned with pragmatic matters such as illness, hunger, reproduction or death, they

also agree on the fact that its presence in a society is entirely dependent on the systemacity
of thought (Keck, 2002) of that culture and on the sociopolitical circumstances that see
it flourish. As such, witchcraft is manifested mostly through language. But in our
world, and especially in the Pagan movement, we rarely talk about the same things
in the same way.
Pagan magic is very complex to understand because its conceptualization in the Pagan
community comes from evolutionist scholars of religion who also have influenced the
development of anthropology and the conceptualization of magic and witchcraft. Scholars know better today than to use James G. Frazer’s or Edward B. Tylor’s theories to
interpret magic in any society, but the laws of sympathetic magic, analogy and contagion
they have discerned in their works still have a hold on Pagan thought and beliefs.
Furthermore, many scholars have shown how Romanticism and esotericism have shaped
the Pagan movement (York, 1995; Hanegraaff, 1996, 2013). Pagans today are well aware
of this historical perspective and their religiosity is still filled with a nostalgia for the land
as well as by a fascination with the mysteries of life and death.
According to S.J. Tambiah, “the performance of the magical rites achieves and marks
changes of quality and state through the ‘activation’ of the analogy by the ‘performative’
rite of magic” (Middleton, 1987: 89). In the case of Pagan magic, this idea can generally
be found in the popular hermetic phrase “As above, so below.” This implies a sort of
immanent transcendence, a valorization of the process of life and death, which doesn’t
occur with the intervention of a higher divinity lodged in the heavens, but rather of one
located in the immensity of the universe, and made apparent in the idea that we humans
are a very small part of a much bigger living organism comprising our planet and our
solar system as well as invisible smaller life forms.
In that sense, this idea that humans are inherently part of a natural system, which
determines the conditions of our existence, is very important to practitioners of witchcraft and to those who use magic as an alternative spirituality. For some of them, this
natural system is one of diversity and chaos, while others see it as being much more
ordered and governed by laws of physics and many more that have yet to be understood
by science (biology, genetics, neurology). Sometimes it is both. Whatever it is, magic
has to do with gaining power over that world, through knowledge and experience. This
was particularly important for the Pagan movement, when Wicca came from Great
Britain to the USA and encountered Goddess spirituality and second-wave feminism
(Adler, 1986; Pike, 2004; Clifton, 2006) as both perceived witchcraft to be a category of
patriarchy and reclaimed the word for themselves. For Bailey,
Conceptions of magic, however designated, function broadly in many cultures to differentiate certain actions sharply from otherwise potentially quite similar rites, rituals, or
practices. Drawing these distinctions is an exercise in power, and a major area of comparative focus must be to explore what roles such differentiation plays in various societies, who
controls this differentiation, and to what ends that created difference is employed. (Bailey,
2006: 9)

My research adopts this comparative focus towards concepts of magic and gender, in
order to determine how they are enacted in my informants’ narratives and by their

practices. For this reason, it considers that gender, as a category of Western thought, just
as magic, is a social construct.
For that matter, in her 1949 book The Second Sex (Le deuxie`me sexe), French existentialist philosopher Simone de Beauvoir wrote “One is not born, but rather becomes, a
woman.”7 Since then, feminist studies have not slowed down in showing the social
construction of gendered identities and sexes. One of the most influential scholars to
have popularized this reflection is the American philosopher Judith Butler. With her
research (1990, 1993), she became, next to the feminist theorist Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick
(1990), an important queer theorist emerging from feminist theory. This constructivist
theory questions the normative association between gender and sexual orientation within
someone’s identity. In this perspective, it is society that forces people to take on gender
roles that tie them to certain types of regulatory behaviors. These theories go against the
ones under the essentialist denomination that claim the existence of a static, unchanging,
biological and natural essence to the sexes.
For Butler, this association between gender and the category of “sex” is basically a
fiction that establishes hierarchy through an endless array of repetitions and categorizations, forcing heterosexuality as the norm. More precisely, Butler’s theories demonstrate
how gender is performative, in the sense that it has the potential to perform an identity.
This notion, borrowed from Austin’s linguistics (1962), supposes that every utterance in
discourse which compels people to identity with one gender or the other (“It’s a boy!”,
“Women are crazy!”; and the way first names are formed) can be understood as a type of
normative ritual where the body becomes the instrument – already gendered – through
which the gender binary will manifest itself. A common example for this is the “I
pronounce you man and wife,” which sets gender roles as well as power relations
between the bride and the groom, women and men (Butler, 1993: 224–225). This pertains to an obligation to constantly reaffirm one’s gender, and ultimately shows that
gender is essentially a social act that we always have to perform. Everything going
against this equation will eventually be forced to fit the norm. In that sense, Butler
clearly explains how the body is the first locus where gender norms are expressed. The
body reproduces these norms through multiple acquired stylizations (with clothing; in
the way the body moves and occupies space; in linguistic codes) that often go unnoticed
by the person performing them, who does so without any awareness.
Nevertheless, gender, being performative and repetitive, is always supported by a
subject who gives it efficacy and who also can subvert it. This is what scholars in queer
theory have come to know as agency, individual people’s power to act upon their own
lives. This concept refers to the idea that a subject is inevitably at the heart of power
relations, which are constructed or transformed even though they are fought against.
Jacques Guilhaumou describes the agency of feminine power in these terms: “a particular method in relation to structures of domination, a relative autonomy in the choice of
means to control one’s life, a capacity to resist dominant discourse, and, finally, at the
most general level, an action (historical agency) conducive to change” (Guilhaumou,
2013: 28; my translation). This short definition of agency can also be applied to every
form of power relations and resistance tactics. In fact, people like Callum and Simon,
who do not conform to the gender binary for a reason of identity, often react quite
strongly against these norms, which are still promulgated within the majority of Pagan

My research suggests that the Pagan community of Montreal, the groups and members who take part in it, adopt a similar attitude towards perceptions of gender and
definitions of magic. Even though the discourse that is spread by Pagans can be very
fluid in regards to those concepts, it still is related to the representations and practices
that can be categorized as opposing the ideologies found in Wicca and in Reclaiming
Witchcraft.
Wicca and Reclaiming Witchcraft often develop specific views of the feminine and
masculine polarities. For Wiccans, the symbolic efficacy of the “magic” ritual is the
product of the symbolic union of feminine and masculine polarities under the guise of the
Great Goddess or the Horned God. For some Montreal Reclaiming witches, magic comes
from the empowering potential the polarities possess at a subjective level. In that sense,
magic, always having to do with the notions of power (in the sense of personal empowerment, for example) and control (of the self and of the energy generated by the ritual
(Rabinovitch and Lewis, 2002)), is employed at times of need often related to identity
issues regarding the sex–gender coupling. For this reason, the journeys of the LGBTQ
Pagans who participated in this study are very particular. These journeys comprise
negotiations based in magical practices, and the LGBTQ Pagans implement them to
reconcile the many sexualized components of Wicca and the Pagan tradition to which
they feel they belong the most, according to their sexual or gender identity.
In analyzing their discourse, which takes the form of a personal life story – the
creation of a narrative surrounding their identity, whether sexual or religious – magic
appears to be as much as a goal to reach and the means by which it can be reached.
Pagans like Callum, Simon, and Sarabee often describe magic in three different but
interconnected ways, as a transformative technique, a healing method and a way to
interact with the world or, in other words, to build themselves up as the subject of their
practices and narratives.
First of all, magic refers to the manipulation of “energy” by means of will, and
represents a symbolic and ritual efficacy (Harvey, 1997; Rabinovitch and Lewis,
2002). This energy allows everybody, regardless of their gender, to provoke transformations. This first motive corresponds to this idea of transformation. The self is the primary
target of this transformation, from an inside point of view to its relational aspect, but it
also can reach other people, the rest of society and even the world (Luhrmann, 1989).
This perspective on magic is very much akin to the type of negotiation Callum performs
in regards to gender within Pagan religiosities. For him, gender must be understood in
terms of fluidity, an energy that can be manipulated for diverse purposes and ritualized
on different appropriate occasions.
A second motive is about healing, which overlaps with the first motive of transformation whilst attributing a specific content. The healing of the self and of others is a major
preoccupation in the testimony of my respondents. It is generally accompanied by some
techniques, magical or energetic, whatever they are, destined to transform a state of
suffering, imbalance or incompleteness into a state of well-being, balance and unity. For
my respondents, healing can be that of a body affected by illness, theirs or their loved

ones’. It can also be a psychic or emotional healing, in the case of acceptation of their
homosexuality and/or a conflicted family situation (Magliocco, 2004; Pike, 2001; Reid,
2004). For the most part, these physical and emotional dimensions are related and find
their solution in some type of “spiritual” anchoring which provides them with the means
to overcome this feeling of disunity, deficiency and imbalance. This particular way to
understand magic echoes Sarabee’s negotiations of gender norms in Pagan religiosities.
She promotes equality between men and women, by giving the bigger part of power to
women within Paganism, and by seeing commonalities and easing differences between
cultures at large.
The third motive refers more to magic as a way of interacting with the world, more on
the side of “meaning itineraries” like the ones Raymond Lemieux has described (2003).
Magical practices are thus inscribed in a project of construction of the self, aiming to make
the person whole and to put into a coherent narrative different contradictions of existence
and singular events (Gauthier and Perreault, 2008; Gauthier, 2012). According to definition, magic “is not a way to form a one-to-one relationship between an individual and an
otherwise unattainable goal; it is an elaborate, dramatic metaphor for the relationship
between an individual and the universe” (Rabinovitch and Lewis, 2002: 152). This suggests that magic, in the metaphorical sense very much present within the narrative of my
respondents, allows them to give meaning to the whole of their experiences, whether they
are positive or negative. This motive was particularly present in the narrative of Simon,
who identifies as a sex mage in order to change, on a larger scale, attitudes and stereotypes
surrounding gender and sexuality. Simon’s negotiated practices pertain to the subversion
of gender norms within Paganism at a higher level. For him, as a male-identified individual, they come into play in the construction of his own ethics, which reach for the fluidity
of sexual and gender identities, as well as for personal empowerment.
Certain parallels can be drawn between these three motives around magic within
Pagan religiosities and the degrees to which queer Pagans transform gender norms inside
and outside of a ritual context. The first one, as portrayed with Sarabee, points to a
process of inversion of the gender binary: giving power to women in the frame of Pagan
religiosities. While it empowers women and LGBTQ individuals on a personal level and
among like-minded Pagan people, it still conveys hierarchies, and ultimately reinforces
the norm on a larger social scale. A second parallel, found in Callum’s narrative, suggests an underlying process around the normalization of gender fluidity: masculinity and
femininity are models that can be played with and manipulated at will to take direct
control and gain power over norms. It is a contradictory type of negotiation which does
affect positively people’s chance to see their non-binary identities accepted within their
communities, but has a limited impact on dominant stereotypes. Finally, a process
leading to the displacement of gender norms, which pushes for an inclusive attitude
towards queer and transgender in society at large, emerges from Simon’s narrative.
Simon’s negotiations around gender norms in Paganism are very complex and they are
sharply reflected in his participation in the Pagan community. They also have an impact
outside of the ritual context as they transform the ways he identifies as a man in his
everyday life. This type of negotiation aims to deconstruct or subvert gender norms, and
without achieving a permanent transformation, opens up the possibilities for different
masculinities and femininities on a larger social scale.

Conclusion
This article has demonstrated some of the negotiations that queer and transgender Pagans
carry out in order to partake in the Pagan community of Montreal. With practices they
reclaim as being magical or dealing in witchcraft, in the multifaceted sense explained in
the first section, Pagans who have different views on gender are able to make sense of
their own experiences, and deal in their own way with gender norms. Moreover, they
also agree in their critique of genders, and on how these should be perceived on a more
equal level. At the same time, they choose to adopt different magical practices that
appeal to the physical and spiritual aspects of their being in a less normative way than
what usually occurs in Wiccan or mainstream Paganism or in Western culture in general.
The way my informants give meaning to different conceptions of gender also shows
how their construction of gender comes into play with a community whose members also
have some forms of gendered religiosities. Callum, Simon, and Sarabee have quite
divergent views on gender but they can agree, even though they identify differently,
that gender and sexual identities are basically highly fluid and subjective realities. With
that said, what seems to matter to them most is the power or the magic they get when they
can identify freely to a non-binary gender or sexual identity and when they can share it
with like-minded people. The different queer negotiations they perform do not seem like
much of a chore anymore, but are more like an occasion to gain agency in the way they
tend to their spirituality.
As we have seen, magical practices or religious activities associated with contemporary witchcraft derived from Wiccan traditions allow more discreet or solitary members
of the Montreal Pagan community to celebrate their identity without feeling as if the
somewhat binary Wiccan way of expressing gender is the only one available. Even if
they do not necessarily subvert the gender binary or change social heteronormativity on a
larger scale, they certainly allow certain Pagans to tend to their own personal needs, as
well as to facilitate or improve their relationship with others, whether they are religious
or not, Christian or Pagan.

Portrait du néo-paganisme
Paganisme et néo-paganisme
D’emblée, le terme « paganisme » est avant tout une
catégorie attribuée par le christianisme : le paganisme
est la religion de l’autre (Augé 1982). Dans sa première

acception, sa racine latine paganus qualifie les gens de
la campagne, des paysans qui, avant l’avènement du
christianisme en Europe, auraient été fidèles à un polythéisme antique, qu’il soit grec, romain, celtique ou
autre. Cette interprétation du paganisme, reprise à
l’époque romantique de la fin du XVIIIe siècle, a répandu la nostalgie d’un passé bucolique disparu jusqu’à
sa redécouverte fantasmatique dans l’ère victorienne
urbanisée du XIXe siècle. Cet imaginaire du paganisme
renvoie à une vision enchantée de la Nature à laquelle
correspondrait un panthéisme de type animiste (Davies
2011:2; Azria et Hervieu-Léger 2010:851; Pearson
2002:16–18).
La seconde acception du terme « paganisme » (pagani) est également le produit de l’histoire chrétienne
et désigne à la fois le paysan et les non-chrétiens. Plus
précisément, durant l’Antiquité et le Moyen Âge, être
chrétien signifiait être citoyen. Ainsi, les paı̈ens, nonchristianisés et non-citoyens, étaient considérés comme
des hérétiques. Le terme « paı̈en » devint alors le qualificatif péjoratif accolé aux peuples premiers, dits primitifs, pratiquant une religion naturelle et spontanée ou,
au mieux, syncrétique et donc bâtarde (Davies 2011:2;
Azria et Hervieu-Léger 2010:851; Pearson 2002:18–19).
Le paganisme renvoie également à la religion de la
localité, enracinée dans une vision du monde antique et
particulariste plutôt qu’universelle, suivant le culte primitif des énergies de la terre et des éléments (Davies
2011:3; Pearson 2002:19–20). Les peuples préchrétiens,
tout comme les paysans, vouaient un culte aux particularités du lieu en y rattachant un riche symbolisme : la
source d’eau alimentant le village, la montagne le surplombant, l’arbre centenaire, la caverne, jusqu’à la
moindre anfractuosité ou singularité. Ce sens le plus
large du « paganisme » recoupe par ailleurs dans ses
grandes lignes les systèmes religieux des peuples aborigènes, amérindiens et africains entre autres, largement
étudiés par la recherche anthropologique (Azria et
Hervieu-Léger 2010:851–853).

L’alternative Reclaiming Witchcraft
C’est au cours des années 1960 que la Wicca a migré de
l’Angleterre en Amérique du Nord, grâce à des personnalités comme Raymond et Rosemary Buckland. La
« sorcellerie contemporaine » allait acquérir davantage

fut à l’origine d’une profonde transformation de la Wicca
de Gardner. C’est sans surprise que l’on apprend que la
côte ouest des États-Unis, et tout particulièrement la
Californie, fut une terre fertile pour l’émergence d’une

libération sexuelle et du féminisme. L’influence du
féminisme est particulièrement patente chez Starhawk
pour qui, militance et spiritualité vont de pair. En ce
sens, la valorisation de l’archétype féminin propre à
la Wicca se prêtait particulièrement bien à ce type
d’investissement. Reprenant l’idée d’une « découverte »,
appuyée par certaines fouilles archéologiques, de sociétés
soi-disant matriarcales dans le pourtour méditerranéen,
Starhawk et ses comparses ont formulé une critique
féministe de l’histoire des civilisations en opposant à la
version masculine (his-story) communément admise une
version féminine (her-story) qui en est à la fois l’envers
et la contestation. Ces sociétés matriarcales et pacifiques sont présentées comme étant le véritable creuset
de la civilisation, et auraient pratiqué une religion originelle et millénaire organisée autour du culte de la
Déesse Mère. Cette réinterprétation féministe de l’histoire a eu une forte résonance dans des pans entiers
du néo-paganisme, au point de devenir un de ses

religion de la Nature associée au Féminin plutôt qu’en
religion de fertilité fondée sur la dualité complémentaire
et procréatrice homme / femme (Pike 2004; Albanese
1990). Cette réorientation s’inscrit par ailleurs dans la
mouvance Nouvel Age. L’expression « Nouvel Age » fait
référence à l’avènement de l’Ère du Verseau, supposée

au Féminin (Heelas 2008, 1996; Pearson 2002; Marron

Le néo-paganisme à Montréal
Si la Wicca (gardnérienne et alexandrienne) constitue
l’influence dominante au Québec et à Montréal, le courant Reclaiming Witchcraft y compte aussi ses adeptes.
On retrouve ces deux courants néo-paı̈ens représentés
parmi nos répondants. Malgré quelques divergences au
niveau des pratiques et des idéologies, la communauté
néo-paı̈enne de Montréal regroupe des néo-paı̈ens de
tous types : sorcières, wiccans, heathens, druides, etc.
En général, le mélange des traditions donne lieu à des
rituels publics très inclusifs. Ces rituels publics, qui se
produisent à l’occasion des différents sabbats au cours
de l’année, sont organisés en collaboration avec le
Centre de Ressources Paı̈ennes de Montréal (CRPM)
par les membres de la communauté qui désirent s’impliquer, peu importe leur affiliation personnelle. Ils gravitent généralement autour de la boutique ésotérique Le
Mélange Magique8 qui héberge le CRPM, de même que
le Crescent Moon School of Magic and Paganism. Une
autre boutique montréalaise, Charme et Sortilège, appartenant à la comédienne Marie-Renée Patry, constitue
un deuxième centre de gravitation qui fédère un public
davantage francophone et avec lequel nous sommes
moins familier (le CRPM est pour sa part bilingue, avec
une prédominance anglophone). Nous avons également
observé les rituels semi-publics qui sont organisés par
la communauté Reclaiming Witchcraft de Montréal, soit
des cercles de chants et de tambours qui ont lieu lors de
la nouvelle lune. Nous avons également pu participer à
certaines rencontres de la branche montréalaise du
coven wiccan Silver Wheel, fondée à Montréal dans les
années 1980. Ce sont les membres de ces deux derniers
groupes qui organisent le plus souvent les rituels publics
du CRPM. Nous avons aussi participé aux rituels organisés par un groupe plus ou moins structuré et qui se
rejoint avant tout sur les médias sociaux (Facebook en
l’occurrence), la « Communauté wiccanne », composée
d’environ 25 personnes. Malheureusement, le nombre
de personnes faisant partie de la communauté néopaı̈enne de Montréal est difficilement évaluable.
Lors des rituels publics ou privés, et à l’occasion de
festivals ou de célébrations à Montréal, les adeptes du
néo-paganisme pratiquent des rituels « magiques »
consistant à manipuler « l’énergie » (dans un objectif de
guérison, par exemple) et à célébrer le cycle des saisons
et les cycles lunaires. Plus précisément, les esbats sont
des célébrations mensuelles ayant pour thème le passage des cycles de la lune, associée à la femme, tandis
que les sabbats, au nombre de huit, sont des célébrations liées au cycle du soleil, associé à l’homme et ayant
apparemment pour origine d’anciennes fêtes agricoles
celtiques. Ensemble, ils divisent l’année en huit, corres-

pondant aux rayons de la Roue de la Vie. Il s’agit d’évènements majeurs dans le calendrier rituel néo-paı̈en où
sont célébrés respectivement :
la naissance du Dieu (le soleil) à Yule ; le rétablissement de la Déesse à l’Imbolc ; l’arrivée du printemps
(Ostara) ; l’union ou le mariage de la Déesse et du
Dieu (Beltane) ; l’arrivée de l’été (Litha) ; la première
récolte (Lughnasadh) ; la dernière récolte (Mabon) ;
la mort du Dieu (Samhain). (Cunningham 1998:142–

Ces festivals, qui dépeignent les cycles naturels comme
une interaction entre le Féminin et le Masculin, sont
célébrés par la majorité de la communauté néo-paı̈enne,
sous différents noms selon les affiliations.
Lors de ces célébrations, les participants (hétérosexuels ou LGBT) expriment leur identité par le biais de
leur corps, que ce soit par la danse ou le port d’un
costume qui reflète leur orientation sexuelle (cf. aussi
Harper 2010; Hasbrouck 2004; Neitz 2004; Pike 2001).
Nous avons observé à plusieurs reprises dans les festivals Wic-Can Fest ou HarvestFest, ainsi que dans les
rituels publics de la communauté montréalaise, des rites
spécifiquement dédiés à un genre ou l’autre, à un sexe
ou l’autre, suivant tous les possibles. Des rituels plus
exclusifs, comme la Tente rouge (Red Tent) par exemple, liée aux « mystères féminins entourant les menstruations », ne concerne que les femmes. D’autres rituels
séparent les hommes et les femmes : placés d’un côté et
de l’autre du centre rituel, ils se rejoignent en chantant,
passant de la polyphonie au chœur. La séparation entre
les sexes s’accommode parfois d’exceptions, notamment
par la présence d’homosexuels préférant s’intégrer au
sexe opposé. Ainsi peut-on retrouver des hommes du
côté des femmes et vice versa. Il n’est pas impossible
de voir certains hommes s’essayer au baladi (le bricolage tolère des emprunts pour le moins inusités !) ou
revêtir des paillettes (le port de costume est fréquent et
laisse libre cours aux expressions et fantaisies individuelles), tandis que certaines femmes aiment à porter la
corne (en référence au Dieu Cornu).

Avant tout, la magie fait référence à la manipulation
de « l’énergie » par la volonté et représente l’efficacité
symbolique et rituelle (Rabinovitch et Lewis 2002;
Harvey 1997). Cette énergie permet à tous, indépendamment du sexe ou du genre, d’opérer des transformations. Le premier motif correspond à cette idée de
transformation. Le soi est l’objet premier de cette transformation, dans ses dimensions intérieures et relationnelles, mais elle peut aussi parfois s’étendre aux autres,
à la société voire au monde (Lurhmann 1989).
Le second motif est celui de la guérison, qui recoupe
le motif de la transformation tout en lui conférant un
contenu spécifique. La guérison de soi et des autres
est une préoccupation particulièrement prégnante dans
les témoignages de nos participants. Les techniques
magiques, quelles qu’elles soient, sont destinées à transformer un état de souffrance, de déséquilibre ou d’incomplétude en un état de bien-être, d’équilibre et
d’unité. Pour nos répondants, la guérison peut être celle
d’un corps atteint par la maladie, le leur ou celui d’un
proche. Elle peut aussi être d’ordre psychique et affectif,
dans le cas de l’acceptation de l’homosexualité ou d’une
situation familiale conflictuelle (Magliocco 2004; Reid
2004; Pike 2001). Pour la plupart, ces dimensions physiques et affectives sont liées et trouvent leur solution
dans un arrimage « spirituel » qui leur procure les
éléments à partir desquels surmonter ce sentiment de
désunion, de manque et de déséquilibre.9

en tant que manière d’interagir avec le monde, sur le
mode des « itinéraires de sens » décrits par Raymond
Lemieux (2003). Ainsi la pratique magique s’inscrit dans
un projet de construction du soi visant l’unité de la
personne et la mise en récit d’une cohérence par-delà
les contradictions existentielles et les évènements singuliers (cf. aussi Gauthier 2012; Gauthier et Perreault
2008). Suivant cette conception, la magie « n’est pas un
moyen pour créer un lien entre un individu et un objectif
autrement inatteignable. Elle constitue plutôt une métaphore complexe et puissante traduisant la relation
entre un individu et l’univers10 » (Rabinovitch et Lewis
2002:152, traduction des auteurs). La magie, dans cette
acception métaphorique très présente dans les récits
des répondants, permet d’attribuer un sens à la totalité
de leurs expériences, tant positives que négatives.

Conclusion
This article tried to demonstrate some of the queer negotiations that
LGBTQI Pagans, especially transgendered and queer people, have
to do in order to take part in the Pagan community. With rituals like
the third-­gender ritual at Kaleidoscope Gathering, Pagans who have
different views on gender but who still ally in their non-­binary con-­
ception of it, can agree that it is primarily a social construct. They
also agree in their critique of gender and on how they should be per-­
ceived on a more equal level. In this manner, they perform gender in
a less normative way than what usually occurs in Wiccan or main-­
stream Paganism or in Western culture in general.
The way our informants give meaning to different conceptions
of gender also show how their construction of gender come into
play with a community who also has some forms of gendered reli-­
giosities. Manon, Maria, and Sam have somewhat divergent views
on gender but they can agree, even though they identify with dif-­
ferent genders or sexual identities, that they are basically highly
ÁXLGDQGVXEMHFWLYHUHDOLWLHV:LWKWKDWVDLGZKDWVHHPVWRPDWWHU
to them most is the power or the magic they get when they can
identify freely to a non-­binary gender or sexual identity and when
they can share it with people like them. The different queer nego-­
tiations they have to perform don’t seem like much of a chore any-­
more, but more like an occasion to gain agency in the way they tend
to their spirituality.
Having a space such as Lokabrenna Kindred, where they can learn
about themselves and share with their friends, allows the members
to take part in the larger Montreal Pagan community celebrations
without feeling like the somewhat binary Wiccan way of expressing
gender is the only one available. They feel less excluded from the
community that is Paganism in general as their identity can be rep-­
resented too, joining forces with a growing number of heterosexual
Pagans who continue to be invaluable allies.
