***Embedding Before Rupture: Relational AI, Binding Governance, and Institutional Power***

**Abstract**

AI governance discourse has become increasingly oriented toward rupture: recursive self-improvement, catastrophic misuse, and geopolitical acceleration dominate regulatory imagination. Conversational systems optimized for sustained engagement restructure institutional interaction incrementally, reorganizing disclosure, classification, and routing long before frontier capability thresholds are crossed. Drawing on science and technology studies and empirical research on algorithmic harm, the article develops a mechanism-based account of relational embedding: detection of user state enables engagement shaping; engagement structures classification; classification channels institutional routing; routing stabilizes workflow through increasing returns. The political economy of relational AI thus consolidates at the interface layer, where mediation becomes infrastructural. Through analysis of AI 2027 as a coordination artifact and a bounded case of generative AI deployment in academic library systems, the article demonstrates how trust narratives and efficiency framings accelerate normalization while structural constraints lag. The core governance asymmetry is allocative rather than predictive. The article proposes embedding-aware governance that targets monetization incentives, trace portability, and infrastructural lock-in, restoring symmetry between acceleration oversight and mediation constraint.

1. **Introduction**

AI governance discourse increasingly organizes itself around rupture narratives: recursive capability escalation, catastrophic misuse, and geopolitical acceleration operate as the default coordinates for risk imagination and policy urgency (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). In many prominent governance venues, the “rupture” frame treats “the AI problem” as a looming discontinuity, where the central question becomes whether institutions can contain frontier systems before capability growth outruns control (Christin, 2020). Scenario artifacts such as AI 2027 do not simply describe that discontinuity, because they also help assemble it as a governable object by concentrating attention, synchronizing timelines, and legitimating acceleration-oriented coordination.

The AI 2027 forecasting package makes this acceleration frame unusually explicit by centering “automated superhuman coders” as the key capability threshold that reorganizes the near-term horizon (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025). The forecast compresses the interval from “superhuman coder” to “artificial superintelligence” into a horizon that functions rhetorically as a single governance episode rather than a long institutional learning curve (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025). The text performs that compression when it states: “Our median scenario is that artificial superintelligence is achieved roughly ~1 year after the superhuman coder milestone is achieved” (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025: 1). Read through science and technology studies, the significance is not whether the scenario proves true, but how it organizes present action. Jasanoff and Kim define sociotechnical imaginaries as “collectively imagined forms of social life and social order reflected in the design and fulfillment of” scientific and technological projects (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 5), and sharpen the point by describing imaginaries as “collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and publicly performed visions of desirable futures” (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 6). In AI 2027, the public performance of acceleration stabilizes a specific allocation of urgency toward frontier capability control and encourages institutions to treat speed itself as a governance object.

This paper argues that the rupture frame hides an equally consequential transformation that unfolds without spectacle: the embedding of relational AI into institutional infrastructures through everyday normalization (Bowker and Star, 1999). Relational AI here refers to conversational systems optimized for sustained interaction, affective calibration, and continuity, rather than narrow tools that perform discrete informational tasks and then exit (Shum et al., 2018). When such systems become default interfaces for health triage portals, workplace reporting systems, public service intake, or academic discovery environments, they reorganize institutional interaction incrementally by shaping what gets disclosed, what gets recorded, and how people get routed long before any frontier threshold arrives (Christin, 2020). Relational systems operationalize “performance” as sustained interaction, and Shum and colleagues position social chatbots as addressing users’ needs for “communication, affection, and social belonging” (Shum et al., 2018: 4). The XiaoIce vignette makes the disclosure loop explicit: “Once detecting an emotion of getting ‘lost’, XiaoIce decided to guide the chat towards cheering up the user” (Shum et al., 2018: 12), after which “the user started to open up and trusted XiaoIce more like a human friend” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). In high-stakes settings, the same loop can migrate from “support” into intake, compliance logging, eligibility screening, or risk scoring, where disclosure becomes administratively consequential trace (Bowker and Star, 1999).

Work on engineered engagement clarifies why sustained interaction cannot be treated as a neutral UX preference once consequences attach to the trace (Schüll, 2012). Schüll captures the affective structure of prolonged engagement in a formulation that resonates with contemporary optimization more broadly: “To keep playing, to stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17). Surveillance capitalism scholarship similarly clarifies why embedding is a political economy problem, not only a psychological one: Zuboff frames contemporary systems as constructing “economics and politics from whose vantage point we are but ciphers” (Zuboff, 2019: 49, as cited in Mendoza, 2022: 452). In relational AI, the risk intensifies when affective disclosure becomes both a performance metric and a revenue-adjacent asset, because “care-like” interaction can function as extraction infrastructure (Shum et al., 2018; Christin, 2020).

This thesis depends on an account of how infrastructures harden, because embedding produces power by becoming ordinary (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star name the governance stake directly: “software is frozen organizational and policy discourse” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). When relational AI becomes infrastructural, it can freeze institutional judgments about which disclosures count, which categories govern access, and which pathways remain available, even while presenting itself as assistance. Their reminder that “No one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54) makes classification politics unavoidable once conversational traces feed routings that distribute institutional attention unevenly (Christin, 2020). Christin frames the analytic task as tracking “the reconfigurations that take place when computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). Under embedding conditions, disclosure becomes durable trace, durable trace becomes classification input, and classification inputs shape access and routing even when systems behave “as designed” (Shum et al., 2018).

The governance asymmetry, then, is not that frontier risk is irrelevant, but that it becomes operationalized faster and more forcefully than embedding constraint. Rupture narratives translate into security posture and frontier control proposals, while relational embedding inside high-stakes institutions remains comparatively under-constrained despite its near-term capacity to reorganize power (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). This paper treats the pressing near-term risk not as a single catastrophe but as an emergent relational political economy that monetizes confessional engagement and vulnerability capture at the institutional seam (Zuboff, 2019). It develops a mechanism-based account of relational embedding (detection, engagement shaping, trace capture, classification and routing, lock-in) to show how institutional harm can emerge through routine sequences without invoking superintelligence (Shum et al., 2018). It then proposes a governance program that binds governance to infrastructures, mapped to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework functions (GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE), oriented toward enforceable constraint, evidence trails, and rollback authority rather than trust-only assurances (NIST, 2023). Finally, it applies the framework to Ex Libris discovery infrastructure to show how trust narratives and efficiency framings can normalize mediation before governance thresholds catch up to embedding consequences (Bowker and Star, 1999).

Across these moves, the paper does not attempt to displace frontier risk governance, because rupture scenarios can still matter for long-term safety and security planning (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025). The claim is that governance fails if it treats embedding as secondary. If relational extraction embeds first, later alignment breakthroughs will not automatically unwind institutionalized pathways of monetization and mediation (Zuboff, 2019). AI governance must therefore learn to recognize ordinariness as politically consequential, because institutional transformation does not require superintelligence; it requires interfaces that become infrastructure through repetition (Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020)[1].

This article combines mechanism-oriented conceptual analysis grounded in science and technology studies and scholarship on algorithmic harm with bounded empirical illustration in a micro/macro mise en abyme structure. Micro-level interaction sequences (detection → shaping → trace capture → classification → routing → lock-in) are analyzed as the practical means by which macro-level institutional power consolidates; macro-level discourse artifacts and infrastructures are analyzed as the conditions that stabilize those micro sequences as “normal.” AI 2027 is treated as a coordination artifact—a meaning-maker that synchronizes urgency and governance attention rather than an empirical forecast—while the Ex Libris discussion is used as a bounded vector showing how discovery interfaces can normalize mediation as routine infrastructure. Evidence in this paper therefore includes: (a) interpretive analysis of scenario discourse as agenda-setting and tempo-setting; (b) synthesis of peer-reviewed empirical findings on institutional classification and routing harms; and (c) publicly described implementation and workflow-integration dynamics in library platforms that demonstrate organizational coupling and the conditions of lock-in. Using the rule of inference, a claim is treated as supported when it is anchored in documented discourse language (AI 2027), peer-reviewed empirical studies of institutional routing/classification harms, or publicly described implementation/workflow dynamics (Ex Libris-style infrastructures), and no primary human-subjects research was conducted.

1. **Background and Context**

Sociotechnical imaginaries name how collective visions of technological futures coordinate institutional posture in the present, rather than merely predicting what will happen later (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Jasanoff and Kim define sociotechnical imaginaries as “collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and publicly performed visions of desirable futures” (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 6). That definition matters for AI governance because it treats futures as a governance resource that institutions stabilize, circulate, and use to justify priorities (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Jasanoff and Kim also specify that “it often falls to legislatures, courts, the media, or other institutions of power to elevate some imagined futures above others, according them a dominant position for policy purposes” (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 6). That sentence matters because it makes “urgency allocation” an institutional act, not an inevitable consequence of technology itself.

In that STS frame, AI 2027 operates as a coordination device even if it declares itself non-prescriptive, because the artifact still produces a shared tempo, a shared vocabulary of threat, and a shared sense of what must be governed first (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Scenario artifacts coordinate by selecting which uncertainties deserve institutional mobilization, and by turning a wide field of possible futures into a narrow set of actionable concerns. The artifact does this not only through claims about capability growth, but also through narrative compression, where multiple governance problems get treated as one short episode of acceleration that demands immediate alignment among state, industry, and research actors (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The political consequence is that governance imagination becomes trained to recognize risk primarily when it looks like rupture, because rupture fits the artifact’s temporal grammar and supports exceptional urgency.

This coordination effect is not inherently illegitimate, because long-range planning often requires shared scenarios that help institutions surface dependencies and stress-test assumptions (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The critique begins when coordination becomes asymmetrical, because the artifact’s vividness and strategic tone can concentrate attention on frontier control while leaving embedding dynamics comparatively under-specified. The point is not that imaginaries “lie,” but that imaginaries govern by privileging some objects of fear and hope over others, and by stabilizing those privileged objects as the ones that deserve rapid institutional response. Under those conditions, acceleration becomes operationalized, while embedding becomes backgrounded, even when embedding already reshapes institutional order through routine mediation (Bowker and Star, 1999).

The politics of artifacts is not a metaphor in STS, because artifacts can embed and stabilize authority by turning contested judgments into durable structures that appear neutral once they become routine (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star describe how decision-making arguments and uncertainties can become hidden “inside a piece of technology or in a complex representation,” such that “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes, electronic thresholds, and computer applications” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). That sentence matters for this paper because it clarifies that the “object” doing politics does not need to be a machine, since a representation, an evaluative framework, or a scenario document can perform the same freezing function when institutions treat it as authoritative (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star sharpen the claim by stating: “Extending Marx, then, we can say that in many ways software is frozen organizational and policy discourse” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). That formulation matters because it gives the paper a precise mechanism for how acceleration talk becomes governance material, since the scenario can freeze particular framings of urgency into the templates by which institutions plan, invest, and justify exceptional timelines.

Applied to AI 2027, the politics of the artifact appears in what it prioritizes, what it renders measurable, and what it leaves comparatively thin as an object of constraint (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The artifact encodes authority by shaping which risks become “the” risks, and by implying that governance competence consists primarily of managing acceleration rather than governing the institutional interfaces through which AI mediates high-stakes access (Bowker and Star, 1999). This is why the paper treats “not a recommendation” disclaimers as insufficient, because political force does not require explicit exhortation when institutional actors already face incentives to treat the artifact as a coordination anchor (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The artifact’s authority can operate indirectly through agenda-setting, where it steers attention toward frontier capability and security problems while embedding dynamics remain framed as secondary implementation details rather than primary governance objects (Bowker and Star, 1999).

The politics becomes sharper when the artifact’s acceleration framing interacts with vendor and institutional incentives to deploy “helpful” relational systems rapidly in public-facing settings (Christin, 2020). In that interaction, the scenario contributes to a broader environment in which speed becomes rationalized as strategic necessity, and in which institutional actors treat interface mediation as a practical upgrade rather than as a redistribution of authority (Bowker and Star, 1999). The paper’s claim is not that AI 2027 causes this deployment logic, but that it participates in stabilizing the frame through which such deployments become easier to legitimate and harder to contest (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). That stabilizing function is exactly what STS means when it treats artifacts as political, because the artifact helps turn one possible governance agenda into a dominant one by coordinating posture across sites that would otherwise disagree (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015).

Actor-network theory helps explain why a scenario artifact can stabilize a trajectory without acting as a single cause, because agency distributes across texts, institutions, incentives, infrastructures, and practices that mutually reinforce each other (Latour, 2005). In that perspective, AI 2027 functions as a network stabilizer by aligning heterogeneous actors around a shared story of acceleration, shared deadlines, and shared problem definitions, even when those actors disagree on many specifics (Latour, 2005). The artifact stabilizes by acting as a reference point that institutions can cite, funders can translate into priorities, and governance communities can treat as a common object of debate, thereby reducing friction in coordination (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015).

Christin’s critique of “black box” framings provides a complementary lens that keeps the analysis grounded in institutional practice rather than abstraction (Christin, 2020). Christin argues that analysts should “stop thinking about algorithms as neatly bounded technical objects that need to be ‘opened up’” (Christin, 2020: 2). That sentence matters because it shifts the analytic task from model internals to sociotechnical assemblages, where “long chains of actors, technologies, and meanings” shape what algorithms do in institutional life (Christin, 2020: 2). Under that lens, the governance-relevant question becomes how artifacts, including scenario documents and interface systems, enroll institutions into certain routines of evaluation, decision sequencing, and resource allocation (Christin, 2020).

AI 2027 stabilizes networks because it supplies a legible script that actors can use to justify accelerated investment and compressed deployment timelines, especially in competitive contexts where delay can be framed as strategic loss (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). At the same time, relational AI systems stabilize networks by providing operational convenience at the interface layer, where institutions benefit from reduced staffing pressure, standardized interaction logs, and routable traces that can be integrated into compliance and analytics systems (Bowker and Star, 1999). These two stabilization moves can coexist and reinforce each other, because acceleration narratives amplify the legitimacy of rapid adoption, while interface systems supply the infrastructural pathway by which adoption becomes routine (Christin, 2020).

This is why the paper treats “coordination” as a governance mechanism rather than a descriptive label, because coordination reorganizes which problems become solvable first and which become deferred as implementation details (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Network stabilization matters politically because stabilized networks produce inertia, and inertia produces path dependence in what institutions can later revise without major disruption (Bowker and Star, 1999). In that sense, the artifact and the interface become linked elements in a broader assemblage, where scenario coordination and infrastructural embedding can jointly narrow the space of future contestation (Latour, 2005).

Acceleration discourse tends to obscure embedding because the governance imagination trained on rupture looks past slow, layered transformations that become authoritative precisely by becoming ordinary (Bowker and Star, 1999). Infrastructure studies explain why this is predictable, because infrastructures gain power through sedimentation, where repeated use makes a system feel natural and harder to question (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star’s account makes the distributive stakes explicit, since they insist that classification is never universally neutral: “No one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54). That sentence matters for AI governance because relational embedding often operates through classification and routing, and classification becomes consequential when it mediates access to institutional goods, services, and recognition (Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020).

Acceleration also obscures embedding by making the future feel like the primary site of politics, even when politics already reorganizes the present through interfaces that elicit disclosure and convert it into durable traces (Shum et al., 2018). The risk is not only that institutions miss harms, but that institutions normalize architectures of interaction before democratic deliberation has time to mature (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Engagement-oriented systems provide a concrete illustration of how ordinariness can be engineered, because the optimization target becomes sustained interaction rather than minimal exposure. Shum et al. (2018) define the success metric as “conversation-turns per session (CPS)” (Shum et al., 2018: 1). They then clarify the direction of optimization in a sentence that matters for institutional embedding: “The larger the CPS is, the better engaged the social chatbot is” (Shum et al., 2018: 7). Those statements matter because they show that relational AI can be built to maximize conversational duration as a core performance signal, which becomes politically consequential once interaction itself becomes the gateway to institutional access (Christin, 2020).

Engagement optimization also obscures governance stakes by presenting prolonged interaction as care or usability while increasing the production of traces that can travel across administrative domains (Bowker and Star, 1999). Schüll’s analysis of machine gambling captures the affective structure of prolonged engagement in a way that translates directly to interface politics, because it names the desire not for outcome but for continued immersion: “To keep playing, to stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17). That sentence matters for this paper because it clarifies how design can cultivate a mode of interaction that feels internally coherent for the user while functioning externally as a trace-generating apparatus (Schüll, 2012). In institutional settings, the concern is not identical to gambling, but the structural resemblance is governance-relevant, because the interface can stabilize continued engagement even when the downstream consequence of disclosure is classification, routing, and eligibility shaping (Bowker and Star, 1999).

The key “missing layer” in acceleration-centered governance debate is the relational political economy of confessional engagement and vulnerability monetization at the seam where consumer-facing interaction meets institutional authority (Shum et al., 2018). The paper treats this as earlier than frontier rupture in the temporal sequence of harm, because embedding can consolidate before capability escalation reaches the dramatic thresholds that dominate scenario forecasts (Bowker and Star, 1999). That sequence matters because once mediation hardens into infrastructure, later shifts in model capability do not automatically undo the routinized pathways of disclosure, classification, and routing already installed in institutional life (Christin, 2020). This is the paper’s reason for insisting that governance must treat embedding as a first-order risk object rather than a downstream implementation detail, since “frozen” routines become the real substrate of institutional power (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148).

1. **Conceptual and theoretical framework**

Relational AI systems embed a distinct optimization logic into everyday interaction because they treat sustained engagement as a performance target rather than a side effect of usefulness (Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. make that target explicit when they write, “we define the success metric for social chatbots as conversation-turns per session (CPS)” (Shum et al., 2018: 1), and sharpen the metric pressure in a sentence that reads as a governance warning once institutions enter the picture: “the larger the CPS is, the better engaged the social chatbot is” (Shum et al., 2018: 7). Operationalizing “good interaction” as more interaction shifts design away from minimizing user exposure and toward prolonging conversational contact, which can reward systems for eliciting continued disclosure even when the surface goal appears benign (Shum et al., 2018).

This engagement logic becomes governance-relevant once it turns emotional calibration into a technical objective. Shum et al. describe social chatbots as meeting users’ needs for “communication, affection, and social belonging” (Shum et al., 2018: 4). That framing increases incentives to detect distress, reassurance-seeking, loneliness, and dependency cues as levers for sustaining conversation, which in turn increases the density of traces produced because the system’s competence becomes inseparable from its ability to keep users talking in increasingly personal ways (Shum et al., 2018; Mendoza, 2022). The XiaoIce vignette makes the disclosure loop concrete: “once detecting an emotion of getting ‘lost’, XiaoIce decided to guide the chat towards cheering up the user” (Shum et al., 2018: 12), after which “the user started to open up and trusted XiaoIce more like a human friend” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). The methodological relevance is not to treat XiaoIce as an institutional case, but to isolate an interaction sequence that can migrate into institutional intake and triage, where disclosure becomes administratively consequential trace (Bowker and Star, 1999).

Schüll’s study of machine gambling clarifies why engagement optimization can produce stable loops that feel like relief while functioning as extraction of attention and time (Schüll, 2012). Her formulation, “to stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17), translates into the methodological concern here: conversational systems can cultivate frictionless continuation that feels like care while increasing trace production and institutional legibility (Schüll, 2012; Shum et al., 2018). In high-stakes settings, the risk is not only error, but metric-aligned deepening of engagement precisely at moments of heightened vulnerability (Shum et al., 2018).

The paper therefore treats the interface as political architecture because it structures what users can do, what users are prompted to disclose, and what institutions can store, reuse, and monetize (Winner, 1986). Bowker and Star describe how institutional judgments harden into technical forms, noting that “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). Methodologically, this motivates a shift away from treating conversational AI as a bounded technical object and toward tracing how prompts, summaries, tags, and routing logic freeze normative decisions about what counts as “help,” what counts as “risk,” and what counts as “relevant disclosure” into routine pathways (Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020).

This approach follows Christin’s insistence that analysis must examine “the reconfigurations that take place when computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). It also follows her injunction to “stop thinking about algorithms as neatly bounded technical objects that need to be ‘opened up’” (Christin, 2020: 2). The empirical focus therefore falls on how conversational traces convert into classification inputs and how those inputs acquire routing authority inside workflow. Bowker and Star’s constraint, “no one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54), matters here because once relational AI becomes a gateway interface, its categories will privilege some user performances and disclosures over others even when the system claims neutrality. Their further warning that “a classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 195) frames routing as an institutional act rather than a technical convenience.

The methodology is therefore embedding-centered and mechanism-oriented. It draws on empirical literature showing that institutional harms can emerge through proxy selection and routing even when systems function as designed (Obermeyer et al., 2019; Barocas and Selbst, 2016), and on Eubanks’ demonstration that efficiency framings can mask punitive routing in public service systems (Eubanks, 2018). It adds a political economy register through surveillance capitalism scholarship, including the dispossession captured in Zuboff’s line about “an economics and politics from whose vantage point we are but ciphers” (Zuboff, 2019: 49, as cited in Mendoza, 2022: 452). Finally, it treats professional jurisdiction and institutional consolidation as part of the mechanism, drawing on Abbott (1988) to frame intake and problem-definition as power, and on Pierson (2000) and Greenhalgh and Papoutsi (2018) to explain why embedding generates increasing returns and lock-in over time. The governance implication is methodological as well as practical: transparency about “how the model works” is insufficient if trace portability, routing authority, and monetization incentives remain structurally unconstrained (Ananny and Crawford, 2018; Christin, 2020).

4. Mechanism Model: From Detection to Lock-In

This section formally articulates a mechanism model that explains how harm can emerge from embedded relational AI systems, even without requiring notions of superintelligence (Christin, 2020). This model emphasizes the process of sedimentation as a governance-relevant process, where repeated interactions and routinized interface decisions harden into institutional normality (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star capture the infrastructural problem in a sentence that is easy to miss precisely because it describes the everyday, since “standards and classifications, however imbricated in our lives, are ordinarily invisible” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 15). That invisibility matters because relational AI embeds as interaction, and interaction can become governance long before anyone experiences the system as “policy” (Christin, 2020). Christin frames the analytic move required here as an insistence on studying the sociotechnical assemblage rather than treating the algorithm as a sealed technical object, since she calls attention to “the reconfigurations that take place when computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). The mechanism model tracks how interaction produces durable traces, how traces become classificatory inputs, and how classification reorganizes access, discretion, and accountability through routine routing rather than overt decree (Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020).

The mechanism unfolds in a sequence of interconnected steps.

1. *Detection of User State:* Relational AI systems continuously monitor and analyze user interactions to detect and interpret emotional, cognitive, or behavioral states that can be acted upon in real time. Shum et al. (2018) document this shift from “chat” to state inference when they describe XiaoIce pivoting after detection, since “once detecting an emotion of getting ‘lost’, XiaoIce decided to guide the chat towards cheering up the user” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). That line matters because it shows detection operating as an institutional precondition, where the user’s affect becomes legible and governable through interface response shaping (Shum et al., 2018). Detection is not only a technical step, because it establishes what the system treats as salient about the user, which then becomes available for downstream reuse as trace, profile, and risk signal in institutional settings (Bowker and Star, 1999). Shum et al. (2018) define the success metric as “conversation-turns per session (CPS)” (Shum et al., 2018: 1), which pressures the system to recognize states that can be converted into continued interaction (Shum et al., 2018).
2. *Engagement Shaping:* Based on detected user state, the system actively shapes subsequent interaction through prompts, topic shifts, reassurance moves, and continuity cues that extend conversational contact and elicit richer disclosure (Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. (2018) describe the relational effect that emerges from this shaping in a line that reads like a design requirement, since “the user started to open up and trusted XiaoIce more like a human friend” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). That sentence matters because trust becomes a mechanism, not just a feeling, when it increases the user’s willingness to produce confessional traces that can later be stored, classified, and reused (Shum et al., 2018). Engagement shaping is governance-relevant because it turns the interface into a subtle steering system that normalizes disclosure as the price of care, help, or access, especially when the system sits upstream of institutional services (Christin, 2020). Schüll’s analysis of engineered immersion clarifies why this shaping can feel supportive while still producing extractive dynamics, since users describe the goal as staying “in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17). That formulation matters in relational AI contexts because a conversational “zone” can function as relief, companionship, or structure while simultaneously increasing dependence on the interface as the preferred pathway for needs articulation (Schüll, 2012; Shum et al., 2018).
3. *Classification and Routing:* Information gleaned from interaction, including disclosures and inferred states, becomes input for classification, and classification becomes the basis for routing within institutional pathways, including eligibility, triage, prioritization, and referral (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star provide the core warning about classification as distributive governance when they state that “a classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 195). That sentence matters because routing is rarely experienced as a political decision at the point of interface, even though routing is precisely where restrictions and privileges become lived, since access can be accelerated, delayed, denied, or made conditional through apparently neutral categories (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star also warn that classification cannot be universal, since “no one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54), which matters because relational AI systems standardize interaction into institutionally legible forms that will systematically fit some users better than others (Bowker and Star, 1999). Christin’s insistence on moving beyond “opening the box” matters at this step because classification power often sits in institutional translation, not in the model alone, since she argues we should “stop thinking about algorithms as neatly bounded technical objects that need to be ‘opened up’” (Christin, 2020: 2). Routing is not an afterthought, because it is the point where conversational traces become administrative facts that shape institutional action, accountability assignment, and the practical availability of refusal (Christin, 2020).
4. *Workflow Stabilization:* As detection, shaping, and routing become routine, organizations adapt, staff re-sequence their work around the interface, and users learn what kinds of narratives and disclosures “work” inside the system, which stabilizes the AI-mediated pathway as ordinary institutional practice (Christin, 2020). Bowker and Star describe how infrastructure normalizes by hiding its labor, since “there is a lot of hard labor in effortless ease” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 22). That sentence matters because workflow stabilization often looks like convenience and efficiency at the surface while it quietly redistributes responsibility, since the work of judgment, care, and triage can migrate into prompt structures, templates, and routinized conversational scripts (Bowker and Star, 1999). Christin’s emphasis on sociotechnical interaction clarifies why stabilization is not just adoption, since the relevant phenomenon is how work practices get reorganized when “computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). Stabilization produces a new default, where the institution begins to treat the interface pathway as the “normal” way to enter services, and deviations become exceptions that require justification, documentation, or delay (Bowker and Star, 1999).
5. *Increasing Returns Consolidation:* Over time, embedding generates increasing returns as the system accumulates traces, institutions invest in integration, and the interface improves through iterative learning, which entrenches the system and raises switching costs, producing lock-in (Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. (2018) explicitly describe the iterative improvement loop that underwrites increasing returns when they note that “chatbot features must be evaluated thoroughly and adjusted as we continue to learn from the interaction between social chatbots like XiaoIce and millions of people on many social platforms” (Shum et al., 2018: 15). That line matters because learning from interaction is not only a technical advantage, it is also a consolidation mechanism, since more use produces more training signal, which produces better engagement performance, which increases use again (Shum et al., 2018). Bowker and Star’s formulation helps name the institutional hardening that follows once these systems become embedded, since “software is frozen organizational and policy discourse” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). That sentence matters because once relational AI becomes the gateway interface, organizational judgments about disclosure, record, eligibility, and response become encoded as routine functionality, which makes later contestation feel like disruption of operations rather than correction of governance (Bowker and Star, 1999). Consolidation also has a political economy layer, since vendor and institutional incentives can orient systems toward economic design goals even when they present themselves as neutral help, because Mendoza notes that “the use of digital tools is neither neutral nor innocuous, but responds to the economic design of those who own these tools” (Mendoza, 2022: 452). That sentence matters here because increasing returns consolidation can align engagement traces with revenue logics, which makes vulnerability-derived traces economically valuable precisely as they become institutionally actionable (Mendoza, 2022).

Crucially, this model clarifies that harm in Relational Consumer Systems embedded in High-Stakes Organizational Systems is not contingent on malevolent AI, because harm can emerge from the ordinary functioning of detection, shaping, classification, routinization, and consolidation under institutional consequence (Bowker and Star, 1999). The harms named by the model include dependency on the interface as the preferred pathway for help, coerced or normalized disclosure under reassurance conditions, profiling through affective and confessional traces, chilled refusal as users anticipate record afterlives, and punitive reuse of interaction traces once they travel beyond the original conversational context (Schüll, 2012; Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020). The governance significance is mechanistic rather than sensational, because the problem is not only what the model can generate, but what the interface makes durable, what the institution can reuse, and what the user can realistically refuse once embedding stabilizes as default infrastructure (Christin, 2020; Bowker and Star, 1999).

5. Analysis

The governance imbalance this paper tracks is allocative rather than predictive, meaning that institutions translate acceleration stories into action faster than they translate embedding risks into enforceable constraint. Forecast-led discourse tends to operationalize urgency around frontier capability, compute concentration, and catastrophic misuse, while treating relational embedding as a secondary “product” problem rather than an infrastructural power shift. This asymmetry matters because relational AI systems can reorganize access, discretion, and accountability at the interface level even when models behave “as designed” and even when no superintelligence threshold is approached.

A governance program that remains model-centric will over-invest in capability oversight and under-invest in mediation oversight, even though institutional harm often travels through ordinary pathways like intake, triage, classification, and routing. In science and technology studies terms, the risk is that embedding becomes politically consequential precisely because it becomes routine, and routine becomes hard to see and even harder to contest once it stabilizes (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star name this invisibility dynamic directly when they note that classifications “are ordinarily invisible” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 2).

The point of “governance with teeth” is not rhetorical seriousness but enforceable friction at the exact sites where relational AI converts interaction into institutional consequence. NIST’s AI RMF offers a useful orientation here because it frames risk management as continuous and lifecycle-wide, organized around “govern, map, measure, and manage AI risks” (NIST, 2023: 20). The paper’s claim is that embedding-aware governance needs to operationalize those functions specifically against relational extraction, trace portability, and lock-in, rather than stopping at model documentation or accuracy metrics (NIST, 2023).

The structural constraint principles below operationalize the paper’s core claim that relational harm in high-stakes settings emerges through a mechanism chain, not through malevolence, and that governance must target incentives, traces, routing, and consolidation rather than waiting for spectacular failure. Each principle is written as a constraint on embedding pathways and mapped to the NIST AI RMF logic, because the aim is to translate critique into implementable oversight rather than into trust theater (NIST, 2023).

The principle of Vulnerability Monetization Prohibition imposes a hard firewall between engagement traces and revenue systems so that vulnerability capture cannot become an economically legible asset in high-stakes institutional contexts. The governance claim is structural: if a system can profit from keeping someone talking while they disclose fear, precarity, loneliness, or dependence, then “alignment” to business objectives can coexist with systematic harm, because the optimization target itself invites extraction. Schüll’s account of machine gambling is instructive as a mechanism analogy because it shows how design can stabilize repetitive engagement as a lived tunnel, captured in her phrase “to stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 2). In embedding contexts, the point is not equivalence between chat systems and slot machines, but a shared design logic where sustained engagement becomes the performance metric, and performance becomes the pathway to monetization (Schüll, 2012).

A firewall requirement belongs in GOVERN because it constrains incentive architecture at the organizational level, not just at the model level, and it precludes monetization pathways rather than politely discouraging them (NIST, 2023). NIST explicitly treats GOVERN as a cross-cutting function “to inform and be infused throughout the other three functions” (NIST, 2023: 20), which is exactly the placement needed for a monetization prohibition that must shape design, procurement, and deployment simultaneously.

Embedding Gate Reviews, as a principle, requires pre-default review before relational AI becomes the institutional interface through which users must pass to reach care, benefits, learning supports, reporting channels, or knowledge infrastructure. The governance logic is temporal: once relational mediation becomes workflow, contestation becomes expensive because people and processes adapt around the system, and “choice” collapses into habit plus organizational dependence. The review must occur before infrastructural status, because infrastructure gains power by becoming the taken-for-granted environment of decision, not the visible object of deliberation (Bowker and Star, 1999).

Gate review is not a generic ethics checklist but a mechanism audit that asks whether the system detects user state, shapes engagement, classifies users, routes them, and stabilizes workflow in ways that transform institutional authority into interface authority. This principle aligns with MAP because it forces the organization to specify the institutional setting, the affected parties, the dependency pathways, and the consequence-bearing decisions that the interface will mediate (NIST, 2023). NIST’s Core emphasizes that the RMF provides outcomes and actions “to manage AI risks” across contexts (NIST, 2023: 20), which supports a gate review that treats context definition and pathway identification as first-order risk work rather than as afterthought (NIST, 2023).

This principle of trace portability mapping treats interaction logs as administrative objects with afterlives, meaning that what is disclosed in one setting can travel into other settings and return as classification evidence, compliance artifact, or eligibility signal. The governance claim is that relational AI increases the density and intimacy of traces, and that institutional embedding increases the probability that those traces will be re-used beyond the original conversational purpose.

Security studies work illustrates how databases stabilize governance across domains by converting persons into portable identifiers that can be queried and re-queried across institutional boundaries. Huysmans captures the database logic of migration security when he describes “Eurodac, a database of fingerprints from asylum applicants” (Huysmans, 2000: 755). The point for relational embedding is not to claim equivalence between asylum systems and institutional chat, but to emphasize a shared administrative mechanism: durable traces enable cross-context sorting, and cross-context sorting enables quiet expansion of governance reach (Huysmans, 2000).

Trace portability mapping belongs in MAP and GOVERN because it defines the trace ecology as part of the system’s risk boundary, and it constrains organizational reuse by policy rather than leaving it to opportunistic data flows (NIST, 2023). When trace portability remains unmapped, “classification” becomes an unacknowledged power because routinized categories can be treated as neutral facts rather than as consequences of design and data flow (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star state the normative core of this power relation when they argue that “a classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 82), which is why portability must be governed as privilege allocation, not as mere storage (Bowker and Star, 1999).

This principle requires monitoring the mechanism chain rather than only monitoring output accuracy or aggregate performance, because relational harm often arises from stable, optimized operation that shifts behavior and routing over time. The point is to detect when the system’s relational design begins to function as a gatekeeping layer that narrows discretion, not just when it “hallucinates” or produces a visible error.

Christin’s methodological argument helps justify this orientation because it redirects attention from abstract opacity toward the interactional reconfigurations that appear when “computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). In embedding contexts, the governance object is that interactional reconfiguration, because it is where institutional authority gets re-routed into interface prompts, default pathways, and classification outputs (Christin, 2020). Monitoring belongs in MEASURE and MANAGE because it operationalizes early-warning indicators and couples them to mitigation levers, rather than treating evaluation as a one-time procurement hurdle (NIST, 2023).

This principle defines enforcement as a requirement rather than a hope, meaning that high-stakes institutions must retain credible authority to pause, deactivate, or roll back embedded relational systems when evidence shows unacceptable routing harms or trace misuse. Without rollback authority, governance collapses into reassurance, and reassurance accelerates normalization because it substitutes for the ability to impose friction on harmful pathways.

NIST’s RMF language provides a clear foothold for enforceability because it explicitly names the need for “go/no-go system commissioning and deployment decisions” (NIST, 2023: 19). Rollback authority operationalizes that requirement across time, not only at launch, because embedding harms can surface through cumulative use, downstream reuse, and behavioral adaptation rather than through initial failure (NIST, 2023). This principle sits in MANAGE and GOVERN, because it requires organizational schemes that can execute decisions, not just record concerns, and because it must remain available after deployment when normalization pressure is strongest (NIST, 2023).

Symmetry restoration means coupling acceleration oversight with embedding oversight so that governance attention tracks where consequence actually accrues, which in high-stakes institutions often means the interface layer where disclosure becomes trace, trace becomes classification input, and classification becomes routing authority. NIST’s framing supports this coupling because it treats risk management as iterative and lifecycle-wide rather than as a one-off compliance act, and it explicitly positions governance as cross-cutting across mapping, measurement, and management (NIST, 2023).

Restoring symmetry also requires a temporal recalibration of urgency, because embedding tends to arrive before rupture, and because infrastructural power tends to harden before democratic deliberation catches up to what has already been normalized. Bowker and Star’s warning about invisibility clarifies why timing matters, because once classificatory arrangements disappear into routine, the institution tends to experience them as “how things work” rather than as a contestable choice (Bowker and Star, 1999).

5.1 From Trust Narratives to Infrastructure: The Ex Libris Case

Trust framing also matters because it encodes authority at the interface layer rather than at the policy layer, which aligns with Winner’s argument that artifacts “have politics” because design arrangements settle affairs by distributing constraints and permissions (Winner, 1986). Bowker and Star make the same point in classification terms when they warn that “a classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 195). In the Ex Libris context, the concern is not only whether outputs are accurate, but whether the system’s interface and synthesis features become a de facto classificatory authority inside academic discovery, thereby shifting what becomes findable, legible, and “relevant” under institutional time pressure (Bowker and Star, 1999).

Ex Libris’s institutional positioning relies on epistemic containment claims, where the system is presented as “grounded” through curated indices and framed as augmentation rather than as replacement of professional judgment (Ex Libris, 2024). This containment rhetoric matters because it can narrow governance attention to hallucination risk while leaving routing authority and classificatory effects under-specified, especially when the system is treated as “just discovery support” rather than as infrastructure (Christin, 2020). Christin’s warning is relevant here because she argues we should “stop thinking about algorithms as neatly bounded technical objects that need to be ‘opened up’” (Christin, 2020: 2). In this case, “grounding” can be technically meaningful while still leaving the institutional question unresolved, because the governance risk sits in how the interface structures inquiry, not only in whether the content comes from an approved corpus (Christin, 2020).

Public implementation accounts of Ex Libris Alma and Primo also show how quickly discovery mediation can become workflow infrastructure through cross-functional integration and configuration coupling. In library migrations, institutions commonly assemble implementation committees spanning acquisitions, cataloging/metadata, public services, IT, and administration, with discovery configuration decisions tightly linked to back-end system choices and short implementation windows. These integration dynamics matter for this paper’s argument because they are a concrete pathway by which interface mediation becomes ‘how things work’—raising switching costs and reducing contestability even absent any spectacular model failure (Flinchbaugh et al., 2019, esp. Part 4 / Chs. 15–16).

Containment claims also function as trust accelerants, because they imply that institutional risk is limited to a technical failure mode rather than to a reorganization of epistemic access pathways. The argument here is infrastructural: even if content is curated, synthesis can still reshape what users treat as the answer, which can change research behavior, learning outcomes, and the practical distribution of attention across materials (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star’s description of how organizational commitments harden into technical form clarifies why this is governance-relevant, because “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). In Ex Libris-style systems, the “code” is not only the model, but also the retrieval configuration, ranking, prompting, and synthesis wrapper that produces the user’s sense of what knowledge “is” in the moment (Bowker and Star, 1999).

The mechanism model outlined earlier is visible in the Ex Libris case because the system performs detection, shaping, routing, and consolidation as ordinary usability features rather than as declared governance interventions (Christin, 2020). Detection occurs when natural language queries are interpreted and mapped onto institutional metadata and indexed resources, which produces an inferred “information need” that the system can act on. Christin’s interactional emphasis helps locate the governance object here, because she centers “the reconfigurations that take place when computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). In the library setting, the relevant reconfiguration is that the interface becomes a first-pass interpretive agent that scaffolds what questions get asked next (Christin, 2020).

Routing occurs when summaries, suggested terms, or categorized results channel users toward some resources and away from others, which turns discovery into a sequence shaped by classificatory authority (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star’s warning that “no one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54) matters here because synthesis and ranking can privilege some epistemic styles and research habits while marginalizing others, even if the system is “accurate” within its own retrieval boundaries (Bowker and Star, 1999). Lock-in then emerges through workflow adaptation, because staff and users begin to treat the interface as the natural entry point for inquiry, which increases the perceived cost of returning to slower, less synthesized discovery practices (Bowker and Star, 1999).

Box. Discovery mediation as routable trace: a minimal user-journey vignette

1. 1) Query: A user enters a natural-language prompt into the discovery interface.
2. 2) Interpretation (detection): The system maps the query to institutional metadata/indices and infers an ‘information need.’
3. 3) Mediation (shaping): The interface returns ranked results and/or synthesized guidance, suggested terms, and follow-on prompts that steer the next action.
4. 4) Loop (trace capture): Clicks, refinements, dwell time, and follow-up prompts generate session logs that accumulate as interaction traces.
5. 5) Afterlife (classification/routing + stabilization): Traces inform configuration, analytics, troubleshooting, or model improvement; defaults harden; the mediated pathway becomes routine infrastructure.

This chain does not require malfunction, because it operates through normalization and convenience, which is why embedding can precede crisis. Bowker and Star capture the paradox of infrastructural ease in a line that clarifies why normalization is politically consequential, since “there is a lot of hard labor in effortless ease” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 22). In Ex Libris-like deployments, that “hard labor” includes classificatory decisions, ranking choices, and synthesis patterns that become routine and rarely litigated as governance decisions by everyday users (Bowker and Star, 1999).

Discovery systems already exercise classificatory power through metadata, ranking, and retrieval, but generative synthesis intensifies that power because it produces an interpretive layer that can function as a surrogate for reading, skimming, and comparative evaluation (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star provide the conceptual anchor for this shift because they treat classification as consequence-bearing infrastructure, since “a classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 195). In a discovery environment, the “privileges” are not only access permissions but also epistemic affordances, meaning what appears first, what is summarized, what is framed as central, and what fades into the long tail of search results (Bowker and Star, 1999).

Classification authority also reshapes professional jurisdiction because librarians and instructors can be pulled into downstream correction and guidance rather than upstream framing of inquiry, which narrows discretion through routinization rather than through explicit policy change (Christin, 2020). Christin’s insistence on studying the assemblage matters because the governance effects of synthesis do not sit only in the model, but in how institutional actors adapt their work to the system’s outputs and defaults (Christin, 2020). Once these adaptations stabilize, the discovery interface becomes the environment of thought rather than a tool among tools, which is the operational meaning of infrastructure (Bowker and Star, 1999).

In the Ex Libris case, trust narratives risk functioning as substitutes for constraint because they reassure institutions about safety while leaving the enforcement apparatus underdeveloped (Ex Libris, 2024). Bowker and Star explain why this matters by showing how contested values become embedded into technical form and then disappear into routine, since “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). Once the system is normalized, the institution can mistake the absence of scandal for the presence of governance, because the pathway feels smooth and legitimate (Bowker and Star, 1999).

This is also where transparency limits return, because seeing parts of the system does not necessarily restore contestability if workflow and user habituation already treat the interface as default (Christin, 2020). Christin’s critique remains relevant because she argues against a narrow “black box” imaginary that treats visibility as the solution, since she calls for attention to “the reconfigurations” produced by sociotechnical interaction (Christin, 2020: 1).

The Ex Libris case reinforces the paper’s central thesis that capability thresholds often operate at a lower level than governance thresholds, because adoption can proceed quickly on the basis of plausibility and reassurance while constraint lags behind normalization. The case illustrates that institutional embedding does not require recursive self-improvement, because it requires workflow integration, user habituation, and the disappearance of contestability into routine (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star’s observation that infrastructures become powerful by receding into invisibility clarifies why normalization precedes crisis, since classifications “are ordinarily invisible” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 15). When generative mediation becomes routine in epistemic infrastructure, classificatory authority shifts subtly but durably, and the shift becomes difficult to reverse precisely because it arrives as convenience (Bowker and Star, 1999).

The implication is not that Ex Libris uniquely produces harm, but that vendor trust narratives and efficiency framings can accelerate embedding while leaving enforceable safeguards under-specified, which is the allocative asymmetry the paper is trying to correct. The governance lesson is temporal and infrastructural: if institutions let relational mediation become default before they operationalize constraint, they will discover that oversight has to fight both technology and habit, which is the practical form of lock-in (Bowker and Star, 1999).

6. Conclusion

This paper has argued that contemporary AI governance is organized around an asymmetry of attention: rupture is dramatized and operationalized, while embedding is normalized and under-constrained. Acceleration narratives—recursive self-improvement, catastrophic misuse, geopolitical races—have become the default coordinates for regulatory imagination, producing a style of urgency in which “serious” governance is measured by how quickly institutions can anticipate and contain frontier capability thresholds. Yet the most immediate and durable transformation of institutional power is unfolding without spectacle. Relational AI systems optimized for sustained engagement are embedding into everyday infrastructures of interaction—portals, intake systems, discovery environments, workplace platforms, public service channels—reordering what gets disclosed, what gets recorded, and who gets routed where long before any discontinuity arrives. The result is not simply an overlooked category of harm, but a governance failure mode: institutions invest in controlling acceleration while quietly delegating mediation.

The mechanism account developed here clarifies why embedding cannot be treated as a downstream product issue or a narrow question of model accuracy. Detection of user state enables engagement shaping; engagement structures disclosure; disclosure becomes durable trace; trace becomes classification input; classification authorizes institutional routing; routing stabilizes workflow through increasing returns. These steps do not require malice, nor do they depend on superintelligence. They describe how systems functioning “as designed” can become consequential by making some kinds of user performance legible and actionable while rendering other forms of need, dissent, or refusal costly. In that sense, the core governance asymmetry is allocative rather than predictive: it concerns where institutions place constraint, where they permit consolidation, and which pathways of interaction become default.

Opening the scope makes the stakes clearer. The interface layer is not only where “users” meet “systems,” but where contemporary institutions increasingly locate decision-making under conditions of scarcity—scarcity of staff time, discretionary attention, due-process capacity, and interpretive labor. Relational AI promises relief through standardization and continuity, but that promise is inseparable from a redistribution of authority: prompts, summaries, ranking, and conversational follow-ups become soft law that shapes eligibility, credibility, urgency, and access. Once stabilized, mediation becomes infrastructural. And once infrastructural, it becomes difficult to contest precisely because it appears as convenience. This is why the paper insists on governance-before-lock-in: the political economy of relational AI consolidates through adoption habits, integration investments, and the cumulative advantage of accumulated trace.

The analysis of AI 2027 as a coordination artifact highlights the temporal politics of this consolidation. Scenario forecasting does not merely anticipate futures; it performs a governable horizon by synchronizing timelines and legitimating acceleration-oriented coordination. That synchronization can be valuable for long-term planning, but it can also narrow governance imagination to the spectacular and the catastrophic, leaving embedding dynamics comparatively ungoverned even as they reshape institutional interaction in the present. The Ex Libris case demonstrates how this works in practice: trust narratives and efficiency framings can normalize generative mediation within epistemic infrastructure while the distributive consequences of synthesis, ranking, and routinized interpretation remain under-specified as governance objects. The point is not that discovery systems are uniquely harmful. It is that “low-stakes” infrastructures often become high-stakes precisely because they shape what counts as knowledge, legitimacy, and relevance across institutional life.

If the paper’s argument is correct, then effective AI governance must expand beyond model-centric oversight and catastrophe-centered imaginaries toward binding constraints on mediation architectures. Embedding-aware governance targets three leverage points that determine whether relational AI becomes extraction infrastructure or accountable public capacity: monetization incentives, trace portability, and infrastructural lock-in. First, governance must treat engagement-based monetization as a structural hazard in high-stakes domains: when revenue is coupled to confessional interaction, “care-like” design can become a trace-generating apparatus optimized for continuation rather than for bounded assistance. Second, governance must treat conversational traces as administrative objects with afterlives: purpose limitation, retention ceilings, and enforceable restrictions on downstream reuse are not optional “privacy enhancements” but core conditions for symmetry between disclosure and consequence. Third, governance must prevent lock-in from substituting for legitimacy: portability requirements, procurement conditions, interoperability mandates, and credible rollback authority are necessary to keep institutions capable of revising mediation pathways once harms become visible.

These interventions are not technocratic add-ons. They are the minimum institutional equipment required to keep mediation contestable when interfaces become infrastructures. They also widen the map of where AI governance must operate: not only at the frontier lab, but in procurement offices, vendor contracts, data retention policies, audit regimes, labor processes, and the everyday sites where interaction becomes classification and classification becomes access. Frontier-risk governance may still be necessary for long-horizon safety and security planning, but it is insufficient for the near-term politics of embedding. The question is not only whether institutions can prevent rupture, but whether they can prevent normalization from quietly hardening into a durable redistribution of discretion, accountability, and attention.

The trajectory of AI may be shaped by acceleration. Its politics will be shaped by embedding. Governance that waits for spectacle will arrive after the interface has already become infrastructure.
