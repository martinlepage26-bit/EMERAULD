An Imagined and Idealised Past as a Source for Revisionist Rhetoric:
The Dual Lives of the 1921 Murray Thesis
Rev Patricia ‘Iolana, PhD
Presented at the American Academy of Religion Nov 2016, San Antonio, Texas
Contemporary Pagan Studies Group
The 1921 Murray Thesis has two contrary lives: a discredited academic life and a life of
inspiration and guidance with some adherents of Wicca, Witchcraft, Goddess
Spirituality, and other Western Goddess-centred faith traditions. Using a prominent
Wiccan High Priestess’ (Phyllis Curott) 1998 memoir, Book of Shadows: A Modern
Woman’s Journey into the Wisdom of Witchcraft and the Magic of the Goddess as
source material for adherents, I shall examine the two lives of the Murray Thesis and
how Murray’s antimodernist rhetoric has both informed the ecological-mindedness of
modern Pagans and divided Witches amongst themselves.
Curott speaks at-length about what she calls the Old Religion in Book of Shadows. Well
educated, she defines her new-found faith tradition through independent research,
information passed down to her from the High Priestesses of the coven, and through
discussions with other Witches. Curott believes she is connecting to a historical lineage
of Witchcraft that predates the Abrahamic traditions; she writes: ‘I had come to
understand the Old Religion as a survival of the ancient Goddess religions.’ The
historical lineage of Wicca is imagined and idealised, and it rests firmly at the feet of
two Folklorists: Charles Leland and Margaret Murray.
The term Old Religion is attributed to American folklorist Charles Leland who published
a study of Italian Witchcraft in 1899, Aradia: Gospel of the Witches; Leland credits
adherents for the term. Old Religion, however, lives on, albeit problematically, in
contemporary writings and beliefs. Ronald Hutton, Michael York, and Jacqueline
Simpson openly refute the term with Simpson calling it ‘deplorable’ in its constant use.
While an American folklorist may have coined the term, it would be a British folklorist
who would define the Old Religion for generations who follow.

Margaret

Murray

was

a

prominent

and

well-respected

British

Egyptologist,

archaeologist, and folklorist; during her years at University College London she was also
the ‘erstwhile assistant and colleague to Sir Flinders Petrie […]’ the pre-eminent
archaeologist and Egyptologist. Murray worked in a variety of academic disciplines,
conducted fieldwork, and published often. Murray was also a staunch suffragette and
actively campaigned for women’s rights.
A specialist in Egyptology, the first World War effectively stopped all archaeological
work in Egypt, so Murray turned her attention to the European Witch trials. Hutton
writes: ‘Between 1917 and 1921 she had appeared to provide sound documentary
support for the theory that medieval and early modern witchcraft had been a pagan
religion.’ However, Murray did not stop there. Simpson writes: ‘Murray maintained that
witches were keeping alive an ancient religion concerned with fertility […].’ Murray,
Simpson writes: ‘created a startling new figure, the witch as a benevolent purveyor of
fertility […]’ Murray’s thesis, contained in her 1921 publication The Witch Cult in
Europe, initially found a welcome academic reception, until later scholars took a closer
look at Murray’s sources and conclusions. Simpson writes: ‘No British folklorist can
remember Dr Margaret Murray without embarrassment and a sense of paradox. She is
one of the few folklorists whose name became widely known to the public, but among
scholars her reputation is deservedly low […].’ Historian Ronald Hutton explains that
the difficulty with Murray’s thesis lie in both her source material and conclusions;
Hutton writes:
The Witch Cult in Western Europe rested upon a small amount of archival
research, with extensive use of printed trial records in nineteenth-century
editions, plus early modern pamphlets and works of demonology. […] Murray
combined it all to give an impression of a fairly uniform pagan religion,
surviving all across Western Europe until the seventeenth century. (1999:
195)
Simpson writes: ‘Her manipulation of sources is sometimes so blatant as to be naïve,
for even a cursory reader can spot what is going on.’ The difficulty appears to be in the
academe’s lack of willingness to publicly refute Murray’s ideas. Murray held a highly
respectable place in the academy working alongside Sir Flinders Petrie. Perhaps Petrie’s

Page 3 of 8

esteem as a pioneer in archaeological methodology and preservation techniques,
extended on to Murray. If so, this might have made Murray appear as almost infallible,
especially if Petrie supported her work. Why were her theories and methodologies not
criticised earlier? Was Petrie protecting his colleague? The lack of scholarly criticism
(with the scant exception of Halliday in 1922) of Murray’s theses from Murray’s peers
meant that her theories were endorsed by silent consent in Murray’s academic
community—allowing her unsubstantiated claims to acquire a following and be
perpetuated into modern times.
By 1929 Murray, still relatively unchallenged, was perceived as the leading academic in
the field of Witchcraft studies; this led to her invitation to write the 1929 entry on
Witchcraft for the prestigious Encyclopaedia Britannica. Simpson writes that Murray:
[…] seized the opportunity to set out her own interpretation of the topic as
if it were the universally accepted one. This entry was reprinted in later
editions up to 1969, making her views virtually infallible in the eyes of the
public […] by now they are so entrenched in popular culture that they will
probably never be uprooted.
Murray’s 1933 sequel was, according to Hutton, ‘[…] a restatement and reinforcement
of the thesis of the earlier book […].’ There were, however, two significant changes
made in this text: first, The God of the Witches was published by a popular press
(Sampson Low) obviously intended at a mass market rather than a scholarly one. Hutton
writes: ‘The approach was no longer analytical, but celebratory, the “Old Religion”
(Murray having taken up Leland’s phrase) being characterised as a joyous and lifeaffirming one, contrasted with the gloom of Christianity […]’ The second change was a
heavy emphasis on the deity at the centre of this Witchcraft phenomenon—Pan.
According to Hutton, Murray asserts: ‘[…] the horned god of the greenwood had been
the oldest male deity known to humans, and traced his worship across Europe and the
Near East, from the Old Stone Age to the seventeenth century.’ With these claims,
Murray was connecting Witchcraft as a distinguishable religion with a history that could
be traced from the Palaeolithic to her present day (1933). Murray was offering a
historical legacy for Witchcraft that predated even the Minoan and ancient Sumer

Page 4 of 8

cultures. However, the academe discredits Murray, and even some notable Pagan
scholars take issue with her work. Michael York writes:
[…] there is little or no evidence that modern earth spiritualties and related
traditions in the West are anything but contemporary creations. Instead, the
modern Neo-pagan movement deriving from Gardner, Murray, […] Leland,
[…] Graves, and […] Valiente can be seen as a multiple conflation of
numerous traditions or sources, many of which were patently contrived or
even erroneous.
York’s statement effectively crushes the historical legacy claimed by many Neo-Pagan
and Wiccan traditions. Major criticisms of Murray’s thesis began appearing two decades
after she first published her thesis in 1921. Scholars such as Runeberg, Parrinder, Rose,
Cohn, Simpson, Purkiss, Hutton, and York have been confronting Murray’s thesis and
research methodology. However, by then, the damage had been done; Hutton writes:
Many of the earlier textbooks and popular works on witchcraft remained in
schools and public libraries well into the 1980s, and people who had left
education before the mid-1970s were likely to preserve the impressions of
history which they had acquired before then. Even when new textbooks were
revised or old texts revised to take account of the new scholarly research,
their authors almost never attacked the obsolete ideas about witchcraft
head-on, but ignored them or deleted them silently.
The longevity of Murray’s thesis, and its inclusion in early feminist academic pursuits
could possibly explain why second-wave feminists (such as Mary Daly) and third-wave
feminists (such as myself) have such disparate views on the history of these faith
traditions.
There are several assertions made in Murray’s body of work on Witchcraft that continue
into modern times: (1) Witchcraft is a continuation of pre-Christian Pagan religion; (2)
Witchcraft’s history dates to the Palaeolithic era; (3) Witchcraft is a fertility religion,
and by extension, Witches are fertility figures; (4) the horned God is the main deity;
and (5) she established a Witches calendar including historic sabbats or holy days.
Murray’s impact in America was far-reaching; Hutton writes:

America’s most distinctive single contribution to that witchcraft, however,
arose from a different phenomenon: its assimilation to the women’s
spirituality movement. This was based upon the simple and fundamental fact
that the witch is one of the very few images of an independent female power
which historic European cultures have bequeathed.
Second-wave feminist Mary Daly played a significant role in perpetuating the feminist
as Witch, and Hutton comments on Daly’s contributions to scholarship which perpetuate
the Murray Thesis: ‘As the United States became the main source of modern feminist
thought in general and radical feminist thought in particular, the appropriation of this
image [Witch as independent powerful female] became virtually inevitable.’ But it was
not merely the academe that had been effected by Murray; Hutton writes: ‘These works
have a considerable impact on the general public.’ Murray’s thesis, however, would
have the most important impact on one individual—Gerald Gardner.
Murray was a prominent member of the Folklore Society in Britain; she would, later in
life, sit as the President of the Society—despite her academic downfall. Another
member of the Folklore Society was Gerald Gardner; Gardner adopted Murray’s thesis
wholeheartedly and created Wicca; York writes:
This tradition of magical practices, along with various English folk customs
and ideas from […] Murray and […] Leland, became synthesized in the 1940s
by Gerald Brosseau Gardner […], a retired English colonial civil servant, who
appears almost singlehandedly to have fashioned the contemporary Wiccan
movement.
Gardner’s most influential book was published in 1954; note the introduction written
by Murray. Gardner followed Witchcraft Today with Meaning of Witchcraft in 1959. His
legacy continues through his adherents.
I find it intriguing that Curott, considering her training in evidence gathering and debate
as a lawyer, would ignore the Murray Thesis and not engage with its criticisms in her
memoir Book of Shadows. Curott’s various resources, however, had all been tainted by
the Murray Thesis. It wasn’t until the 1980s that international academic scrutiny of the
Murray Thesis came into the scholarly dialogue. However, by then, it was too late. The

second wave of American feminists, and especially radical feminist theologians such as
Mary Daly, grasped on to the Murray Thesis and continue, to this day, to propagate
Murray’s ideas to the detriment of modern Witchcraft and Wicca.
I do however, have a simple theory as to why Curott does not engage with these
critiques—she is, as an adherent, unaware of them. Leading writers, including Starhawk
and Margot Adler, both write about Murray in their influential texts. In The Spiral
Dance, originally published in 1979 and later revised for its 20th anniversary, Starhawk
only makes brief mention of Murray citing her for popularising the Horned God in
Witchcraft. Adler, however, does address the controversy in her expanded 2006 edition
of her 1979 classic Drawing Down the Moon. In a chapter entitled ‘The Wiccan Revival’
Adler discusses, at length, what she deems ‘The Murrayite Controversy’. Writing
sympathetically, Adler seemingly dismisses Murray’s thesis while lauding her
importance as a writer; Adler writes: ‘The primary value of Murray’s work was her
understanding of the persistence of Pagan folk customs in Britain and her realisation
that Witchcraft could not be examined in isolation […]. But most scholars today dismiss
most of her work.’
Considering these sympathetic views of Murray’s thesis, it is no wonder that Curott was
unaware of the scholarly rejection of Murray’s methodologies and findings. As
evidenced by both Adler and Starhawk, authors Curott acknowledges reading, there is
a hesitancy to engage in criticism of Murray’s thesis. It could be argued that this critique
of the historical lineage of Wicca is being intentionally kept away from adherents. I can
attest, as a practicing Pagan for over twenty years, it was not until I started to examine
this issue from an academic perspective that I encountered both Murray and the
critiques of her thesis. Perhaps this is the same situation in which Curott found herself
in 1998.
Or perhaps Murray’s ideas still resonate today. In Book of Shadows, Curott writes:
The Old Religion is frequently referred to as spiritual environmentalism. […]
As an embodiment of the divine, the earth is not treated as a utilitarian

object to be exploited, polluted, and destroyed for man’s short-term greed.
Rather, it is inherently sacred in its value.
This foundational principle, taken directly from Murray, evokes an environmental
activism in most contemporary adherents. The first national survey of Neo-Pagans in
America exhibited that a dramatic percentage of American Witches and Neo-Pagans are
politically active in environmental concerns when compared against the general
American population. Berger, Leach, and Shaffer argue: ‘The large disparity between
the responses of Neo-Pagans and the general American public supports Harvey’s (1997)
claim that participation in Neo-Paganism strengthens individual’s commitment to
environmental issues.’ However, they also rightfully point out, however, that ‘Those
drawn to Neo-Paganism may come from part of the American public that is already
sensitive to environmental issues.’
Murray may not have accurately or critically charted the history of Witchcraft, but she
certainly shaped the future of it, and has become inextricably interconnected with
modern Paganism and Witchcraft.
However, not all modern Pagans agree on the validity of the Murray Thesis. Despite an
apparent desire to understand how one’s faith tradition was founded, there is a marked
dichotomy between those who accept her ideas on ‘blind faith’ and those with a healthy
critical scepticism. While Ethan Doyle White refers to the unwavering acceptance of
the Murray Thesis as a ‘legitimisation strategy’ in search of a ‘source of authenticity’
there are adherents who are unconvinced. In a July 2016 blog for Patheos, James Ford
posted ‘Margaret Murray, the Mother of Modern Wicca’. Ford does not engage with
Murray’s critiques, instead opting for Doyle White’s legitimisation strategy. But it is the
comments on the post that are the most enlightening—and indicates the rift. My
favourite is this one: ‘No. Just. No. she is *not* the ‘Mother of Modern Wicca […] I am
really tired of revisionists, erroneous “history” about Wicca being pushed like this once
again.

In June 2014, Wiccan Cassie Beyer writes: ‘What books that cite her as a credible source
fail to mention is her witchcraft theories were thoroughly discredited several decades
ago. […] No historian considers her witchcraft work valid.’ Ford and Beyer exemplify
the current division over the lingering influence of Murray’s debunked 1921 thesis and
demonstrate the diversity of beliefs amongst adherents—placing Murray inextricably
deep in a dogmatic divide between modern Wiccans, Witches, and Pagans. It also
reveals a rejection of Murray’s thesis by modern Pagans and raises a much-needed
critical voice from within the community.
