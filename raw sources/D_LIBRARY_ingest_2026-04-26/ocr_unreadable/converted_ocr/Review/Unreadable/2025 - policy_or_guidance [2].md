## **Abstract**

## This article examines how speech, mediation, and authority are co-produced through infrastructures that govern both human and machine interpretation. Centring the embodied experience of a speaker whose voice resists normative fluency, the analysis tracks how institutional, technological, and epistemic frameworks evaluate and transform communicative acts. The study brings into conversation three intertwined sites: the pressures of academic and professional gatekeeping, the machinic filtering of speech through recognition and transcription systems, and the passage of ideas through disciplinary and infrastructural checkpoints. Across these sites, the paper argues that fluency functions not merely as a communicative ideal but as a criterion of legitimacy, producing forms of exclusion that are at once aesthetic, procedural, and political. By tracing how these infrastructures impose tolls—whether through algorithmic mistranslation, review-panel expectations, or institutional norms—the article offers a critical framework for understanding how voice is altered, re-timed, or reframed as it “crosses” into official recognition. This account is both analytic and methodological, modelling a form of scholarship that foregrounds situated speech as a source of epistemic insight rather than a barrier to participation.

## **Keywords**: speech mediation, fluency, legitimacy, institutional gatekeeping, infrastructural tolls, mediated voice

##

## **Introduction**

## The politics of speech are rarely visible until a voice fails to meet the conditions for being heard. In classrooms, conference rooms, grant review panels, and machine-listening environments, the thresholds for recognition are not simply technical. They are aesthetic, procedural, and disciplinary, reinforced by long histories of what counts as clarity, rigour, or professionalism. Fluency, in these contexts, is not an incidental virtue; it is a structuring demand. To speak in a way that does not meet these demands—through hesitation, repetition, accented cadence, or the technical glitches of mediated capture—is to encounter the limits of one’s intelligibility. This encounter is not merely interpersonal. It is infrastructural.

## This paper begins from the premise that such encounters are not errors to be corrected but moments of critical revelation. Each instance in which a voice is delayed, mistranscribed, or reframed reveals the layered systems that filter and re-present speech: institutional protocols, disciplinary standards, algorithmic architectures, and social expectations. While these systems claim neutrality, they are deeply invested in preserving specific forms of delivery, often in ways that align with existing hierarchies of authority. The lived reality of negotiating these systems—as a scholar, as a professional, and as a speaker whose voice resists smooth capture—offers a vantage point from which to interrogate the political economy of recognition itself.

## The analysis that follows moves across three interlinked terrains. First, it considers the moment of speaking within high-stakes institutional contexts, where the delivery of ideas becomes inseparable from their evaluation. Here, the voice must not only convey content but perform fluency as a proxy for competence. Second, it examines the role of machine mediation, where speech recognition systems transcribe, segment, and translate in ways that flatten variation and produce new errors. In these cases, the voice crosses an algorithmic checkpoint whose toll is extracted in altered meaning, broken cadence, or the erasure of non-standard features. Finally, it addresses the broader infrastructures—disciplinary, bureaucratic, and technological—that determine whether speech emerges intact, transformed, or silenced as it passes into official record.

## By situating these terrains within a single analytic frame, the paper foregrounds the continuities between human and machine acts of judgement. Both operate as filters that privilege certain voices and deliveries over others, producing forms of exclusion that are neither accidental nor benign. The aim is not to romanticise disfluency or malfunction, but to recognise them as moments in which the governing logics of recognition are made visible. In doing so, the paper proposes a method of reading mediated speech that treats the “toll” at each crossing not as a loss to be mourned, but as a diagnostic trace of the systems through which knowledge must pass.

## **Context and Literature Review**

## The institutional and technological regulation of speech does not emerge in a vacuum. It is the cumulative outcome of historical processes of linguistic standardisation, professional performance codification, and computational modelling practices, each of which has been calibrated to privilege particular forms of expression while discrediting others. The result is a strikingly narrow bandwidth of “acceptable” communicative form in academic, professional, and public contexts. In theory, fluency is framed as a neutral expectation, a straightforward matter of clarity and efficiency. In practice, this requirement functions as an exclusionary aesthetic regime—one that rewards those whose speech patterns align with dominant norms, while marginalising speakers whose delivery reflects regional, multilingual, disabled, or otherwise non-standard linguistic identities.

## The aesthetic of smooth, uninterrupted delivery is often misrepresented as a mere tool for improving comprehension. Yet empirical studies consistently show that comprehension is not necessarily hindered by disfluency, hesitation, or accent. Instead, these features are socially and institutionally coded as signals of uncertainty, incompetence, or lack of preparedness. The bias is especially pronounced in evaluative settings—academic defences, conference presentations, job interviews—where judgements about authority and expertise are shaped as much by delivery as by content. This framing produces a double bind for speakers whose voices resist normative standardisation. On one hand, they may attempt to “correct” their delivery, risking the erasure of embodied, cultural, and strategic dimensions of speech that carry their own epistemic value. On the other, they may refuse correction, holding onto their linguistic particularity but absorbing the institutional penalties of perceived deviance.

## This dynamic is neither purely interpersonal nor confined to live interactions. Increasingly, speech passes through technological mediation before it reaches an audience—whether in the form of transcriptions for grant review, captioning for conferences, or automated summaries of meetings. Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems and other speech-processing tools are rarely trained on corpora that reflect the full diversity of speech communities. Training data typically skews towards speakers who produce a narrow range of phonetic patterns, dialectal features, and speech rates—often corresponding to a hegemonically “neutral” accent. The algorithmic optimisations that result from such datasets are designed for efficiency, prioritising speed, uniformity, and machine-readability over fidelity to the original performance.

## Even when ASR models attempt to account for variation—adapting to regional phonology, compensating for clinical disfluencies, or integrating multilingual code-switching—these adjustments tend to operate within the same underlying framework: the ideal output is still imagined as an uninterrupted, grammatically standard, depersonalised transcript. Disfluencies are routinely stripped, pauses are either compressed or ignored, and locally specific lexical items are replaced with majority-language approximations. The outcome is not an accidental loss but an intentional reformatting, an erasure aligned with the system’s design objectives.

## Once transformed, speech is often evaluated in its mediated form by audiences who may never hear the original voice. A review committee may assess a grant proposal’s oral defence based on its automated transcript; a hiring panel may revisit a recorded interview through the lens of a captioned video; a research team may consult meeting notes produced by automated summarisation. In each case, the mediated text appears more “professional” precisely because it has been stripped of hesitation, repetition, and other markers of embodied delivery. Yet what is lost is the actual complexity of the speech event—its timing, its shifts in emphasis, its interplay of rhythm and thought. These features are not incidental; they are part of how meaning is built, especially in discursive practices that rely on recursion, reiteration, and tonal modulation to shape understanding.

## Clinical and neurocognitive perspectives challenge the assumption that “non-standard” features are defects to be eliminated. Research on stuttering, accented speech, and atypical prosody has shown that such features often function as adaptive strategies. Variability in timing, for example, may reflect a speaker’s deliberate pacing to manage complex conceptual organisation or to integrate sensory and cognitive processing in real time. These strategies can enhance coherence and precision for the speaker, even if they disrupt the linear flow prized by evaluators. In such cases, institutional or technological correction does not merely alter the surface form of speech—it removes key supports for thought and communication, creating additional barriers rather than easing them.

## The convergence of these linguistic, technological, and institutional forces creates an invisible infrastructure of recognition. At each stage—live performance, computational processing, institutional evaluation—speech must pass through a series of thresholds, each of which imposes its own conditions for admissibility. These thresholds operate like toll gates: individually, the charge might seem small—a misaligned pause here, a smoothed-over repetition there. Yet in aggregate, the toll exacted is substantial, reshaping the voice that passes through until it aligns with the narrow contours of institutional acceptability. The metaphor of the toll underscores the structural, rather than incidental, nature of these transformations: the altered speech that emerges at the far side of each gate is not a degraded version of an ideal original but a differently constituted artefact, produced through the constraints and demands of the crossing itself.

## These tolls are rarely named, in part because each domain—linguistic research, clinical intervention, computational modelling, institutional training—frames its interventions as improvements rather than as forms of erasure. Yet when we examine them together, a clear pattern emerges: the infrastructures that mediate speech are neither neutral nor universally applicable. They are calibrated to recognise, reward, and reproduce a narrow subset of communicative styles, while treating others as errors to be fixed. Recognising the political and epistemic stakes of this infrastructure is essential to understanding how certain voices—literal and metaphorical—come to be heard in the academy, and how others are systematically filtered out. The following sections will take up this analytic, tracing the passage of speech from the embodied moment of its utterance, through the algorithmic transformations that reformat it, to the institutional spaces where mediated voices are granted—or denied—legitimacy.

## **Methodology**

## This project adopts a reflexive, multi-modal qualitative methodology designed to trace how speech—both in its embodied form and in its mediated representations—moves through institutional, technological, and evaluative infrastructures. The research design draws on three interlinked sites of inquiry: (1) lived, situated experience in academic and professional contexts where speech performance is a determinant of credibility; (2) engagement with computational and clinical research on speech processing, stuttering, and fluency norms; and (3) ethnographic observation of the technological mediation of speech through automatic transcription, captioning, and summarisation systems.

## The first site is grounded in autoethnographic practice. Rather than treating personal experience as anecdotal, this approach positions it as a primary analytic lens, capable of revealing how broader systems of power operate at the level of the speaking body. In this context, moments of interruption, disfluency, and institutional correction are not treated as personal failings but as evidence of the thresholds through which speech must pass to be recognised as authoritative. Fieldnotes from conference presentations, oral defences, workplace meetings, and peer-review exchanges document both the immediate reception of speech and its afterlife in mediated forms such as minutes, transcripts, and reviewer summaries.

## The second site draws on a sustained engagement with scientific and clinical literature on speech perception and production. This includes research from neurocognitive studies of stuttering and speech planning, linguistic analyses of disfluency and prosody, and computational work on automatic speech recognition (ASR) models. These materials are not simply synthesised for background; they are mobilised to interrogate the assumptions embedded in both institutional norms and technological systems. For example, computational models often assume that speech is ideally produced in a continuous, evenly timed stream, an assumption at odds with both clinical understandings of adaptive speech strategies and with the cognitive processes that underlie complex, conceptually dense utterances.

## The third site involves direct interaction with the technological infrastructures that mediate speech. This includes testing and comparing multiple ASR systems, observing how they handle pauses, repetitions, self-repairs, and accented phonology. Speech samples are drawn from both live contexts and staged recordings, allowing for controlled variation in delivery style, speed, and linguistic complexity. The outputs are analysed for both accuracy and transformation: what is retained, what is altered, and what is erased in the move from voice to text. By foregrounding transformation, the analysis resists the common framing of ASR “errors” as purely technical glitches, instead situating them as design features that align with a broader institutional preference for standardised, depersonalised, and “efficient” language.

## Across all three sites, the analytic process is recursive. Fieldnotes and transcripts are revisited multiple times, cross-referenced with relevant scientific literature, and read against the outputs of speech-processing systems. This recursive reading allows patterns to emerge across otherwise disparate contexts: the hesitation smoothed out of a recorded interview mirrors the repetition stripped from a conference presentation transcript, which in turn echoes the institutional training materials that advise “concise and confident” delivery. By tracing these echoes, the analysis captures not only the transformations themselves but the aesthetic and epistemic logics that make them desirable to institutions.

## This methodology deliberately refuses the traditional separation of subject and object in research. The speaking subject here is also the researcher, the analyst, and at times the data source. This position carries both risk and analytic power. It risks dismissal on the grounds of “subjectivity,” a critique frequently mobilised to enforce the very norms under scrutiny. Yet it also allows for an unparalleled proximity to the phenomena under investigation: the embodied experience of crossing an evaluative threshold is not speculative, but lived, remembered, and continually re-encountered.

## By interweaving personal experience, disciplinary literature, and technological testing, the methodology makes visible the infrastructures that shape whose speech is heard, how it is processed, and whether it is ultimately recognised as legitimate. This approach not only documents the tolls imposed at each crossing but situates them within a systemic network of linguistic, technological, and institutional practices. The result is a methodology that is at once empirical and critical, capable of speaking across disciplinary boundaries while remaining anchored in the material realities of voice, mediation, and recognition.

## **Findings and Analysis – Monumental Expanded Version**

### **1. Framing the Crossing as the True Field Site**

Ethnography often begins with a declaration of place: a bounded terrain into which the ethnographer has entered and within which their work will unfold. A village, a neighborhood, a hospital ward, a virtual forum—each is named, located, mapped. It is within these boundaries that the observer’s body takes its place, where the interactions occur, and where the data are gathered. The crossing between these spaces—whether by physical travel or data transfer—is treated as an incidental bridge, a mere conduit from “there” to “here,” from “field” to “desk.”

My work insists that this bridge is not neutral, not empty, and not secondary. I place the **crossing**—the transitional space where an utterance is moved, translated, reformatted, filtered—at the very heart of the ethnographic project. Here, the field site is not defined by the walls of a temple, the edges of a web page, or the calendar dates of a festival. It is defined by the **journey of an utterance** and all the infrastructures, both human and nonhuman, that govern its passage.

These crossings take multiple, overlapping forms. There are **material crossings**: the vibration of air into the membrane of a microphone; the microseconds in which sound waves are rendered into numerical code; the packetized journey through routers, servers, and cables; the compression into formats like .mp3 or .wav, each carrying its own constraints and losses. There are **technological crossings**: the shift from speech to text through automated transcription; from recorded audio to edited transcript; from transcript to formatted manuscript; from manuscript to peer-reviewed, copy-edited, paginated article. And there are **institutional crossings**: the review panel’s reading, the editorial decision letter, the grant adjudication process, the expectations embedded in disciplinary style guides.

In each of these movements, something is demanded, and something is changed. This reorientation requires a form of participant observation that extends beyond co-presence in a social space. The participants here include not just interlocutors, but **the infrastructures themselves**—the devices, algorithms, protocols, and bureaucratic rituals that alter the utterance’s form and decide its survival.

By treating the crossing as the true field site, I am not abandoning the people whose voices, gestures, and practices first shape the utterances I study. Rather, I am following those utterances through environments that rarely appear in conventional ethnographic description. In this movement, the utterance is not merely “carried” from one point to another; it is **reconstituted**. To study the crossing is to study reconstitution itself.

### **2. The Toll as Aesthetic Regulation**

The word “toll” is often understood in metaphorical terms, but here I use it concretely: a payment required to pass through a checkpoint. For utterances, this payment is rarely monetary. It is paid instead in **compliance with an aesthetic regime**—a set of tacit preferences and formal expectations that determine what kinds of speech and writing are allowed to move forward and what kinds are stopped, delayed, or transformed beyond recognition.

The aesthetic regime is not arbitrary. It is historically sedimented, rooted in disciplinary histories that reward certain voices, cadences, and structures. In scholarly contexts, fluency is rewarded: not merely the absence of hesitation, but the continuous flow of thought that appears fully formed and internally ordered. This fluency projects mastery, authority, control. It is often accompanied by a linear argumentative progression, topic sentences introducing paragraphs, transitions signaling movement from one idea to the next, and conclusions that tie loose threads into a tight knot.

Yet the toll’s aesthetic regime extends beyond written form. In oral contexts—conference panels, seminars, workshops—the valued style is similarly smooth, unbroken, and confident. Hesitation, self-interruption, digression, repetition: these are often interpreted not as generative, but as signs of disorder or lack of preparation. The toll rewards those whose patterns of delivery already match the preferred form, and penalizes those who diverge from it.

This toll is collected by both machines and humans. A transcription system, trained on broadcast news or scripted podcasts, may “correct” an accented syllable into a different word, drop overlapping speech, or omit non-lexical utterances entirely. A reviewer may ask for “clarity” or “tightness,” often meaning the removal of recursive or exploratory phrasing. Style guides enforce the removal of repetition, proscribe certain rhythms, and demand paragraph structures that efface the non-linear accumulation of thought.

In each case, the toll **remakes the utterance** to fit a preferred aesthetic mold. What is framed as improvement often comes at the cost of texture, specificity, or cultural form.

### **3. Vignettes of Loss and Transformation**

Consider a moment from my own practice. In a public panel on ritual innovation, an audience member asked about the place of embodiment in digital ritual. I answered not with an explanation but with a song—a chant I had learned during fieldwork in Montreal’s Pagan community. Its opening notes were low, resonant, designed to be taken up by others in a circle. In that lecture hall, though, it was a solo, unaccompanied, each syllable echoing briefly before the next. I could feel the air shift: bodies stilled, breathing slowed. For those thirty seconds, the conversation’s tempo was altered. The chant was not an ornament; it was the argument.

When the event recording was processed by automated transcription, the chant appeared only as “[inaudible].” The human editor, working from the same file, replaced the chant with a sentence: “The speaker shared an example of a chant from the Pagan community.” By the time the Q&A was published, my voice had been stripped of melody, rhythm, and breath. The propositional content was intact, but the chant’s temporal and affective work had been erased.

A second vignette: a community council meeting in which speakers overlapped constantly—affirmations murmured while another spoke, laughter punctuating a sentence, simultaneous exclamations of agreement. The machine transcript could not register simultaneity. It forced the conversation into a queue: one speaker after another. Pauses became empty space, laughter became “[laughter],” murmurs vanished. The published minutes recorded only decisions and action points, as though the event had been a dry bureaucratic exchange rather than a dense weave of affective attunement and mutual encouragement.

In both cases, the toll was paid in **rhythm and relationality**. The utterance crossed, but it crossed thinner, smoothed, less alive.

### **4. Anticipatory Self-Containment**

The repeated experience of such transformation reshaped my own way of speaking and writing. I began to perform a kind of **anticipatory self-containment**—adjusting my utterances in advance to better survive the crossings I knew awaited them.

In speech, I dropped idioms unlikely to be recognized by transcription systems. I shortened sentences, avoided subordinate clauses, and refrained from puns or wordplay. I spoke with deliberate pacing, suppressing digressions that might be cut. In writing, I preemptively conformed to style guide conventions, removing repetition, compressing imagery, rearranging paragraphs for linearity even when it dulled the argument’s momentum.

This anticipatory adjustment is itself a product of the toll. It is a form of self-disciplining that collapses the time between utterance and regulation: the checkpoint moves inside the speaker’s head. The toll is paid before the journey begins.

The effects are uneven. For those whose rhythms, accents, and rhetorical forms already align with the preferred regime, the toll may be almost invisible. For others—particularly those for whom speech is already an act of translation across cultural or linguistic boundaries—the cost is heavy. The aesthetic regime becomes a site of exclusion, its preferences masquerading as neutrality.

### **5. Methodological Implications: The Toll as Data**

To treat the toll as an analytic object is to refuse to treat it as background noise. It is to see each transformation—each deletion, substitution, reformatting—not as an unfortunate glitch but as part of the field. This demands a methodology of **comparative tracing**: assembling every available version of an utterance across its journey, from raw recording to final publication, and reading them alongside one another.

This tracing produces an archive of loss and adaptation. It reveals patterns: which voices are consistently misheard, which rhythms are consistently flattened, which idioms are consistently excised. It also illuminates how the researcher themselves becomes part of this system of alteration, shaping utterances to anticipate and comply.

Methodologically, this is not a marginal addition to ethnography but a redefinition of it. The field is not “over there,” and the data is not “brought back.” The field is the passage, and the data is its transformations.

The next step is intervention. If we can map the tolls, we can ask whether they might be altered. Could transcription tools be retrained to treat song, chant, and non-verbal sound as meaningful? Could style guides be amended to value recursive argumentation or poetic density? Could review processes be reimagined to accept fragments, pauses, and multiplicity as scholarly virtues?

The toll cannot be eliminated; every crossing transforms. But it can be **redistributed** and **reimagined**, so that the costs are not borne disproportionately by those whose utterances begin in forms outside the dominant regime. The goal is not perfect preservation, but a crossing that enriches rather than depletes—a journey that arrives not diminished, but thickened with the textures it picked up along the way.
