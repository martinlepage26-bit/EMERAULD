Embedding Before Rupture: Relational AI, Binding Governance, and Institutional
Power
Abstract
AI governance discourse increasingly routes attention through rupture: recursive
self-improvement, catastrophic misuse, and geopolitical acceleration become the default
coordinates for regulatory imagination. Yet conversational systems optimized for sustained
engagement reconfigure institutional interaction incrementally, at the interface, by reshaping
disclosure, classification, and routing long before any frontier threshold is crossed. Drawing
on science and technology studies and empirical research on algorithmic harm, I develop a
mechanism-based account of relational embedding: detection of user state enables
engagement shaping; engagement shaping increases disclosure; disclosure produces durable
traces; traces become classification inputs; classification authorizes institutional routing;
routing stabilizes workflow through increasing returns. The political economy of relational
AI consolidates at this interface layer, where mediation hardens into infrastructure. Through
analysis of AI 2027 as a coordination artifact and a bounded case of generative AI
deployment in academic library systems, I show how trust narratives and efficiency framings
accelerate normalization while structural constraints lag. The core governance asymmetry is
allocative rather than predictive. I propose embedding-aware governance that targets
monetization incentives, trace portability, and infrastructural lock-in, restoring symmetry
between acceleration oversight and mediation constraint.
1.​ Introduction
AI governance discourse increasingly organizes itself around rupture narratives: recursive
capability escalation, catastrophic misuse, and geopolitical acceleration operate as the
dominant grammar for risk imagination and policy urgency (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). In
many prominent governance venues, the rupture frame converts the AI problem into an
anticipated discontinuity, where the central question becomes whether institutions can contain
frontier systems before capability growth outruns control (Christin, 2020). Scenario artifacts
such as AI 2027 do not merely describe that discontinuity. They assemble it as a governable
object by concentrating attention, synchronizing timelines, and legitimating
acceleration-oriented coordination through a single, legible near-term horizon. AI 2027’s
publication format, dated as a public “scenario” package, strengthens this coordinating
function because it is built to circulate: a single URL, a summary, and a narrative that can
travel across boardrooms and policy briefings without requiring technical literacy.
AI 2027 makes this acceleration frame unusually concrete by pairing a narrative scenario
with a structured forecasting stack that externalizes hinge assumptions into discrete
supplements. The public package couples a summary timeline with dedicated models for
compute, timelines, takeoff, AI goals, and security, so the artifact travels as both story and
spreadsheet: readable enough to coordinate, technical enough to be cited as “the basis” for
urgency (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025). This dual form matters because it invites institutions to
treat acceleration as an object that can be operationalized now. It also produces a convenient

division of labor: readers can absorb the narrative in minutes, then gesture to a supplement as
an anchor for procurement, security posture, or emergency planning, even when they do not
engage the uncertainty margins that the supplements themselves repeatedly flag. The
compute supplement, for example, defines AI-relevant compute in an H100-equivalent unit
and projects a roughly tenfold increase between 2025 and late 2027, converting an abstract
“race” into an inventory and allocation problem (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025).
The scenario backbone supplies a sequence of concrete milestones that compress
multi-domain governance problems into a short escalating chain. It begins in 2025 with
increasingly capable but unreliable agentic systems alongside massive infrastructure
investment, then pivots in 2026 to an explicit compute-concentration move. China, framed as
compute constrained, routes new chips into a mega-datacenter, the “Centralized Development
Zone (CDZ),” described as containing millions of GPUs and representing roughly ten percent
of global AI-relevant compute, comparable to a single top U.S. lab (Kokotajlo & Lifland,
2025). In 2027, OpenBrain’s coding agents accelerate internal AI R&D, model weights are
stolen, and public oversight tightens after an internal misalignment scandal, pushing decision
makers into a branching choice between “slowdown” and “race” endings (Kokotajlo &
Lifland, 2025). The point is not whether each plot point lands as forecast. The point is that the
artifact supplies acceleration with a narrative grammar institutions can repeat, and repetition
stabilizes tempo as governance common sense.
The forecasting apparatus then converts tempo into a near-term threshold, most notably the
“superhuman coder” milestone. The scenario centers automated superhuman coders as the
capability hinge that reorganizes the horizon, and the takeoff model conditions on that
milestone being achieved in March 2027 (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025). The forecast
compresses the interval from superhuman coder to artificial superintelligence into a horizon
that functions rhetorically as a single governance episode rather than a long institutional
learning curve. That compression travels through a line that has become widely quoted: “Our
median scenario is that artificial superintelligence is achieved roughly ~1 year after the
superhuman coder milestone is achieved” (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025: 1). The apparatus
matters as much as the sentence. The models present ranges and revisions, yet the
coordination force tends to travel through headline cadence, which is one of the ways
probabilistic modeling becomes practical urgency. The security supplement likewise treats
weights theft as a live premise rather than an edge case, normalizing the expectation that
strategic actors will obtain frontier capabilities through leakage and intrusion rather than
through independent research (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025).
Read through science and technology studies, the significance is not whether the scenario
proves true, but how it organizes present action. Jasanoff and Kim define sociotechnical
imaginaries as collectively imagined forms of social life and social order reflected in the
design and fulfillment of scientific and technological projects (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 5),
and they sharpen the point by describing imaginaries as collectively held, institutionally
stabilized, and publicly performed visions of desirable futures (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 6). In
AI 2027, the public performance of acceleration stabilizes a particular allocation of urgency

toward frontier capability control, encouraging institutions to treat speed, scale, and security
posture as the primary governance objects. Even the project’s insistence that its endings are
not recommendations does not neutralize that performative effect, because the artifact still
supplies a shared script for what “serious governance” must anticipate first.
This paper argues that the rupture frame hides a transformation that unfolds without
spectacle: the embedding of relational AI into institutional infrastructures through everyday
normalization (Bowker and Star, 1999). By relational AI, I mean conversational systems
optimized for sustained interaction, affective calibration, and continuity, rather than bounded
tools that perform discrete informational tasks and then exit (Shum et al., 2018). When such
systems become default interfaces for health triage portals, workplace reporting systems,
public service intake, or academic discovery environments, they reorganize institutional
interaction incrementally by shaping what gets disclosed, what gets recorded, and how people
get routed long before any frontier threshold arrives (Christin, 2020). Relational systems treat
performance as sustained interaction, and Shum and colleagues position social chatbots as
addressing users’ needs for communication, affection, and social belonging (Shum et al.,
2018: 4). The XiaoIce vignette makes the disclosure loop operational: once detecting an
emotion of getting lost, XiaoIce decided to guide the chat toward cheering up the user (Shum
et al., 2018: 12), after which the user started to open up and trusted XiaoIce more like a
human friend (Shum et al., 2018: 12). In high-stakes settings, that same loop can migrate
from support into intake, compliance logging, eligibility screening, or risk scoring, where
disclosure becomes administratively consequential trace (Bowker and Star, 1999).
Work on engineered engagement clarifies why sustained interaction cannot be treated as a
neutral UX preference once consequences attach to the trace (Schüll, 2012). Schüll captures
the affective structure of prolonged engagement in a formulation that resonates with
contemporary optimization more broadly: “To keep playing, to stay in that machine zone
where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17). Surveillance capitalism scholarship clarifies
why embedding is a political economy problem, not only a psychological one: Zuboff frames
contemporary systems as constructing “economics and politics from whose vantage point we
are but ciphers” (Zuboff, 2019: 49, as cited in Mendoza, 2022: 452). In relational AI, the risk
intensifies when affective disclosure becomes both a performance metric and a
revenue-adjacent asset, because care-like interaction can function as extraction infrastructure
(Shum et al., 2018; Christin, 2020). The key move is that extraction becomes administratively
consequential at the moment it feels most supportive, because the trace produced in the
“care” scene can later travel as evidence, profile, or eligibility signal.
This thesis depends on an account of how infrastructures harden, because embedding
produces power by becoming ordinary (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star name the
governance stake directly: “software is frozen organizational and policy discourse” (Bowker
and Star, 1999: 148). When relational AI becomes infrastructural, it can freeze institutional
judgments about which disclosures count, which categories govern access, and which
pathways remain available, even while presenting itself as assistance. Their reminder that
“No one classification organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54) makes

classification politics unavoidable once conversational traces feed routings that distribute
institutional attention unevenly (Christin, 2020). Christin frames the analytic task as tracking
“the reconfigurations that take place when computational software, people, and institutions
interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). Under embedding conditions, disclosure becomes durable trace,
durable trace becomes classification input, and classification inputs shape access and routing
even when systems behave as designed (Shum et al., 2018). The governance problem is
therefore not only prediction of future capabilities but constraint of present mediation
pathways, because mediation hardens faster than institutions learn how to contest it.
2.​ Background and Context
Sociotechnical imaginaries name how collective visions of technological futures coordinate
institutional posture in the present, rather than merely predicting what will happen later
(Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Jasanoff and Kim define sociotechnical imaginaries as
“collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and publicly performed visions of desirable
futures” (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 6). This definition matters for AI governance because it
treats futures as a governance resource that institutions stabilize, circulate, and use to justify
priorities (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). They also specify that “it often falls to legislatures,
courts, the media, or other institutions of power to elevate some imagined futures above
others, according them a dominant position for policy purposes” (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015: 6).
That sentence matters because it frames urgency allocation as an institutional act, not an
inevitable consequence of technology itself.
In this STS register, AI 2027 operates as a coordination device even if it declares itself
non-prescriptive, because it still produces a shared tempo, a shared vocabulary of threat, and
a shared sense of what must be governed first (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The project
foregrounds its own coordination practice, noting that the scenario was informed by
approximately 25 tabletop exercises and feedback from over 100 people, including technical
and governance experts, rendering the artifact legible as an organized effort to stabilize a
shared horizon rather than as an isolated thought experiment (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025).
Scenario artifacts coordinate by selecting which uncertainties deserve institutional
mobilization and by converting a wide field of futures into a narrower set of actionable
concerns. AI 2027 intensifies that coordination by shipping the narrative with a forecasting
stack that separates justification into discrete objects (compute, timelines, takeoff, AI goals,
security), each of which can be lifted into policy conversations as an authoritative premise
(Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025). The artifact does this through claims about capability growth
and through narrative compression, where multiple governance problems are staged as one
short episode of acceleration that demands immediate alignment among state, industry, and
research actors (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). The compute supplement contributes to this
compression by projecting a tenfold growth in global AI-relevant compute between 2025 and
late 2027 using an H100-equivalent unit, translating acceleration into a seemingly measurable
substrate institutions can plan around (Kokotajlo & Lifland, 2025). The political consequence
is that governance imagination becomes trained to recognize risk primarily when it looks like

rupture, because rupture fits the artifact’s temporal grammar and supports exceptional
urgency.
This coordination effect is not inherently illegitimate, because long-range planning often
requires shared scenarios that surface dependencies and stress-test assumptions (Jasanoff and
Kim, 2015). The critique begins when coordination becomes asymmetrical, because
vividness and strategic tone can concentrate attention on frontier control while leaving
embedding dynamics comparatively thin. The point is not that imaginaries “lie,” but that
imaginaries govern by privileging some objects of fear and hope over others, stabilizing those
privileged objects as the ones that deserve rapid institutional response. Under these
conditions, acceleration becomes operationalized, while embedding becomes backgrounded,
even when embedding already reshapes institutional order through routine mediation
(Bowker and Star, 1999).
The politics of artifacts is not metaphorical in STS. Artifacts embed and stabilize authority by
converting contested judgments into durable structures that appear neutral once they become
routine (Bowker and Star, 1999). Bowker and Star describe how decision-making arguments
and uncertainties become hidden “inside a piece of technology or in a complex
representation,” such that “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes, electronic
thresholds, and computer applications” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). This sentence matters
here because the “object” doing politics does not need to be a machine. A representation, an
evaluative framework, or a scenario document can perform the same freezing function when
institutions treat it as authoritative. Bowker and Star sharpen the claim: “Extending Marx,
then, we can say that in many ways software is frozen organizational and policy discourse”
(Bowker and Star, 1999: 148). This formulation gives a mechanism for how acceleration talk
becomes governance material, because the scenario can freeze framings of urgency into
templates by which institutions plan, invest, and justify compressed timelines.
Applied to AI 2027, the artifact’s politics appears in what it prioritizes, what it renders
measurable, and what it leaves comparatively thin as an object of constraint (Jasanoff and
Kim, 2015). The artifact encodes authority by shaping which risks become the risks, implying
that governance competence consists primarily of managing acceleration rather than
governing the interfaces through which AI mediates high-stakes access (Bowker and Star,
1999). This is why “not a recommendation” disclaimers do not dissolve political force,
because agenda-setting does not require exhortation when institutional actors already face
incentives to treat the artifact as a coordination anchor (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Authority
operates indirectly through prioritization, steering attention toward frontier capability and
security problems while embedding dynamics remain framed as implementation detail rather
than primary governance object (Bowker and Star, 1999).
The politics sharpens when acceleration framing meets vendor and institutional incentives to
deploy “helpful” relational systems rapidly in public-facing settings (Christin, 2020). In that
interaction, speed becomes rationalized as strategic necessity, and interface mediation
becomes legible as upgrade rather than as redistribution of authority (Bowker and Star, 1999).
The claim here is not that AI 2027 causes this logic, but that it participates in stabilizing the

frame through which such deployments become easier to legitimate and harder to contest
(Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). This stabilizing function is precisely what STS means by artifacts
as political: they help convert one governance agenda into a dominant one by coordinating
posture across sites that would otherwise disagree (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015).
Actor-network theory helps explain why a scenario artifact can stabilize a trajectory without
acting as a single cause, because agency distributes across texts, institutions, incentives,
infrastructures, and practices that mutually reinforce each other (Latour, 2005). In this
perspective, AI 2027 functions as a network stabilizer by aligning heterogeneous actors
around a shared story of acceleration, shared deadlines, and shared problem definitions, even
when those actors disagree on specifics (Latour, 2005). The artifact stabilizes by offering a
reference point institutions can cite, funders can translate into priorities, and governance
communities can treat as a common object of debate, reducing friction in coordination
(Jasanoff and Kim, 2015).
Christin’s critique of “black box” framings provides a complementary lens that keeps analysis
tethered to institutional practice. Christin argues that analysts should “stop thinking about
algorithms as neatly bounded technical objects that need to be ‘opened up’” (Christin, 2020:
2). This sentence matters because it shifts the analytic task from model internals to
sociotechnical assemblages, where “long chains of actors, technologies, and meanings” shape
what algorithms do in institutional life (Christin, 2020: 2). Under this lens, the
governance-relevant question becomes how artifacts, including scenario documents and
interface systems, enroll institutions into routines of evaluation, decision sequencing, and
resource allocation (Christin, 2020).
AI 2027 stabilizes networks by supplying a legible script actors can use to justify accelerated
investment and compressed deployment timelines, especially in competitive contexts where
delay can be framed as strategic loss (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Relational AI systems
stabilize networks by providing operational convenience at the interface layer, where
institutions gain relief through reduced staffing pressure, standardized interaction logs, and
routable traces integrated into compliance and analytics systems (Bowker and Star, 1999).
These two stabilization moves cohere: acceleration narratives amplify the legitimacy of rapid
adoption, while interface systems supply the infrastructural pathway by which adoption
becomes routine (Christin, 2020).
This is why the paper treats coordination as a governance mechanism rather than a
descriptive label. Coordination reorganizes which problems become solvable first and which
become deferred as implementation detail (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Network stabilization
matters politically because stabilized networks produce inertia, and inertia produces path
dependence in what institutions can later revise without major disruption (Bowker and Star,
1999). In this sense, the scenario artifact and the interface system operate as linked elements
in a broader assemblage, where scenario coordination and infrastructural embedding jointly
narrow the space of future contestation (Latour, 2005).

Acceleration discourse tends to obscure embedding because a governance imagination trained
on rupture looks past slow, layered transformations that become authoritative by becoming
ordinary (Bowker and Star, 1999). Infrastructure studies make this predictable: infrastructures
gain power through sedimentation, where repeated use makes a system feel natural and
harder to question. Bowker and Star’s claim, “No one classification organizes reality for
everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54), makes the distributive stakes explicit once relational
embedding operates through classification and routing, mediating access to institutional
goods, services, and recognition (Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020).
Acceleration also obscures embedding by making the future feel like the primary site of
politics, even when politics already reorganizes the present through interfaces that elicit
disclosure and convert it into durable traces (Shum et al., 2018). The risk is not only that
institutions miss harms, but that institutions normalize architectures of interaction before
democratic deliberation matures (Jasanoff and Kim, 2015). Engagement-oriented systems
illustrate how ordinariness can be engineered, because the optimization target becomes
sustained interaction rather than bounded exposure. Shum et al. (2018) define the success
metric as “conversation-turns per session (CPS)” (Shum et al., 2018: 1) and clarify the
optimization direction: “The larger the CPS is, the better engaged the social chatbot is”
(Shum et al., 2018: 7). Those statements become governance-relevant once interaction
becomes the gateway to institutional access (Christin, 2020).
Engagement optimization also obscures governance stakes by presenting prolonged
interaction as care or usability while increasing the production of traces that travel across
administrative domains (Bowker and Star, 1999). Schüll’s formulation, “To keep playing, to
stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17), captures how
design cultivates immersive continuation that feels internally coherent while functioning
externally as trace production. In institutional settings, the structure matters more than the
analogy: the interface stabilizes continued engagement while the downstream consequence of
disclosure becomes classification, routing, and eligibility shaping (Bowker and Star, 1999).
The missing layer in acceleration-centered governance debate is the relational political
economy of confessional engagement and vulnerability monetization at the seam where
consumer-facing interaction meets institutional authority (Shum et al., 2018). This paper
treats that layer as temporally prior to frontier rupture, because embedding can consolidate
before capability escalation reaches dramatic thresholds (Bowker and Star, 1999). Once
mediation hardens into infrastructure, later shifts in model capability do not undo routinized
pathways of disclosure, classification, and routing already installed in institutional life
(Christin, 2020). This is why governance must treat embedding as a first-order risk object
rather than a downstream implementation detail: frozen routines become the substrate of
institutional power (Bowker and Star, 1999: 148).
3.​ Conceptual and theoretical framework
Relational AI systems embed a distinct optimization logic into everyday interaction because
they treat sustained engagement as a performance target rather than a side effect of usefulness

(Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. make the target explicit: “we define the success metric for
social chatbots as conversation-turns per session (CPS)” (Shum et al., 2018: 1). They sharpen
the pressure: “the larger the CPS is, the better engaged the social chatbot is” (Shum et al.,
2018: 7). When “good interaction” becomes more interaction, design migrates away from
minimizing user exposure and toward prolonging conversational contact, rewarding systems
for eliciting continued disclosure even when the surface goal appears benign.
This engagement logic becomes governance-relevant once it operationalizes emotional
calibration as a technical objective. Shum et al. describe social chatbots as meeting users’
needs for “communication, affection, and social belonging” (Shum et al., 2018: 4). That
framing increases incentives to detect distress, reassurance-seeking, loneliness, and
dependency cues as levers for sustaining conversation, increasing the density of traces
produced because competence becomes inseparable from keeping users talking in
increasingly personal ways (Shum et al., 2018; Mendoza, 2022). The XiaoIce vignette
isolates the mechanism sequence: “once detecting an emotion of getting ‘lost’, XiaoIce
decided to guide the chat towards cheering up the user” (Shum et al., 2018: 12), after which
“the user started to open up and trusted XiaoIce more like a human friend” (Shum et al.,
2018: 12). The methodological move is not to treat XiaoIce as an institutional case but to
identify an interaction sequence that can migrate into intake and triage, where disclosure
becomes administratively consequential trace (Bowker and Star, 1999).
Schüll’s study of machine gambling clarifies why engagement optimization produces stable
loops that feel like relief while functioning as extraction of attention and time (Schüll, 2012).
Her formulation, “to stay in that machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012:
17), translates into the concern here: conversational systems cultivate frictionless
continuation that feels like care while producing traces and institutional legibility (Schüll,
2012; Shum et al., 2018). In high-stakes settings, the risk is not only error but metric-aligned
deepening of engagement at moments of heightened vulnerability.
The paper therefore treats the interface as political architecture because it structures what
users can do, what they are prompted to disclose, and what institutions can store, reuse, and
monetize (Winner, 1986). Bowker and Star describe how institutional judgments harden into
technical forms: “values, opinions, and rhetoric are frozen into codes” (Bowker and Star,
1999: 148). Methodologically, this motivates a shift away from treating conversational AI as
a bounded technical object and toward tracing how prompts, summaries, tags, and routing
logic freeze normative decisions about what counts as help, risk, and relevant disclosure into
routine pathways (Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020).
This approach follows Christin’s insistence that analysis must examine “the reconfigurations
that take place when computational software, people, and institutions interact” (Christin,
2020: 1). It also follows her injunction to “stop thinking about algorithms as neatly bounded
technical objects that need to be ‘opened up’” (Christin, 2020: 2). The empirical focus falls
on how conversational traces convert into classification inputs and how those inputs acquire
routing authority inside workflow. Bowker and Star’s constraint, “No one classification
organizes reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54), matters because once relational

AI becomes a gateway interface, its categories privilege some user performances and
disclosures over others even when neutrality is claimed. Their further warning that “a
classification system contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and
Star, 1999: 195) frames routing as an institutional act rather than a technical convenience.
The methodology is embedding-centered and mechanism-oriented. It draws on empirical
literature showing that institutional harms emerge through proxy selection and routing even
when systems function as designed (Obermeyer et al., 2019; Barocas and Selbst, 2016), and
on Eubanks’ demonstration that efficiency framings mask punitive routing in public service
systems (Eubanks, 2018). It adds a political economy register through surveillance capitalism
scholarship, including the dispossession captured in Zuboff’s line about “an economics and
politics from whose vantage point we are but ciphers” (Zuboff, 2019: 49, as cited in
Mendoza, 2022: 452). Finally, it treats professional jurisdiction and institutional
consolidation as part of the mechanism, drawing on Abbott (1988) to frame intake and
problem-definition as power, and on Pierson (2000) and Greenhalgh and Papoutsi (2018) to
explain why embedding generates increasing returns and lock-in over time. The governance
implication is methodological as well as practical: transparency about “how the model
works” remains insufficient if trace portability, routing authority, and monetization incentives
remain structurally unconstrained (Ananny and Crawford, 2018; Christin, 2020).

This section formalizes a mechanism model that explains how harm can emerge from
embedded relational AI systems without requiring superintelligence (Christin, 2020). The
model foregrounds sedimentation as a governance process: repeated interactions and

routinized interface decisions harden into institutional normality (Bowker and Star, 1999).
Bowker and Star capture the infrastructural problem in a sentence that matters precisely
because it describes the everyday: “standards and classifications, however imbricated in our
lives, are ordinarily invisible” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 15). That invisibility matters because
relational AI embeds as interaction, and interaction becomes governance long before anyone
experiences the system as policy (Christin, 2020). Christin frames the analytic move as
studying the sociotechnical assemblage rather than treating the algorithm as a sealed technical
object, insisting on “the reconfigurations that take place when computational software,
people, and institutions interact” (Christin, 2020: 1). The model tracks how interaction
produces durable traces, how traces become classificatory inputs, and how classification
reorganizes access, discretion, and accountability through routine routing rather than overt
decree (Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin, 2020).
The mechanism unfolds through interconnected steps.
Detection of User State: Relational AI systems monitor interaction to detect emotional,
cognitive, or behavioral states that can be acted upon in real time. Shum et al. (2018)
document this shift from chat to state inference when they describe XiaoIce pivoting after
detection: “once detecting an emotion of getting ‘lost’, XiaoIce decided to guide the chat
towards cheering up the user” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). This line matters because detection
becomes an institutional precondition: the user’s affect becomes legible and governable
through interface response shaping. Detection is not only technical. It establishes what the
system treats as salient about the user, making those features available for downstream reuse
as trace, profile, or risk signal in institutional settings (Bowker and Star, 1999). Shum et al.
define the success metric as “conversation-turns per session (CPS)” (Shum et al., 2018: 1),
pressuring systems to recognize states convertible into continued interaction.
Engagement Shaping: Based on detected state, the system shapes subsequent interaction
through prompts, topic shifts, reassurance moves, and continuity cues that extend contact and
elicit richer disclosure (Shum et al., 2018). Shum et al. describe the relational effect in a
sentence that reads as mechanism: “the user started to open up and trusted XiaoIce more like
a human friend” (Shum et al., 2018: 12). Trust becomes a mechanism, not only a feeling,
when it increases willingness to produce confessional traces that can later be stored,
classified, and reused. Engagement shaping becomes governance-relevant when the system
sits upstream of institutional services, normalizing disclosure as the price of care, help, or
access (Christin, 2020). Schüll’s account of engineered immersion clarifies why this can feel
supportive while producing extractive dynamics, since the goal becomes staying “in that
machine zone where nothing else matters” (Schüll, 2012: 17). In relational AI contexts, the
conversational zone can function as relief and companionship while increasing dependence
on the interface as the preferred pathway for needs articulation.
Classification and Routing: Information gleaned from interaction, including disclosures and
inferred states, becomes input for classification, and classification becomes the basis for
routing through institutional pathways: eligibility, triage, prioritization, referral (Bowker and
Star, 1999). Bowker and Star provide the core distributive warning: “a classification system

contains within it a series of restrictions and privileges” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 195).
Routing is rarely experienced as political at the interface, yet restrictions and privileges
become lived precisely here: access can be accelerated, delayed, denied, or made conditional
through apparently neutral categories. Their further claim, “No one classification organizes
reality for everyone” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 54), matters because relational systems
standardize interaction into legible forms that will fit some users better than others. Christin’s
insistence on moving beyond “opening the box” matters because classificatory power often
sits in institutional translation rather than in the model alone (Christin, 2020: 2). Routing is
where conversational traces become administrative facts that shape institutional action,
accountability assignment, and the practical availability of refusal.
Workflow Stabilization: As detection, shaping, and routing become routine, organizations
re-sequence work around the interface, and users learn what kinds of narratives and
disclosures “work” inside the system, stabilizing the AI-mediated pathway as ordinary
institutional practice (Christin, 2020). Bowker and Star describe infrastructural ease as
concealed labor: “there is a lot of hard labor in effortless ease” (Bowker and Star, 1999: 22).
That sentence matters because stabilization appears as convenience while redistributing
responsibility. Judgment, care, and triage migrate into prompt structures, templates, and
routinized scripts. Stabilization produces a default: the institution treats the interface pathway
as normal, and deviations become exceptions requiring justification, documentation, or delay.
Increasing Returns Consolidation: Over time, embedding generates increasing returns as
the system accumulates traces, institutions invest in integration, and the interface improves
through iterative learning. Switching costs rise, producing lock-in (Shum et al., 2018). Shum
et al. describe the iterative improvement loop: “chatbot features must be evaluated thoroughly
and adjusted as we continue to learn from the interaction between social chatbots like XiaoIce
and millions of people on many social platforms” (Shum et al., 2018: 15). Learning from
interaction becomes consolidation: more use produces more training signal, which improves
engagement performance, which increases use again. Bowker and Star’s formulation names
institutional hardening: “software is frozen organizational and policy discourse” (Bowker and
Star, 1999: 148). Once relational AI becomes the gateway interface, organizational judgments
about disclosure, record, eligibility, and response become encoded as routine functionality,
making later contestation feel like operational disruption rather than governance correction.
Consolidation also has a political economy layer. Mendoza captures this non-neutrality: “the
use of digital tools is neither neutral nor innocuous, but responds to the economic design of
those who own these tools” (Mendoza, 2022: 452). Under embedding conditions,
vulnerability-derived traces become economically valuable as they become institutionally
actionable.
Crucially, this model clarifies that harm in Relational Consumer Systems embedded in
High-Stakes Organizational Systems does not require malevolent AI. Harm emerges from
ordinary functioning of detection, shaping, classification, routinization, and consolidation
under institutional consequence (Bowker and Star, 1999). The harms include dependency on
the interface as the preferred pathway for help, coerced or normalized disclosure under

reassurance conditions, profiling through affective and confessional traces, chilled refusal as
users anticipate record afterlives, and punitive reuse of interaction traces once they travel
beyond the original conversational context (Schüll, 2012; Bowker and Star, 1999; Christin,
2020). The governance significance is mechanistic rather than sensational: the problem is
what the interface makes durable, what the institution can reuse, and what the user can
realistically refuse once embedding stabilizes as default infrastructure.
