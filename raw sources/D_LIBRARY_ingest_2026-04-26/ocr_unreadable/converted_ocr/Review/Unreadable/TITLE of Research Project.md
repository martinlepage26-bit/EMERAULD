## TITLE of Research Project

Masculinities in passing: perceptions of community in the age of chemsex among Montreal queer men

## STUDY BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE

Between the 1960s and the 1990s, the idea of a broader common gay identity among homosexual men laid ground for what was to later become the LGBTQ community. Along with the club scene, the HIV/AIDS crisis and the ever-ongoing political fights for equal rights in society, many factors over the years resulted in a larger number of safe spaces for gay, bisexual, trans and queer men. Many studies show how diverse gay and queer masculine identities allow men to explore and express their authentic selves through a variety of more or less sexual practices (Higgins, 1997; Hennen, 2008; Hébert, 2012). Despite safer spaces allowing for exploration of alternative non-heterosexual masculinities, some aspects of traditional masculinity that can be perceived as predatorial still find ways into the LGBTQ community. Practices like BDSM, leather kink, polyamory, to name a few, are often beneficial in the exploration and development of queer-centric masculine identities. However, they do not preclude other practices from being detrimental to the community. Alcohol and recreational drugs have often been associated with the gay lifestyle, and in the last decade, the gay community has seen new problems arise with the gain in popularity of chemsex or pnp, mostly around the use of crystal meth.

Chemsex, a term referring to behaviors and patterns that have appeared in academia only in recent years, is characterised as the intentional use of “specific drugs, used specifically for sex, by gay and other men who have sex with men” (Stuart, 2019) and trans people (Gaudette, 2022). Crystal meth, GHB and ketamine are the main substances used by people who practise chemsex (PPC), and they are used to enhance sexuality both in length (going up to several days) and in intensity (regarding number of partners and the types of sexual activities) (INSPQ, 2019). The lives of chemsexers are often centered around “binge” periods during which they may be using substances non-stop for several days. This often leads to unpredictable behaviour, risk taking and vulnerability in the larger social sense. Unlike other substances, there exists no known pharmacological substitution treatment. Rather, interventions centre around (CBT, talk therapies, identifying triggers, etc.). This increased vulnerability intersects with many chemsexers’ queer identities and has an exponential effect that often leads to social isolation, lack of self esteem, psychological disorders, inter alia.

Chemsex is becoming more and more rampant among increasingly gay, trans and queer men. This is greatly facilitated by encounters of sexual nature that begin online, more specifically on social networks and dating websites, one can ask what about the notion of community. While networks that cater to queer men can be helpful in fostering a sense of community and belonging, they can also have an isolating effect on the public's awareness of the issues surrounding chemsex. Media nowadays portrays gay identities in very bright colourful lights, on one hand, that often occult, on the other, pressing social public health issues. How do queer men identity with a community between those two extremes? This qualitative study is an exploration of perceptions among gay men of the notion of community today, in contrast to what it was before the sharp increase in magnitude of chemsex and methamphetamines (crystal meth) use in gay urban settings. More specifically, it aims to uncover and compare current trends among Montreal queer men who have experienced different stages of the LGBTQ community, in regards to the propagation of chemsex or pnp. Focusing specifically on the use of methamphetamines in a sexual context as a vector of harm and division within the LGBTQ community, it will underline possible changes in perceptions of community as a source of safe space, and sexual encounters as a community-making practice. In other words, are drugs and sex still as meaningful for queer men as a way to identify with their community as it was before? Furthermore, what are the social, ethical and health factors prescribing possible new ways of perceiving the queer community? We now know of the many darker consequences of chemsex and pnp on queer men’s lives, but are there any beneficial ones as well?

## Hypothesis

The popularisation of chemsex and the increasing numbers of people using crystal meth in Montreal has impacted in different ways the perceptions of younger and ageing queer men of the LGBTQ community as a safe space.

## Objectives and Endpoints

Primary objective is to assess perceptions of community among Montreal queer men in regards to chemsex and how they differ between younger and ageing MSM (men who have sex with men). Primary endpoint is to link changes in perception around community as safe space to the growing popularity of chemsex as a social phenomenon. Secondary objective is the production of data around gendered identities and the historicity of community as a safe space, and secondary endpoint is to further knowledge around health risk factors and harm-reduction in social behaviours within queer men’s experience of sexuality and sex as a normative act of self expression.

## Study Design

Research methods for this study will consist in a qualitative analysis of queer men’s perceptions of the impact of chemsex on their identification with the LGBTQ community. It will identify the more prominent issues among queer men’s experiences of the idea of community using categories like sense of belonging, sense of self, safe spaces, general well-being, mental health, sexual satisfaction, awareness of social and public health issues, among others. Data will be obtained from a questionnaire on the current state of the queer community, which will serve as a way to highlight in more detail the connections between perceptions of drug use, sexual behaviours, alternative masculinities and queer identities.

Study will be conducted as follows. Participants will be approached during priorly scheduled visit at Clinic L’Actuel, after basic screening for inclusion criteria. Participants will be presented a QR code with a hyperlink to an online questionnaire on the impact of chemsex on the LGBTQ community over time. This questionnaire will include a 10 questions scale survey, as well as 5 open-ended questions, allowing for deeper insights into participants’ lived experiences.

Sample size aims a total of 30 participants: 15 queer men between 18 and 50 years and 15 queer men above 50 years old. This will allow for data saturation and comparison between age groups potentially associating to different facets of the LGBTQ community. It will also account for cultural and religious backgrounds as they can offer deeper insights into queer men’s perceptions of community as a whole. Recruiting participants will be facilitated with Clinique L’Actuel, which caters mainly to queer men.

## Study Population

Clinique L’Actuel has developed in the last few years a unique expertise in the care of people practising chemsex (PPC) through our specialised psychosocial team. We have privileged access to Montreal’s chemsex community through our partnership with community organisations and specialised resources. We feel that a project of this magnitude designed for people practising chemsex as well as for people who have concerns about it will be well received in the community and that our partners will be happy to help with recruitment. We are also considering approaching participants from another study on chemsex that is currently taking place at Clinique L’Actuel.

Inclusion criteria

* Men who have sex with men (MSM) who are above 18
* Must know what chemsex or pnp is, whether or not they are PPC
* Must be able to express their experiences and perceptions in substantial terms in either English or French

Exclusion criteria

* Participants who do not have the capacity to express informed consent from admitting to be on drugs at the moment of the visit
