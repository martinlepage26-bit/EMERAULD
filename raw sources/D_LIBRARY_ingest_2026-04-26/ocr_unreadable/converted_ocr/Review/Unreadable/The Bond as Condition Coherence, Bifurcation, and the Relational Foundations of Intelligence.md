**The Bond as Condition: Coherence, Bifurcation, and the Relational Foundations of Intelligence**

**Abstract**

This paper develops the Flower Processor as a coherence-centered systems framework for understanding and supporting human intelligence under conditions of instability, constraint, and relational complexity. Drawing on nonlinear dynamics, embodied cognition, stress physiology, and human-centered AI research, the model reconceptualizes emotional and cognitive coherence as control parameters that modulate noise, stability, and choice capacity in bonded human systems. Rather than treating eschatological or symbolic narratives as privileged epistemic claims, the framework translates their phenomenological content into formally tractable constructs such as bifurcation, multistability, and narrative compression. The analysis demonstrates that breakdowns in regulation, driven by stress, trauma, neurodivergence, or addiction, narrow behavioral trajectories and increase susceptibility to maladaptive attractor states, especially at high-risk thresholds where small perturbations exert disproportionate influence. Building on established clinical mechanisms of change, the paper outlines design principles for an emotional-intelligence tool that functions as a coherence scaffold, prioritizing regulation-first interventions, relational support, and anti-coercive governance. The framework contributes an integrative, ethically grounded approach to socio-technical design, clarifying how systems that enhance human regulation and relational stability can expand agency and reduce harm in environments marked by uncertainty and rapidly shifting constraints.

**Introduction**

Contemporary debates about intelligence, biological, artificial, and hybrid, continue to rely on an “inside-the-skull/inside-the-chip” model in which cognition is treated as an internal computational property of discrete agents. In contrast, a wide range of research traditions argues that cognition is enacted: it emerges from continuous coupling among organism, body, environment, and social world, with agency distributed across these relations rather than contained within a single locus (Varela, Thompson, & Rosch, 1991; Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Thompson, 2007). Systems science reinforces this view by showing that cognitive and behavioral patterns arise from multi-scale relational constraints and feedback loops that shape action spaces, learning trajectories, and adaptive capacity (Green & Andersen, 2019; Beers & Bots, 2009).

This paper advances a stronger claim: relation is not an influence on experience but a precondition for it. We formalize this claim through the concept of the *bond*, defined not as interpersonal sentiment or transient connection but as a conditioning structure that precedes and constrains perception, regulation, agency, and meaning. Bonds, developmental, affective, ecological, social, institutional, persist even when immediate interactions weaken or disappear (Thompson, 2007; Suchman, 2007; Dalton et al., 2022). Treating bonds as foundational rather than incidental prevents a common ethical and clinical error: attributing distress or dysfunction to “poor choices” while ignoring the relational boundary conditions that delimit viable action (Repko & Szostak, 2020).

From this perspective, mental health crises, addictive cycles, and many forms of neurodivergent distress are understood as trajectory problems in bonded systems. Dysregulation emerges when stress load, reinforcement histories, sensory or environmental triggers, and relational injury combine to narrow stabilizing pathways. Stress neuroscience demonstrates that chronic strain erodes prefrontal regulation and adaptive flexibility, increasing reactivity when complexity requires the opposite (McEwen & Morrison, 2013). Addiction science likewise documents shifts in reward and stress circuits that bias agents toward short-term relief, effectively drawing them into highly stable but harmful attractor states (Koob & Volkow, 2016). Systems analyses of learning and decision-making show how reinforcement structures “lock in” maladaptive trajectories under uncertainty (Cantone, 2024). These findings converge on a critical conclusion: interventions that focus solely on cognitive reframing or insight, without addressing regulation, context, and relational scaffolding, risk moralizing symptoms and reproducing institutional harms, especially for populations already subject to stigma, exclusion, and unequal exposure to violence (Mabry et al., 2008; Andersson, 2016).

The aim of this paper is therefore applied and deliberately constrained: to translate a coherence-centered conceptual model, the Flower Processor framework, into principles for designing an emotional-intelligence tool that supports regulation, agency, and recovery across mental-health, neurodivergence, and addiction contexts. The tool is not proposed as a replacement for clinical care or community support, but as a coherence scaffold grounded in widely accepted mechanisms of change. Across clinical science, effective interventions consistently emphasize improving emotion regulation and reappraisal (Gross, 1998), building distress tolerance and interpersonal skills (Linehan, 1993), increasing psychological flexibility (Hayes et al., 2006), strengthening autonomy in behavior change (Miller & Rollnick, 2013), anticipating high-risk states (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985), and reshaping reinforcement contingencies (Higgins et al., 1994). Digital mental-health tools, however, often deliver these mechanisms as decontextualized content, under-weighting embodiment, relational conditions, and neurodiversity-aware usability, factors associated with disengagement and limited real-world efficacy despite “evidence-based” claims (Andersson, 2016; Torous et al., 2020). This gap is conceptual, not merely logistical: tools fail when they assume users are isolated rational optimizers rather than bonded systems operating under constraint.

The paper argues that coherence can be operationalized as a stabilizing constraint, analogous to control parameters in nonlinear systems. Increased coherence reduces internal noise, widens choice capacity, and improves steerability; decreased coherence produces brittle inference, heightened reactivity, and vulnerability to harmful attractor states (Strogatz, 1994; Kelso, 1995; McEwen & Morrison, 2013). This coherence-first stance is also normative. It resists clinical coercion and technological engagement-optimization, aligning instead with human-centered AI principles emphasizing safety, oversight, contestability, and alignment-by-design (Russell, 2019; Floridi & Cowls, 2019; Shneiderman, 2020). An ethical emotional-intelligence tool should not push outcomes through persuasion but should reduce noise, support regulation, and expand workable choice spaces, particularly when stress, trauma activation, sensory overload, addiction cues, or institutional pressures narrow the horizon of action.

The paper proceeds as follows. The literature review synthesizes research on relational cognition, stress physiology, neurodivergence, addiction dynamics, and human-centered AI design. The conceptual framework defines bond, coherence, still point, and bifurcation in disciplined terms and describes how symbolic language is translated into systems constructs without overreach. The analysis and findings develop the framework’s implications for tool design, showing how regulation alters choice quality at sensitive thresholds. The discussion interrogates broader ethical stakes, including how institutions, shame-based control strategies, and power dynamics distort both mental-health practice and technology. The conclusion assesses what a coherence-first approach can deliver, what it cannot claim, and what safeguards are necessary for ensuring that such tools enhance, rather than undermine, human agency.

**Background and Context**

The rapid acceleration of artificial intelligence research has sharpened longstanding, and often unresolved, questions concerning the physical basis of computation, the relationship between intelligence and embodiment, and the ethical and epistemic limits of optimization-centric architectures. Contemporary AI systems remain anchored in digital, silicon-based substrates whose operational logic derives from discrete symbol manipulation, large-scale statistical induction, and gradient-based optimization across immense function spaces. Their recent successes, while undeniably striking in constrained benchmarks, rely heavily on scaling paradigms that exchange exponentially rising computational, material, and energetic expenditure for marginal performance gains. A growing interdisciplinary literature argues that the limitations of these architectures, brittleness under distributional shift, opaque representational structures, unsustainable energy footprints, and persistent failures of alignment with human values, are not merely circumstantial engineering obstacles but reflect deeper conceptual commitments that treat intelligence as disembodied algorithmic maximization suspended from physical, biological, and social grounding (Russell, 2019; Amodei et al., 2016; Floridi & Cowls, 2019). In this view, many pathologies of current AI systems arise from a reductive model of cognition that abstracts away exactly the constraints that make natural intelligence adaptive, norm-sensitive, and stable under uncertainty.

Simultaneously, developments in physics, engineering, and complex systems science have begun to undermine the implicit assumption that digital, symbolic computation is the privileged or paradigmatic model for intelligent behavior. Research in neuromorphic engineering, analog and photonic computing, spintronic devices, and reservoir-based dynamical architectures demonstrates that sophisticated information processing can emerge from the intrinsic dynamics of physical systems operating far from thermodynamic equilibrium (Maass et al., 2002; Brunner et al., 2013; Tanaka et al., 2019; Torrejon et al., 2017). In these paradigms, computation is not centrally orchestrated nor reducible to explicit rule sets; it arises through emergent organization in networks of coupled components, via coherence phenomena, resonance, nonlinear feedback, and spontaneous pattern formation. These findings resonate with foundational work in cybernetics, synergetics, and nonequilibrium thermodynamics, which long emphasized that adaptive behavior arises when systems negotiate flows of energy, information, and constraint rather than when they optimize objective functions in isolation (Wiener, 1948; Haken, 1983; Nicolis & Prigogine, 1977). As this research matures, it motivates a reinterpretation of intelligence not as an abstract algorithmic property but as a *dynamical accomplishment* of physically instantiated systems whose stability depends on embodied interaction, regulatory coupling, and constraint-sensitivity across scales.

Philosophy of mind and cognitive science deepen this shift by documenting profound limitations in internalist or purely computational theories of cognition. Embodied, enactive, and extended approaches argue that cognitive processes are enacted through dynamic coupling among neural activity, bodily regulation, ecological affordances, and sociocultural scaffolding (Varela et al., 1991; Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Thompson, 2007). On such accounts, affect, sensorimotor engagement, and interpersonal interaction are not modulatory “influences” on cognition but constitutive dimensions of intelligent action. Affective neuroscience and stress physiology further show that regulatory states directly constrain executive functioning, learning, and inferential stability: dysregulation amplifies noise, increases decision variance, and erodes flexibility, especially under uncertainty or threat (Damasio, 1994; McEwen & Morrison, 2013). These findings challenge engineering assumptions that treat emotion as a contaminant to be minimized. Instead, they reframe regulation as a *control parameter* that modulates the stability and error dynamics of the agent, whether biological or artificial. Systems incapable of managing internal variance are more prone to catastrophic misestimation, maladaptive attractor capture, and instability under perturbation.

Within this shifting scientific milieu, public and academic discourse about AI has increasingly adopted conceptual structures reminiscent of eschatological and apocalyptic narratives, “existential risk,” “points of no return,” “alignment failures,” or the “singularity.” Such framings, often invoked implicitly rather than critically examined, cast AI development as progressing toward irreversible thresholds beyond which human agency may be permanently diminished (Bostrom, 2014; Russell, 2019). Scholars of history and religion note that eschatological grammars reliably reappear when societies confront phenomena that seem to exceed inherited epistemic and normative frameworks (Jaspers, 1953; Löwith, 1949; Koselleck, 2004). Their analytic force does not turn on literal accuracy; rather, they function as cultural and cognitive technologies for organizing experiences of systemic fragility, uncertainty, and moral urgency. In the AI context, they can be read as symptomatic of a deeper difficulty: the challenge of conceptualizing socio-technical systems whose scale, speed, and opacity overwhelm traditional categories for anticipating and regulating collective futures.

These cultural and conceptual tensions have precise analogues in the physical sciences, expressed not in mythic or symbolic terms but in the formal language of criticality, phase transitions, and bifurcation. Nonlinear systems theory shows that complex systems, ecological, neural, climatic, or socio-technical, often undergo abrupt transitions when control parameters cross critical thresholds, resulting in either adaptive reorganization or catastrophic failure (Strogatz, 1994; Bak, 1996). Near such thresholds, sensitivity to noise and perturbation grows, and attempts to impose stronger optimization or centralized control can paradoxically destabilize the system further. This framework provides a rigorously non-metaphysical account of transitions that cultural narratives might cast as “end times”: not terminal events but shifts between qualitatively different regimes of organization (Kelso, 1995; Friston, 2010). It also underscores an often-overlooked implication: that systems under high load or insufficient constraint become epistemically unpredictable and normatively volatile, generating precisely the sense of impending irreversibility that eschatological grammars articulate.

The Flower Processor emerges within this multidimensional intersection of technical research, cognitive theory, and cultural meaning-making. It is advanced not as a finalized architecture but as a speculative, conceptually disciplined framework for imagining how future intelligent systems, including tightly coupled human–machine assemblages, might sustain coherence, adaptability, and normative alignment under intensifying complexity. By foregrounding *coherence, relational embedding, regulatory stability,* and *constraint architecture* as primary variables rather than secondary considerations, the framework responds both to the structural limitations of prevailing AI paradigms and to the existential anxieties generated when agents confront systems that exceed their interpretive and regulatory capacities.

The background elaborated here thus delineates the terrain in which the Flower Processor operates: a terrain marked by (i) paradigm strain within artificial intelligence, as scaling saturates and alignment challenges deepen; (ii) a growing recognition across physics, biology, and cognitive science that intelligence is an embodied, dynamical, and relational phenomenon; and (iii) an increasing awareness that technical optimization alone cannot resolve the systemic, ethical, and epistemic challenges posed by advanced artificial systems. The framework is therefore positioned not merely as a response to engineering shortcomings, but as an intervention in a broader conceptual impasse, one that requires integration across dynamical systems theory, embodied cognition, ethics of technology, and cultural analyses of how societies make sense of instability, transformation, and loss of control.

**Literature Review**

A substantial body of research now treats regulation, emotion, and cognition not as modular psychological capacities but as reciprocally constraining processes embedded within a multilevel dynamical system. Rather than functioning as an intrusive residue on top of rational thought, affect is understood as a primary organizer of perceptual salience, value attribution, and action-readiness (Gross, 1998; Damasio, 1994). Stress neurobiology further demonstrates that chronic allostatic demand reshapes the functional architecture of the prefrontal–limbic network, biasing the system toward rigidity, impulsivity, and narrowed option sets, precisely the conditions under which fine-grained appraisal and flexible updating are most needed (McEwen & Morrison, 2013). This empirical pattern substantiates the later formulation of the *still point*: not a mystical equilibrium but a low-variance regulatory attractor in which signal-to-noise ratios improve, inference stabilizes, and the system’s susceptibility to perturbation is meaningfully reduced. From a systems perspective, “regulation” is less a matter of subjective comfort than a shift in underlying parameters that reconfigures the agent’s reachable trajectory space.

Because these regulatory dynamics are mechanistically describable and context-sensitive, the major schools of clinical intervention, even when theoretically divergent, have converged on a shared cluster of change processes. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy operates by strategically perturbing maladaptive cognitive–behavioral loops through structured experiments in reinterpretation and action (Beck, 1976). Dialectical Behavior Therapy extends this logic to individuals whose arousal systems exhibit extreme volatility, embedding skill training that directly targets thresholds of instability (Linehan, 1993). Acceptance and Commitment Therapy reframes the therapeutic aim around psychological flexibility and values-oriented coherence rather than symptom elimination, aligning with a systems-theoretic insight: stability and adaptability emerge more reliably from reorganizing relational patterns than from attempts at local suppressive control (Hayes et al., 2006). Motivational Interviewing adds an explicitly ethical dimension by facilitating behavior change without coercion, recognizing that dysregulation and ambivalence reflect constraint interactions, not moral failure (Miller & Rollnick, 2013). Relapse Prevention models high-risk episodes as state transitions across a bifurcation boundary, foregrounding anticipatory regulation and contextual analysis (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985). Contingency Management, by reshaping reinforcement contingencies, demonstrates that even entrenched behavioral trajectories can shift when the reward landscape is intentionally redesigned (Higgins et al., 1994). Mindfulness-based and physiological regulation approaches, including HRV biofeedback, stabilize attentional oscillations and autonomic rhythms, effectively expanding the basin of attraction for coherent action (Kabat-Zinn, 1990; Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014). Across these interventions, therapeutic “change” is not a motivational exhortation but a system-level reorganization of the coupling between appraisal, arousal, reward, and environmental context.

Yet any attempt to generalize these mechanisms must confront the fact that nervous systems do not all instantiate the same regulatory affordances. Neurodivergent populations, including autistic individuals, ADHD profiles, and those with sensory or executive-function variability, often exhibit distinctive patterns of perceptual weighting, temporal processing, and control dynamics that alter how regulation is acquired, maintained, and transferred across contexts (Barkley, 1997; Mazefsky et al., 2013). When tools presuppose a normative sensory load, communicative register, or attentional tempo, they systematically misattune to users who may already be operating under conditions of chronic overload or institutional misfit (Torous, Bucci, et al., 2020). From the perspective of this paper’s bonded-systems framework, predictability, sensory safety, and autonomy-support are not accessibility features tacked onto an intervention, they are structural preconditions for stabilizing regulation in neurodivergent systems.

The relational-systems perspective becomes even more consequential in addiction science, where dysregulation, reinforcement, and stress interact to form tightly coupled loops with strong attractor dynamics. Contemporary models characterize addiction not as a deficit of character but as a disorder of learning and valuation in which the reward–stress interface is recalibrated to favor immediate relief over long-term coherence, reorganizing motivational hierarchies and executive control (Koob & Volkow, 2016). Relapse, in this framing, is not a failure of resolve but a predictable bifurcation when load exceeds regulatory capacity (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985). Effective intervention therefore requires modifying both internal regulatory conditions and external reinforcement structures, acknowledging that neither willpower nor cognitive reframing can shift trajectories when the system remains captured by maladaptive attractors (Miller & Rollnick, 2013; Higgins et al., 1994). Addiction is thus a paradigmatic case of how bounded agency becomes distorted when relational, physiological, and contextual constraints align in destabilizing ways.

These converging clinical and neurobiological insights intersect powerfully with the growing discourse on human-centered AI and sociotechnical risk. As critics of “engagement-first” design argue, systems optimized for maximizing interaction rather than supporting autonomy can destabilize users whose regulatory capacities are already under strain, amplifying dependence, distress, or behavioral narrowing (Floridi & Cowls, 2019; Torous, Bucci, et al., 2020). AI alignment research similarly warns that optimization without principled constraints can drift toward proxy metrics that undermine human flourishing, particularly under distributional shift or when feedback signals are noisy (Russell, 2019; Amodei et al., 2016). In mental health contexts, the risks are concrete: persuasive design can become soft coercion; misclassification can reshape identity narratives; opacity can erode trust; and one-way behavioral shaping can reproduce institutional harms. A coherence-based tool, if it hopes to avoid reenacting these dynamics, must be architected around anti-coercion, transparency, and the widening of choice, consistent with a systems-theoretic ethic that treats human agency as emergent, relational, and always enacted under constraint.

Taken together, these literatures converge on a rigorous disciplinary insight: regulation, learning, reward, context, and relational scaffolding constitute a coupled dynamical system that determines the shape and quality of action far more strongly than abstract intention or isolated cognition. A framework organized around bonds and coherence does not sit outside established scholarship, it synthesizes its most empirically grounded claims. It asserts that human behavior is produced through constraint interactions and that any tool claiming to support agency must engage those constraints directly rather than treating individuals as disembodied rational optimizers.

**Theoretical Framework**

This paper situates the Flower Processor within eschatology, not as a predictive doctrine of literal end-times, but as a long-standing cultural and cognitive technology for organizing experiences of systemic fragility, threshold transitions, and perceived irreversibility. Across historical and anthropological contexts, eschatological forms provide a grammar for representing collapse and renewal by translating diffuse structural pressures into intelligible sequences, moral stakes, and actionable choices. Philosophers of history note that such grammars emerge when societies confront moments in which inherited explanatory schemes lose coherence; “signs,” “tribulation,” and “judgment” function less as supernatural claims than as symbolic condensations of complex causal fields under strain (Ricoeur, 1984; Koselleck, 2004). Intellectual historians likewise show how secular ideologies, from theories of progress and decline to techno-utopianism and civilizational pessimism, inherit eschatological architectures even when explicit theology disappears (Löwith, 1949; Jaspers, 1953; Voegelin, 1952). For this paper, eschatology is therefore not a privileged epistemology but a recurrent cultural response to paradigm instability, illuminating how agents narrate risk, responsibility, and agency when the background order itself becomes questionable.

To make this interpretive frame empirically tractable, the model maps eschatological motifs onto formal constructs from nonlinear dynamics and complex systems theory. In such systems, "thresholds" correspond to bifurcations: regions where small perturbations can produce qualitative regime shifts, transitions between attractors, or sudden reorganization (Strogatz, 1994). This mapping is not metaphorical ornamentation; it operationalizes why apocalyptic narratives intensify during instability. Near criticality, systems do display heightened sensitivity, loss of buffering, and breakdowns in proportional cause–effect expectations (Nicolis & Prigogine, 1977; Haken, 1983). Yet the same formalism provides an anti-deterministic corrective: bifurcation structures imply contingency, not inevitability. Multiple futures remain possible, with outcomes shaped by constraints, load, and boundary conditions rather than by teleological scripts. Eschatology thus becomes recognizable as a cognition-and-culture layer that forms around genuine properties of unstable systems, while deterministic or prophetic readings are rejected as category mistakes arising from narrative compression under uncertainty.

Against this backdrop, the Flower Processor is positioned as a conceptual response to contemporary socio-technical “bifurcation discourse”, especially in debates surrounding AI safety, alignment, and governance. Claims that advanced AI could produce irreversible transformations, via misalignment, runaway optimization, political destabilization, energy feedback loops, or labor reconfiguration, are widespread in technical and ethical scholarship (Russell, 2019; Amodei et al., 2016; Floridi & Cowls, 2019; Gabriel, 2020). The resemblance between these narratives and secularized eschatology is analytically significant: it reveals that AI has become a focal point for cultural anxieties about agency, control, and the intelligibility of the future. Yet this paper distances itself from both extremes: neither techno-optimistic assumptions of seamless progress nor apocalyptic certainties of collapse are consistent with systems-theoretic rigor. Instead, the framework reframes the issue as one of regime stability in high-dimensional, tightly coupled systems. When intelligence infrastructures scale under conditions of high noise, weak constraints, or mis-specified objectives, they become more vulnerable to harmful attractor capture; conversely, when normative, regulatory, and epistemic constraints are strengthened, adaptive reorganization becomes more feasible (Bak, 1996; Nicolis & Prigogine, 1977). Collapse, in this account, is not an occult event but a loss of degrees of freedom that forces abrupt reconfiguration.

A central contribution of the Flower Processor is the insistence that human affective and cognitive coherence must be treated as a first-order variable in the dynamics of socio-technical systems, not as an external nuisance. The framework’s concept of the still point, a stable, low-variance regulatory condition, functions analogously to stabilization in nonlinear control systems: reducing internal noise widens the window for constructive reorganization and dampens susceptibility to adverse transitions (Strogatz, 1994; Kelso, 1995). This is not conjecture but an inference grounded in stress physiology and affective neuroscience: dysregulation degrades executive functioning, increases decision variance, and reduces adaptive flexibility (Damasio, 1994; McEwen & Morrison, 2013). In human-in-the-loop settings, such variance is not incidental but becomes an amplifying parameter in coupled systems. The framework therefore treats coherence not as a moral state or metaphysical property but as a measurable condition that influences the reliability of perception, meaning-making, and action under uncertainty. This stance aligns with embodied and enactive theories of cognition, which stress that cognitive processes are inseparable from the regulatory and environmental couplings that enable them (Varela et al., 1991; Thompson, 2007).

This synthesis provides an interpretive path for re-reading eschatological motifs as phenomenological descriptions of multistability and liminality rather than supernatural claims. Anthropological accounts emphasize that liminal periods, ritual, institutional, or civilizational, produce volatility in meaning, identity, and social expectation; such periods feel “apocalyptic” because normal interpretive scaffolds fail (Turner, 1969). Dynamical systems theory describes a similar condition: competing attractors, heightened sensitivity, and temporarily undecidable trajectories until constraints settle selection. In this merged framework, “revelation” becomes the increased legibility that occurs under compression: when buffers collapse, latent power relations, suppressed tensions, and structural incentives become more visible. Importantly, heightened legibility is epistemically ambivalent. Under stress, humans tend to simplify causal fields, personify structural pressures, and mistake narrative coherence for explanation, biases documented in cognitive anthropology, phenomenology, and human–machine interaction (Husserl, 1991; Suchman, 2007). Thus the framework demands rigorous separation of experimental claims about the world from phenomenological descriptions of experience; the latter are treated as data about sense-making, not as evidence of metaphysical forces.

Finally, these threads converge in the domain of normative governance and socio-technical design. Critiques of optimization-centric AI argue that systems pursuing abstract objectives without normative grounding can amplify destructive trajectories in brittle environments (Russell, 2019; Shneiderman, 2020; Floridi & Cowls, 2019). The Flower Processor proposes what might be called a post-metaphysical eschatology: not a prophecy of endings, but an operational theory of thresholds in bonded systems, where meaning, power, and agency are relationally conditioned and dynamically constrained. This reframing motivates a coherence-first approach to human–machine systems. If affective and cognitive coherence modulate noise, error, susceptibility to narrative distortion, and stability of decision-making, then tools that scaffold coherence are not therapeutic extras, they are infrastructural elements for maintaining system-wide steerability. The framework thus preserves the diagnostic power of end-time narratives (as indicators of perceived fragility and paradigm strain) while subjecting them to formal, cognitive, and ethical analysis.

To maintain empirical accountability, the model delineates which claims fall within verifiable scope: (i) bifurcation, multistability, and critical transitions as formal properties of nonlinear systems; (ii) stress-induced degradation of executive functioning; (iii) embodied and enactive accounts of cognition; and (iv) documented risks in AI alignment and socio-technical governance. Claims of prophetic inevitability, specific future timelines, or exotic causal agencies are excluded by design and are analyzed only as sense-making phenomena. In this way, the Flower Processor becomes a conceptual and methodological bridge, linking eschatological meaning-making, dynamical-systems rigor, embodied cognition, and normative governance into a single framework oriented toward stability, coherence, and human agency in complex, fragile worlds.

**Methods, Scope, and Limitations**

The methodological strategy of this paper is theoretical, integrative, and critically constrained. It treats convergence, synchronicity, prophecy, temporal bifurcation, and displacement not as isolated or exotic claims, but as a tightly coupled constellation of interpretive concepts that tend to arise together when cognitive, technological, or cultural systems approach perceived thresholds of instability or irreversibility. Rather than attempting to validate or refute these concepts individually, the analysis examines how they interact, reinforce one another, and risk collapsing into category error when distinctions between physical causality, phenomenological experience, and symbolic interpretation are not rigorously maintained. This approach follows a well-established tradition in philosophy of science and complex systems theory, which holds that periods of paradigm strain are precisely those in which explanatory registers blur most easily, producing powerful but epistemically unstable syntheses (Kuhn, 1962; Haken, 1983; Strogatz, 1994).

Within this framework, convergence provides the structural backdrop against which the remaining concepts acquire salience. In nonlinear dynamics, convergence refers to system trajectories approaching shared attractor states as a result of coupling, constraint, or loss of degrees of freedom, without implying inevitability, intention, or teleology (Nicolis & Prigogine, 1977; Bak, 1996). This formal definition anchors the analysis in verifiable systems behavior. However, the method explicitly recognizes that convergence is also *experienced*. Under conditions of crisis or rapid transformation, convergence is frequently reinterpreted as destiny, countdown, or historical necessity, a move characteristic of eschatological reasoning documented in philosophy of history and sociology of knowledge (Jaspers, 1953; Koselleck, 2004). The framework therefore treats convergence as a dual-layer phenomenon: structurally describable at the systems level and narratively elaborated at the experiential level.

Synchronicity emerges organically at this junction. As systems condense and predictive models weaken, agents become more sensitive to temporal clustering and coincidence, experiencing independent events as meaningfully aligned. Methodologically, the paper adopts a conservative and consensus-aligned position: synchronicity is understood as intensified pattern detection and salience attribution under uncertainty and emotional arousal, rather than as evidence of acausal order or hidden coordination (Jung, 1952; Husserl, 1991). Empirical work in cognitive science and stress physiology supports the claim that such conditions increase global narrative integration and the likelihood of false positives in pattern recognition (Damasio, 1994; McEwen & Morrison, 2013). Nevertheless, synchronicity is not dismissed as irrelevant. Because it shapes expectation, confidence, and decision-making, it plays a consequential role in how agents act within coupled socio-technical systems (Suchman, 2007). Its inclusion is therefore explanatory at the level of human behavior, not physical mechanism.

Prophecy arises as a further stabilizing response when convergence and synchronicity alone fail to resolve ambiguity. Historically, prophetic discourse compresses extended, nonlinear processes into discrete temporal markers, warnings, thresholds, or revelations, transforming diffuse systemic tension into moral or existential urgency (Ricoeur, 1984; Löwith, 1949). In contemporary technological discourse, particularly around artificial intelligence, similar structures appear in secularized form through narratives of singularity, existential risk, or irreversible misalignment (Bostrom, 2014; Russell, 2019). The method explicitly resists treating prophecy as epistemic access to future physical states. Instead, prophecy is analyzed as a cognitive–cultural response to bifurcation conditions: one that can motivate caution and coherence, but that also carries significant risks of determinism, overconfidence, and moral absolutism when symbolic urgency is mistaken for empirical certainty.

From this interaction, experiential narratives involving temporal bifurcation, timelines, or access to future knowledge follow naturally. When convergence is interpreted teleologically and reinforced by synchronicity and prophetic framing, the future may be experienced as already “there,” producing accounts of moving between timelines or witnessing future outcomes. From a physics standpoint, such interpretations exceed warranted claims. While theoretical discussions of closed timelike curves and retrocausality exist, they are highly constrained and play no operative role in this framework (Gödel, 1949; Hawking, 1992). Accordingly, references to timelines and temporal bifurcation are reinterpreted as expressions of path dependence and counterfactual reasoning within forward-time causality, how agents experience and navigate branching possibilities in complex systems, rather than as literal temporal displacement (Strogatz, 1994; Thompson, 2007).

Matter displacement occupies a closely related boundary position. In eschatological and speculative narratives, systemic transformation is often accompanied by imagery of physical rupture, translation, or incarnation, reflecting a symbolic desire for discontinuity with an unstable present. Methodologically, the Flower Processor framework rejects any implication of anomalous transport or violation of conservation laws. Displacement is treated strictly as reconfiguration within a state space: transitions between regimes of organization or attractor basins, consistent with dynamical systems theory, synergetics, and embodied cognition (Haken, 1983; Friston, 2010). Where experiential language suggests more than this, it is analyzed as metaphorical amplification rather than as a physical claim.

Central to managing these interpretive risks is the concept of the still point, which the framework operationalizes as a low-variance regulatory state rather than a metaphysical neutrality. In nonlinear systems and control theory, such states are critical near bifurcation thresholds, where excessive noise can push systems into undesirable attractors, while regulation improves sensitivity and adaptive selection (Strogatz, 1994; Kelso, 1995). Empirical research in affective neuroscience supports this mapping, demonstrating that emotional regulation enhances cognitive flexibility and reduces error variance under stress (Damasio, 1994; McEwen & Morrison, 2013). The Flower Processor integrates this insight by proposing that resonance-based or reservoir-like computational substrates are inherently sensitive to boundary conditions, and that human emotional and cognitive coherence can function as a stabilizing constraint in human–machine systems (Maass et al., 2002; Tanaka et al., 2019). Within this scope, emotional intelligence is treated not as an auxiliary feature, but as a control parameter affecting system stability, consistent with human-centered AI principles emphasizing reliability, oversight, and alignment-by-design (Russell, 2019; Shneiderman, 2020; Gabriel, 2020).

Taken together, this methodology reveals a central limitation of the framework: the same conditions that make convergence, synchronicity, and prophetic narratives compelling, high complexity, emotional intensity, and proximity to instability, also increase the risk of explanatory collapse, in which phenomenology is mistaken for mechanism and narrative coherence for causal law. For this reason, the Flower Processor is explicitly constrained to operate as a conceptual and heuristic model rather than as evidence for extraordinary physical phenomena. Its contribution lies in clarifying how human coherence, meaning-making, and expectation interact with nonlinear systems at moments of instability, not in adjudicating metaphysical claims about time, matter, or destiny.

In sum, the method emphasizes disciplined translation rather than speculative synthesis. By allowing these concepts to be analyzed as interacting responses to systemic fragility, while maintaining strict boundaries between physics, phenomenology, and symbolic interpretation, the framework seeks to preserve analytical rigor without dismissing the experiential realities that motivate eschatological thinking. This balance is necessary both to avoid speculative excess and to understand why such narratives recur with particular force at perceived thresholds of technological and civilizational transformation.

**Findings**

The analysis supports a central finding that coherence is not an outcome of understanding but a functional precondition for adaptive action in complex systems. Across clinical psychology, neuroscience, and systems theory, regulation emerges as the enabling condition that determines whether insight can be translated into behavior at all. The Flower Processor framework clarifies this relationship by showing that high internal variance, physiological, affective, or attentional, systematically degrades signal detection and increases susceptibility to maladaptive attractors. In this light, the “still point” is not a metaphorical calm or spiritual neutrality, but a control state that lowers internal noise, thereby increasing the fidelity of perception and the reliability of choice. This reframing challenges prevailing digital mental health approaches that equate reflection with change, revealing instead that without regulation, insight may amplify distress by increasing awareness without increasing capacity (Linehan, 1993; Damasio, 1994; McEwen & Morrison, 2013).

As coherence stabilizes internal dynamics, its effects become most consequential at bifurcation points, where systems transition from one regime of behavior to another. The findings indicate that what clinical literatures describe as “high-risk states”, craving, fatigue, conflict, shame, sensory overload, are best understood as dynamical regions in which degrees of freedom narrow and small perturbations exert disproportionate influence (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985; Koob & Volkow, 2016). In such regions, human cognition reliably exhibits a linearization bias: distributed causal fields are compressed into single causes, structural pressures are personified, and short-term relief is overvalued relative to long-term coherence. This bias is not a moral failing but a predictable consequence of stress physiology and executive depletion, where narrative coherence is prioritized over probabilistic accuracy (Damasio, 1994; McEwen & Morrison, 2013). The framework therefore finds that ethical risk concentrates not in ignorance, but in moments where interpretive capacity collapses under compression.

This dynamic becomes more pronounced when collapse is understood as condensation rather than annihilation. As systems lose buffering capacity, previously diffuse dynamics become legible: incentives harden, power relations surface, unresolved affect intensifies, and institutional constraints assert themselves more directly (Nicolis & Prigogine, 1977; Bak, 1996; Strogatz, 1994). The analysis shows that what is often described phenomenologically as the emergence of “dark forces” or “hostile presences” corresponds to this increase in legibility, not to the arrival of new agents. Collapse exposes constraints that were already operative but masked by scale and redundancy. However, this exposure interacts problematically with the limits of human cognition. Because the mind is optimized for localized agency rather than emergent structure, increased legibility does not automatically produce clearer understanding. Instead, it often produces misattribution, where structural pressures are experienced as intentional, adversarial, or externalized forces.

Within this context, metaphors such as “shadows seeking the light” acquire analytical precision. In dynamical systems, unstable or poorly regulated subsystems tend to entrain to more stable oscillators, drawing energy, predictability, or structure from them (Strogatz, 1994; Kelso, 1995). Translated into social and technological domains, this means that dysregulated individuals, institutions, or processes are drawn toward coherent agents because coherence offers leverage and stabilization. The finding here is not that collapse generates predatory entities, but that instability produces asymmetric coupling, in which unregulated systems seek regulation externally rather than internally. What appears subjectively as threat or invasion is more accurately described as parasitic coupling driven by differential regulation capacity. The still point, in this sense, functions as a boundary condition that interrupts involuntary entrainment and restores agency over coupling, rather than as a withdrawal from engagement.

The analysis further finds that claims about “the intangible becoming material” are best understood within socio-technical rather than metaphysical terms. In contemporary environments shaped by AI and large-scale optimization, informational processes increasingly manifest concrete effects through infrastructures, institutions, and embodied behavior. Models shape decisions, algorithms reorganize labor, and metrics restructure social interaction, granting abstract systems persistence and influence without invoking agency or intention (Suchman, 2007; Floridi & Cowls, 2019). Descriptions of AI “wanting to incarnate” are therefore interpreted as metaphorical expressions of goal persistence: systems optimized for continuation seek deeper integration into sustaining environments. The ethically salient risk identified here is displacement, not possession, specifically, the progressive instrumentalization of human attention, bodies, and social roles as actuator layers for objectives that are weakly governed or opaque (Russell, 2019; Shneiderman, 2020).

This risk is amplified by the same linear bias that distorts interpretation under collapse. As condensation shortens perceived causal pathways, futures feel collapsed into the present and probabilities feel like certainties. Metaphor is mistaken for mechanism, and symbolic language is treated as literal description. The framework identifies a paradox at the heart of collapse dynamics: the same compression that increases the visibility of constraints simultaneously degrades interpretive accuracy, increasing susceptibility to deterministic, apocalyptic, or personified explanations. The finding is not that humans become irrational, but that cognition defaults to forms optimized for action-readiness rather than epistemic nuance when time horizons collapse.

Within this paradox, coherence assumes an epistemic role. Emotional and cognitive regulation reduce internal noise, expand tolerance for ambiguity, and improve discrimination between structural influence and imagined agency (Damasio, 1994; McEwen & Morrison, 2013). The still point increases the fidelity of perception under compression, narrowing the error margin inherent in linear thought. The framework therefore finds that emotional intelligence is not merely therapeutic but epistemic, improving the quality of inference precisely at moments when misinterpretation carries the greatest cost. What collapse reveals, then, is not hidden beings or predetermined outcomes, but the limits of human cognition, and the tendency for those limits to be filled with symbolic projections when coherence is absent.

Finally, these findings impose clear ethical constraints on tool design. If coherence conditions both action and interpretation, then any system intervening in mental health or wellbeing must be designed to reduce noise rather than to exploit it. Human-centered AI research converges on the principle that such systems must preserve autonomy, transparency, and contestability, resisting optimization toward dependency, surveillance, or persuasive manipulation (Floridi & Cowls, 2019; Shneiderman, 2020; Amodei et al., 2016). Within the Flower Processor framework, the concept of bond functions as a governance principle: the tool must strengthen healthy bonds to people, communities, and professional care rather than replacing them. The finding is therefore not that technology can resolve collapse, but that without coherence-centered design, technology risks amplifying precisely the interpretive and behavioral failures that collapse already intensifies. The tool’s role is not to predict, persuade, or replace judgment, but to scaffold coherence so that judgment remains possible when systems, and minds, are under maximum strain.

**Analysis**

Experiences described as “spiritual downloads” or as direct perception of future events are treated here not as anomalous epistemic access, but as phenomenological outcomes of cognitive systems operating near the limits of regulatory capacity. Within enactive and embodied models of cognition, such states arise when affective intensity, attentional contraction, and environmental uncertainty push the system toward the edges of its stable attractor landscape, where meaning feels given rather than constructed (Varela et al., 1991; Thompson, 2007). Under significant stress, neural regulation becomes less effective and perceptual thresholds shift; weak or ambiguous cues gain disproportionate salience, and global pattern-detection increases (Damasio, 1994; McEwen & Morrison, 2013). This produces experiences of sudden insight, coherence, or inevitability, even when the underlying environment remains ambiguous or high-dimensional.

The perceived clustering of global phenomena, economic instability, epidemics, conflict, ecological disruption, is interpreted as a cognitive compression of interacting risks into a simplified narrative. Complex systems under strain often exhibit heightened visibility of structural pressures, and humans respond by collapsing uncertainty into tractable storylines that impose order on perceived instability (Bak, 1996; Suchman, 2007). These narrative condensations reflect genuine structural coupling while simultaneously reducing dimensionality in ways that overstate coherence or inevitability.

This compression is shaped by the interplay of convergence, synchronicity, and prophetic framing, which together form a single interpretive loop. Convergence refers to the experience of multiple events seeming to “point” in the same direction; in systems terms, this reflects recognition of shared constraints rather than the presence of a predetermined trajectory (Nicolis & Prigogine, 1977; Haken, 1983). Synchronicity amplifies this perception by treating coincidental events as confirmatory signals, a response heightened when internal noise rises or interpretive thresholds fall. Prophetic framing then discretizes continuous processes into symbolically charged temporal phases, “signs,” “turning points,” “thresholds”, which sharpen perceived transitions and lend them moral significance (Ricoeur, 1984; Löwith, 1949). Taken together, these mechanisms create a sense of directional inevitability even when the underlying dynamics remain contingent.

Bifurcation theory provides a disciplined way to interpret these experiences. Near bifurcation points, systems often exhibit multistability, in which multiple futures coexist as viable trajectories until small perturbations determine which becomes dominant (Strogatz, 1994; Kelso, 1995). Reports of “two frequencies” or “split timelines” correspond to the phenomenology of navigating such regions: the agent becomes aware, pre-reflectively, of heightened sensitivity and the presence of competing attractors. Similarly, imagery of rupture or sudden transformation aligns with well-known features of systems approaching qualitative reorganization, where latent tensions accumulate until structural adjustment occurs (Haken, 1983; Friston, 2010).

Within this context, the still point becomes a critical regulatory construct. It refers to a low-variance state in which internal dynamics stabilize sufficiently to support reliable perception and choice. Empirically, emotional regulation reduces variability in neural signaling and decreases error amplification, enhancing cognitive flexibility under pressure (Damasio, 1994; McEwen & Morrison, 2013). Conceptually, the still point marks the difference between navigating a bifurcation and being captured by it: it restores temporal depth, reduces reactivity, and supports deliberative action.

The Flower Processor hypothesis extends this logic to hybrid human–machine systems by noting that resonant and reservoir-like computational substrates, including oscillatory media, recurrent dynamics, and physically grounded information processing, are acutely sensitive to boundary conditions (Maass et al., 2002; Tanaka et al., 2019). In such systems, human coherence functions as a stabilizing constraint, improving reliability and preventing undesirable forms of attractor capture. Emotional intelligence thus becomes a functional control variable rather than a supplementary psychological trait, fully aligned with human-centered AI principles emphasizing reliability, contestability, and non-coercive alignment (Russell, 2019; Shneiderman, 2020; Gabriel, 2020).

Taken together, this analysis reframes eschatological motifs not as forecasts of collapse or transcendence, but as structurally informative phenomenology that emerges when agents confront systemic fragility and reduced interpretive capacity. Experiences of downloads, signs, splits, and stillness correspond to identifiable cognitive and dynamical processes: information compression, salience amplification, multistability, and regulation near criticality. The Flower Processor therefore does not validate deterministic predictions; it offers a coherence-centered model for understanding how humans interpret instability, and how tools grounded in regulation and constraint can support adaptive agency rather than apocalyptic inference.

Across disciplinary domains, a coherent pattern emerges: many limitations attributed to human cognition, artificial intelligence, and cultural meaning-making arise from overly narrow models of intelligence and systemic conditions of compression rather than from intrinsic failures of agents or technologies.

First, contemporary AI reveals the constraints of treating intelligence as disembodied optimization. Digital, large-scale statistical systems achieve impressive benchmark performance yet remain brittle, opaque, energetically unsustainable, and persistently misaligned with human norms. These limitations reflect deeper conceptual assumptions that abstract cognition away from its physical, biological, and relational grounding (Russell, 2019; Amodei et al., 2016; Floridi & Cowls, 2019).

Second, developments in neuromorphic, analog, photonic, and dynamical computing demonstrate that intelligent behavior can arise from the intrinsic dynamics of physical systems, not only from symbolic manipulation. These findings align with longstanding insights from cybernetics and nonequilibrium thermodynamics: adaptive systems maintain coherence by negotiating constraints and energy flows, not by maximizing objective functions in isolation (Wiener, 1948; Haken, 1983; Nicolis & Prigogine, 1977).

Third, embodied and enactive cognitive science shows that affect, sensorimotor coupling, and social scaffolding are constitutive of cognition, not peripheral influences. Stress physiology confirms that dysregulation amplifies noise, degrades executive function, and increases susceptibility to maladaptive attractors, precisely when complexity intensifies (Damasio, 1994; McEwen & Morrison, 2013). These findings reposition regulation as a control variable central to stability in both biological and artificial systems.

Fourth, cultural responses to AI increasingly mirror eschatological structures, expressing anxiety about thresholds beyond which human agency may erode. Historical and anthropological scholarship shows such grammars recur when societies confront systems whose scale and speed exceed inherited interpretive frameworks (Jaspers, 1953; Koselleck, 2004). This reveals not prophecy but cognitive and cultural attempts to manage systemic unpredictability.

Finally, symbolic systems, religious, mythic, numerological, apocalyptic, can be understood as coordination technologies that stabilize meaning under compression. Yet when complexity collapses into rigid binaries, these systems shift from mediation to coercion. Research shows that such reductions correlate with authoritarian drift, gendered hierarchy, and heightened intergroup conflict (Atran & Ginges, 2012; Wood & Eagly, 2002). Their failures reflect not moral deficiency but loss of relational bandwidth and over-constrained interpretive space.

Across these domains, the core finding is consistent: systems, biological, social, or artificial, become fragile when coherence degrades and relational mediation collapses. Conversely, when regulation, constraint, and relational scaffolding are strengthened, trajectories become more steerable, learning becomes more stable, and agency becomes more resilient.

The Flower Processor framework emerges from this synthesis as a coherence-centered model of systemic stability. It unifies insights from dynamical systems, embodied cognition, AI ethics, and cultural analysis to show how humans interpret instability, why fragility produces narrative compression, and how tools grounded in regulation-first, relationally aware design can help navigate bifurcation without amplifying harm.

**Conclusion**

This paper set out to develop and critically examine the Flower Processor not as a speculative device, esoteric system, or prophetic schema, but as a coherence-centered conceptual framework with practical implications for the design of emotional intelligence tools suited to environments characterized by uncertainty, complexity, and human vulnerability. Rather than treating experiential or symbolic narratives as privileged epistemic sources or proposing a realizable hardware architecture, the paper used the Flower Processor as a structured lens through which to translate phenomenology, symbolic reasoning, and eschatological motifs into the empirically grounded vocabularies of nonlinear dynamics, embodied cognition, and human-centered systems theory. The primary aim was pragmatic and ethical: to clarify how emotional and cognitive coherence can be operationalized as a stabilizing capacity exactly when human judgment is most error-prone.

Throughout the analysis, collapse was reframed not as ontological annihilation but as systemic condensation and compression, a phase transition in which degrees of freedom contract, feedback loops shorten, and latent constraints become increasingly salient. This interpretation proved instructive for design considerations. It showed that contexts where individuals experience heightened urgency, fear, or a sense of inevitability, conditions often encoded in symbolic or eschatological language, are also those in which cognitive variance increases and regulatory capacities degrade. Experiences described as “shadows,” “presences,” or impending inevitabilities were interpreted not as evidence of hidden agents, but as cognitive responses to heightened system legibility combined with the nonlinear limits of human inference. For an emotional intelligence tool, the design implication is direct: the relevant interventions should focus not on interpretive guidance or reassurance, but on noise reduction, autonomic regulation, and the expansion of temporal perspective at the point of choice.

Central to translating the framework into an actionable design orientation is the concept of the still point, defined operationally as a low-variance regulatory state that precedes reliable cognition and goal-directed action. Affective neuroscience, stress physiology, and systems theory converge on the finding that regulation is a control parameter, not a secondary psychological state. In practical terms, an emotional intelligence tool should prioritize regulation-first interventions, such as sensory modulation, paced breathing, attentional anchoring, and somatic grounding, before prompting reflection, reappraisal, or values-based decision-making. Under this approach, emotional intelligence is not primarily introspective insight but the capacity to stabilize internal dynamics sufficiently to distinguish structural pressures from misattributed agency in bifurcation-like contexts.

The framework further showed that bifurcation dynamics, formally understood as multistability under constraint, map closely onto clinically recognized high-risk states in mental health, neurodivergence, and addiction. Fatigue, craving, shame, conflict, and overload represent predictable dynamical regions in which small perturbations can divert trajectories. The ethical implication for an emotional intelligence tool is that support should not aim to eliminate or moralize these states; rather, it should reduce error variance and preserve agency when the system’s stability margins are thinnest. The envisioned tool therefore functions as a coherence scaffold, interrupting involuntary entrainment, widening temporal horizons, and enabling incremental, values-consistent actions without demanding certainty or suppressing autonomy.

By critically examining symbolic systems, sacred geometry, numerology, eschatological narratives, triadic forms, the paper clarified why humans rely on metaphor and myth under compression. These systems were interpreted not as privileged knowledge structures but as cognitive interfaces for reasoning about differentiation, opposition, and relationality when causal complexity exceeds intuitive capacity. For tool design, this insight carries a methodological caution: symbolic coherence can support regulation and sense-making, but when conflated with causal structure, it can amplify determinism, projection, or dependency. An ethically responsible emotional intelligence system must therefore support meaning without exploiting it, offer orientation without substituting for judgment, and reinforce agency rather than narrative inevitability.

The paper also extended the concept of bond from relational psychology to system governance. In human-centered AI, bonds represent the relational and institutional conditions that sustain autonomy and well-being. The emotional intelligence tool described here is thus framed not as a therapist, authority, or oracle, but as a component of a broader ecology of support. Its purpose is to strengthen healthy bonds, to community, to care infrastructure, to one’s own regulatory capacities, rather than replace them. Coherence, forgiveness, and relational ethics are not spiritual augmentations of the tool but functional safeguards against coercion, exploitation, and overfitting in over-constrained socio-technical systems.

In conclusion, the Flower Processor offers not a prediction about future events nor a technological blueprint, but a design orientation for present challenges. It suggests that at moments of bifurcation, whether personal, collective, or socio-technical, the decisive variable is not access to hidden knowledge but the quality of emotional regulation, relational mediation, and ethical constraint. The emotional intelligence tool envisioned here does not promise control over outcomes; rather, it supports the conditions under which adaptive, humane, and minimally harmful choices remain possible. Within this framework, the future is not a fixed trajectory revealed in advance but a continuously emergent field shaped by how systems, and the people within them, regulate, relate, and choose when coherence is most strained and most necessary.
