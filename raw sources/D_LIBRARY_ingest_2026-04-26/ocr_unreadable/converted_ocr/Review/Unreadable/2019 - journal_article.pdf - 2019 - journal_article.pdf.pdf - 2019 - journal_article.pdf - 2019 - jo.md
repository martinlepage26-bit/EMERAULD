Review of: The Data Gaze: Capitalism, Power and Perception
David Beer in his latest offering further unravels the thread started in his previous work
Metric Power (2016). Whilst Metric Power rooted itself in Heidegger’s territory and
was largely concerned with the political ramifications of the push towards quantification
and the exclusion of the unquantifiable, this volume is plunged into the murky waters of
big data. Exploring along what Amoore (2009) calls “lines of sight”: pre-emptive
visualisations of futures unknown. It is within these lines of sight that the data gaze is
deployed, treating what is a visualization as the concrete future, and in turn causing it to
materialise.
The notion of the line implies a rigid space, a finite quantity. What Beer unveils
big data to be, is more akin to non-Euclidian spaces. The data gaze is of an everexpanding fluid nature, never static. For this work Beer stepped on the shoulders of
Foucault’s (1963/2003) Birth of the Clinic, and utilised the clinical gaze as a conceptual
framework, whilst at the same time demonstrating a uniqueness of approach that tends
to movement, rather than the fixedness inherent in the clinical gaze. If the clinical gaze
regarded its subject from a static vantage point, then the data gaze seeps, onto, into and
over its subjects from every possible angle. In perfect alignment with both his theme
and Foucault, Beer breaks free from the academic interview-findings-analysis triptych
and boldly does as the data gaze does. Everything is data which one can draw from, and
so Beer draws from an astounding array of sources that simply float around us:

marketing materials, press interviews, audio-visual snippets, all are drafted in and
interpreted.
The first chapter functions as an overview situating us in the frame of cognitive /
platform capitalism, starting with the figure of Derrida’s archon: the ultimate
gatekeeper, the lord of the archive. An ideal parallel as the mediation towards what we
can access, how is it accessed and how is it interrogated, is what big data hinges on.
Data is never raw, in its interpretation is where its transformative power lies. A
fundamental point is brought before the reader: There is a difference in what big data
can do on its own accord, and what we are led to believe it does: What big data strives
to be seen as doing, the data imaginary. The key concept that occupies most of Beer’s
offering is how this data imaginary increasingly draws us into what is referred to as a
data rationality: To think only within the bounds of data.
After laying down the initial groundwork, focus shifts towards the notions of
accessibility and speed. The dominant motto being that anyone can utilise the data to
peer into “anything-anytime-anywhere”. Big data is seen as having the ability to tap into
the hidden potentiality of things, as it is bestowed with a prophetic vision of the future.
This almost metaphysical property leads to a sense of urgency. If you are a business
user, you cannot afford to be blind to the future. If you don’t use the prosthetic vision
afforded by the data gaze others will leave you behind. In short, predictions are
presented as error-free, accurate insights. Thus, to employ the data gaze becomes an
obligation and not an option. This obligation is presented under a cloak of naturalness
and ease. Turning to Pasquale (2015) and the notion of the Black Box Society, Beer
suggests that the algorithm is vested with anthropomorphic features. All the inhuman
complexity that happens inside the box is dealt with in ways that are of no concern to
the end user. The algorithm is smart, it learns, it will do all the thinking. What we

interact with is a friend, an unobtrusive natural aide, someone easy to talk to, offering us
error-free insights, that would otherwise lay outside our reach.
The faith ingrained in the algorithmic imaginary results in the future being
steered towards what the data envisages it to be. This results in the cyclical outcome of
the faith in the data’s prediction being self-validated, thus propelling a further expansion
of what Beer calls the data frontiers: the limits of datafication. More and more of
everything is looked upon as data, with the increase in data volume not making the gaze
prone to errors or dampening speed. Everything is analysed not merely quickly but at
real-time. It is no longer a matter of slowing down the cosmos in order to elaborate on
it. The data gaze allows us to be up to speed, making reflection or interpretation
obsolete. This is an ongoing production with last moment choices being constantly
made, each choice producing a different iteration of the future.
Progressing his argument, Beer moves to what is the most important part of his
work. He opens up the black box and looks past the veneer of accessibility and ease.
The data gaze now emerges as impenetrably technical. First, the infrastructure the data
gaze is built on is unpacked. Following suit with the ever expansion narrative
encountered in the data imaginary, the infrastructure itself is fluid, scalable. Not an
issue of hardware but software, whose evolvement and spinoffs are never at rest. Beer
does a brilliant task of looking at the software components that make data work,
focusing on the Hadoop software ecosystem and its many bolt-ons. Hadoop, which
serves as Beer’s main point of scrutiny, is one of the most prominent cogs of the big
data universe, used by household technology names ranging from Facebook and Twitter
on to Spotify. The modular ecosystem and the nature of a significant amount of its
inhabitants go from being arcane acronyms for the uninitiated, (i.e. PIG, YARN, or
SPARK), through an analysis of their functions. What the reader emerges with is an

understanding of what each component is geared towards, what does it do, and how
does this impact the very nature of the gaze. Most important aspects that surface being
that the gaze watches over itself, and evaluation moving from post-transaction to pretransaction. Data sanitisation is central, as the self-learning algorithm refines and
prepares the data for processing. There is no need to have an objective, piles of
unorganized data are troweled through and what is important will be identified by the
system.
All this talk of data cleaning, importance selection, constant updating and
fluidness, subtly begin pointing towards the elephant in the room: criteria. What data is
deemed dirty and is in need of cleaning? What is left out? How is one set of data more
important than another? Beer never engages directly with answering all these questions,
yet he takes up the far more rigorous task of opening them up so clearly and presenting
all the actors involved so meticulously, that the answers grow in the reader.
An important part that is brought into focus is the issue of licensing. Hadoop is
revealed as coming from an open source community driven model, yet whose key parts
fall under a closed license, copyrighted by the Apache Software Foundation. Thus,
making the Apache Foundation dictate the workings of the gaze. So, what we have
before us is a project driven by collaboration yet governed in a very tight and
centralised manner. There is a very clear chain of command behind Hadoop’s
development. A ladder starting at the user level and climbing up to contributor,
committer, release manager, with the top rug occupied by the project management
committee. An invitation-only committee fully controlled by Apache.
Somewhere under all this talk of data, and corporations Beer uncovers the
human element inside the box. The roles of the data analyst and the data engineer come
to the fore in the closing chapter of this volume. Starting with an interesting story

situating the birth of the data gaze in an imaginary company created by D. R. Howe in
the early 1980s, we see that from its conception the whole system was bifurcated in two
distinct parts. That of data itself and that of the data systems. The human actor behind
data systems is the data engineer, who essentially manages the infrastructure for the
facilitation of needed data transactions; a regulator of flow might be a way to think
about the role. The one responsible for transporting the data under the analyst’s eyes. As
the data gaze is always fixed in the future so is the infrastructure. Always evolving to
support the future. Knowing where to look rather than knowing what to do is the mark
of the data engineer.
The data itself, on the other hand, exists independently of the transactions that it
is taken through, and is the domain of the data analyst. The analyst facilitated by the
engineer emerges as a post-human hybrid: “When I look at the numbers what I see are
stories” (p.106). The mark of expertise being the level one can interact with raw data
and pick out “interestingness”. The data analyst is the one who holds sway over what is
interesting and what not, cleaning the data and translating it, making it fit for utility.
What both these roles have in common is that they are products of an
organisation wide restructuring. As the data gaze becomes engrained in the organisation
there is a certain reorientation taking place. A reorientation which is part technical
(catering to the new skills in demand), but most importantly what gets reoriented is the
outlook. Data is seen as the source of truth, the patterns encountered are treated as
models, they are not data snapshots but become seen as “fully descriptive” of the data
itself.
To recapitulate, Beer in this volume has taken the reader through a three-step
process. Firstly, establishing the concept of the data imaginary and the tremendous sway
it has over us. A dreamwork, setting an unreachable ideal in whose endless pursuit it is

engaged, feeding its own expansion. Then Beer moves on in an analysis of the
mechanisms that underpin the data gaze, ultimately leading us back up to its true
archons; those who interpret the data and most importantly that which shapes the
criteria for that interpretation to take place. As frequently mentioned by Beer the gaze
watches itself, so even the criteria under which it operates are not beyond its reach nor
its mutative powers.
The Data Gaze emerges as an astute fact-based analytical work, through which
Beer carves a path for sociology to peer through the technical umbra of Big Data. A feat
of Herculean scale, which Beer completes triumphantly given that technical jargon is
unpacked yet not simplified. What some readers might comment on is that the Data
Gaze is too quiet a work, it does not shout enough. This seems to be a conscious choice
made by Beer and one that accounts for the fact that he has delivered us a monolithic
foundational work that elegantly hangs in the balance. It is for the reader to tip the
balance armed with what Beer has meticulously unpacked for us. There is an almost
inescapable clause that plagues all efforts of sociology when it engages with
technology. A flag branded with the slogan of the technological stemming from the
social, is almost inescapably waved. What makes Beer’s attempt stand out is that it
abstains from waving any sort of flags. The task of this volume is for us to know the
workings of big data, and also to be reminded that the technological also clearly aims to
shape the social. Highly recommended for readers of varied backgrounds, blazing a
channel of communication between scholars of technology and sociology.
