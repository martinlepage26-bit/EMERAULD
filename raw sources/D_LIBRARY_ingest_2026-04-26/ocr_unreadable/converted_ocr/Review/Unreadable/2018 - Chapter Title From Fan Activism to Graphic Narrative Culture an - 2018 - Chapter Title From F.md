From Fan Activism to Graphic Narrative
Culture and Race in Gene Luen Yang’s Avatar:
The Last Airbender—The Promise
Tim Gruenewald

From 2005 to 2008, three seasons of the animated television series Avatar:
The Last Airbender (henceforth Avatar) were first broadcast on Nickelodeon.
Although the show premiered on a children’s channel, its reach extended far
beyond that demographic. It became a global hit and spawned a vast fandom
in the United States and beyond. Among the fans were graphic novelists Gene
Luen Yang and Derek Kirk Kim. Following the commercial and critical failure
of M. Night Shyamalan’s 2010 live-action film adaptation The Last Airbender
(henceforth Airbender), Yang was hired to write a graphic novel trilogy, which
was published by Dark Horse Comics in 2012 under the title Avatar: The Last
Airbender—The Promise (henceforth Promise). This trilogy marks an unexpected
departure from Yang’s previous, highly acclaimed publications.1 In contrast to
his graphic novels American Born Chinese and Level Up, which are original and
autobiographically inspired stories, Promise is part of a vast commercial media
franchise, and the narrative is set in the fictional fantasy world of the original
television series. Yang’s contract work was one component for Nickelodeon’s
strategy to revive the Avatar franchise, which had been one of its most valuable
properties before the fans soundly rejected Airbender in the blogosphere and
at the box office. Yang’s trilogy bridges the narrative gap between the end of
Avatar and the beginning of a new twelve-episode television miniseries Avatar:
The Legend of Korra (henceforth Korra), which originally aired from April 14
until June 23, 2012.2 Yang’s previous work on Asian American coming-of-age
narratives made him an unlikely choice for Nickelodeon. It is even more surprising that he was chosen given the fact that he played a prominent role in
protesting the casting decisions for Shyamalan’s adaptation as he joined other
activists in calling for the film’s boycott in 2008.

The casting controversy exploded in late 2008 after the announcement that
Avatar’s three central Airbender characters, Aang, Sokka, and Katara, would
all be cast with white actors. Using white actors to play Asian characters in
American film and performing arts has a long and troubled history ranging from
Broken Blossoms (1919) to Breakfast at Tiﬀany’s (1961) to the Broadway production of Miss Saigon in 1990 and, more recently, to Cloud Atlas (2012). Airbender’s
casting controversy provides a particularly instructive case study of the importance of race and ethnicity in the production and reception of popular film and
graphic narrative in the contemporary United States because fans extensively
debated this controversy in online forums. Moreover, Yang’s intervention into
the casting debate, which most prominently occurred in the form of a graphic
short story, provides an insightful link between his earlier work and Promise.
Yang, a second-generation Chinese American himself, reflects in the webcomic
on his personal investment in Asian American identity formation issues that
play out in the triangle of American racial categories, stereotypes of Asians, and
Asian culture, all of which he had already explored in American Born Chinese.
Yang’s and Kim’s critiques of the casting politics especially revealed the significance of the Avatar television series in providing young Asian Americans with
positive points of identification in mass media narratives and in contributing
to Asian American identity formation as it was the first American television hit
series to be set in an imagined Asian universe featuring apparently Asian characters. In Avatar the characters do not have Asian nationality, names, heritage,
or ancestry, and they are not racially marked as Asian. Hence, the viewer reads
their Asianness into them because of the contextual Asian cultural references
such as costume and architecture. In response to Shyamalan’s controversial
casting decision, Yang and Kim joined a fan protest, which also drew on essentializing notions of “Asia” and “Asian culture.” The fundamental argument of the
online fan intervention was that a fictional fantasy world created from a broad
range of Asian cultures should be inhabited by actors of Asian descent.
The protesters’ logic moves discussions about race from culture to biology
as it demands an imagined “Asian” culture to be represented by characters with
specific physical attributes conforming to the imagined racial category “Asian.”
In the case of Avatar, “Asian” culture is simply an essentialized abstraction
because the series’ cultural allusions range from Tibet to the Arctic to Japan
and include countless cultures from this geographic expanse. The demand that
Asian-looking actors be cast in Airbender is equally essentializing. What does this
request imply in a case where the cultural references are so broad and varied?

Should the actors look Indian, Inuit, Japanese, Korean and what would that
mean specifically? The logic of the demand is the flipside of cultural racism
in which cultural essentialism has replaced biological racism. Critics such as
Henry A. Giroux and Paul Gilroy have developed theories of cultural racism
in Europe to analyze and confront a “new” racism that had replaced the “old”
racism based on biological definitions of race, which had become taboo after the
Holocaust. Giroux describes the basic premise of this cultural racism:
As old borders and zones of cultural difference become more porous or even
collapse, questions of culture increasingly become interlaced with the issues
of power, representation and identity. Dominant cultural traditions once
self-confidently secure in the modernist discourse of progress, universalism and objectivism are now interrogated as ideological beachheads used to
police and contain subordinate groups, oppositional discourses and dissenting social movements. (1)

When culture becomes the basis for singling out minorities and when notions of
culture become fixed and identified with specific ethnic groups, cultural racism
may also discriminate against “physical appearance or ancestry but does not
require any form of biological determinism” (Modood 12). The shift towards
cultural racism took place in Europe as a response to growing Muslim immigrant communities (Bralo and Morrison 117) and in the United States during
the Reagan/Bush years as a reaction to the Civil Rights era (Giroux 9–11).
It has accelerated in the West during the War on Terror in the aftermath of the
September 11 terrorist attacks (El-Tayeb 3). Modood has argued that in the
United Kingdom Asians, in contrast to black Caribbean groups, are primarily
subject to cultural racism “as the racialized images of Asians [are] not so extensively linked to physical appearance. [Racism] very soon appeals to cultural
motifs such as language, religion, family structures, exotic dress, cuisine, and
art forms” (7). If one looks at the critical discourse about Asian American
identity in the United States regarding the term “model minority,” for example,
it appears that Asian Americans are also subject to cultural racism. Chih-Chieh
Chou argues:
Another significant implication of the notion of model minority is that
“race” is replaced by “cultural difference.” That is, when cultural racism
replaces biological racism, race is subsumed into a pure realm of cultural
difference, and race as a sociohistorical category becomes obscured. When
cultural differences are treated as fixed objects, however, race is reduced to
essentialized cultural differences. (227)

Paradoxically, the anti-racist fan protest movement against the casting for
Airbender depended precisely on attaching simplified and problematic notions
of “Asian culture” to the category “Asian” in a way that is specific to US racial discourse. In fact, the fan activists relied on the same problematic amalgamation of
race with culture that Yang had so skillfully critiqued in American Born Chinese,
for example, by means of the hyperbolic “Chinaman” character Chin-kee which
parodied the combination of discriminatory imagination of phenotype with
grotesquely exaggerated and imagined cultural practices. In the following, I will
explore this conundrum in greater detail before analyzing how Yang, surprisingly, managed to investigate the relationship between culture, ethnicity, and
race in the Promise trilogy.

Resisting “Racebending” through Fan Activism
Avatar deviates from Nickelodeon’s usual programming primarily through its
three season-long story arch, complex characterization, and character development, as well as its exploration of political themes such as nationalism, war,
empire, colonization, and even genocide. The television series takes place in a
world consisting of four nations named after and associated with one of the four
elements water, fire, earth, and air. Select members of each nation are able to
master a martial arts technique called bending that allows them to manipulate
the element of their respective nation. The Avatar is an incarnate spirit that has
the potential to master the manipulation of all four elements. He is expected to
use his unique ability to balance the powers and create harmony among the four
nations. The original series follows the quest of the Avatar Aang and his friends
to end a global conflict called the Hundred Year War.
Avatar was an exceptionally successful animated television series. During
its initial run, it was among the channel’s most popular shows, consistently
ranking among the top ten for its target group. It also became a global phenomenon as it was syndicated to 105 countries and was the channel’s top show in
several markets in Europe, Asia, and South America.3 The popularity of the show
generated a vast, primarily online-based fan community, including numerous
dedicated websites, a strong presence on leading social websites, large amounts
of narrative and visual fan art, and a wiki that currently contains over 10,000
pages exploring all aspects of the franchise’s fictional universe.4 Although the
series was originally targeted at viewers aged six to eleven years, the show
managed to reach far beyond this demographic to include adults such as Yang

and Kim with whom Yang had co-authored the graphic narrative collection The

Following the conclusion of the original television show in 2008, much of
the fan community’s interest was directed towards a live action film adaptation
that Paramount announced in 2007. When the casting decision of the four main
characters was publicized in December of 2008, the fan community reacted with
outrage and protest. The fans had imagined the characters to be Asian since the
show’s fictional world appropriates numerous elements from various Asian and
Inuit cultures, yet the studio cast four white actors for the roles (Lopez 1). The
frustrated fans quickly organized in grassroots movements such as “Saving the
World with Postage,” which inundated Paramount with protest mail proclaiming, “Aang can stay Asian and still save the world” (“Timeline”). Two leaders
of the protest movement eventually founded the advocacy group and accompanying website Racebending by playfully borrowing the concept of manipulating elements (bending) from the Avatar universe. Thus activists “mark their
fandom and attachment to the world of the franchise, even as they use the same
term to articulate their frustration with an industry where roles are systematically taken from Asian Americans and given to white actors” (Lopez 3).6 Yang
followed this subversive dual strategy when he entered the discussion with his
webcomics (“The Last Airbender Movie”), paying visual homage to the Avatar
franchise while at the same time attacking the film industry for destroying his
own imagination of the Avatar fantasy world as Asian.
During the early stages of the fan protest movement and on the day of Barack
Obama’s inauguration, Kim posted on his blog an extensive entry titled “New
Day in Politics, Same Old Racist World on the Silver Screen.” After pronouncing
his deep admiration for Avatar, calling it “the greatest, most ambitious animated
action adventure TV series ever hatched in the U.S.,” Kim formulates his main
complaint:
It’s wholly and inarguably built around Asian (and Inuit) culture. Everything
from the costume designs, to the written language, to the landscapes,
to martial arts, to philosophy, to spirituality, to eating utensils!—it’s all an
evocative, but thinly veiled, re-imagining of ancient Asia . . . It would take a
willful disregard of the show’s intentions and origins to think this wouldn’t
extend to the race of the characters as well. (“New Day in Politics”)

In the same post, Kim quoted Yang, commenting on the film’s casting: “It’s like
a white Asian fetishist’s wet dream. All the Asian culture they want, without any
of the Asian people.” Kim continued to elaborate on Hollywood’s discriminatory

casting in the tradition of early-twentieth-century vaudeville and Hollywood’s
practice of using white actors to portray Asian characters.
As Krystyn R. Moon explains, “Yellowface, a term used primarily during the
twentieth century to describe the ways in which white actors portrayed Asians,
manifested degrading images of Chinese immigrants on stage—images that
were also appearing in contemporary political cartoons and magazine covers”
(6). The primary purpose of yellowface was to “mark the Chinese body as inferior
and foreign” (Moon 6).7 This mechanism does not apply to Airbender because
the white actors did not play Asian characters. Following the visual style of
manga and anime, Avatar characters could be described according to the concept
of mukokuseki, which could be translated as “stateless” or “culturally odorless”
(Iwabuchi 455). While non-Japanese audiences could understand the physical
attributes of manga and anime characters to be Caucasian (Bryce et al.), in fact
their race is not determined. However, through the act of casting white actors in
Airbender, the characters de facto take on the race of those actors. This casting
decision is, however, reflective of Kent A. Ono and Vincent N. Pham’s explanation for the popularity of yellowface:
White European audiences wanted to see Orientalism without seeing Asian
and Asian American people and their bodies. Furthermore, it reminds us
that, despite systematically excluding Asians and Asian Americans—from
the screen, the stage, and even migration and citizenship—US Americans
demonstrated enough interest, curiosity, and intrigue about them to construct a complex representational edifice to include them visually and narratively but to exclude them physically. (50)

Kim and Yang sensed that the audience’s desire to consume Asian culture while
preferring to exclude the Asian body from the performance applied also in this
twenty-first-century version of yellowface. Yang would further explore this
viewpoint in his next artistic contribution to the online fan protest.

Over one year after his first intervention and one month before the opening
of the film, Yang posted a short comic on his website which humorously summarized the position of the protest movement (Figure 7). Yang appears in the
webcomic as a character who expresses his admiration of Avatar: “Here was an
American cartoon with multi-faceted characters and addictive plot lines, all
set in a beautifully constructed Asian fantasy world” (panel 2). Moreover, his
fandom is expressed through imitation of the Avatar visual style (panels 2, 6,
and 8). Once again, Yang emphasizes the show’s “deep respect for and knowledge
of Asian cultures,” illustrating his point through a drawing of himself reveling in
an apparent cultural reference to the show (panel 3). After revealing the production company’s racial bias by quoting from the casting call (panel 7), Yang formulates his central critique in the ninth panel. Interestingly, this panel visually
stands out from the rest of the webcomic as it contains no image, only typescript, which is inverted white on black for emphasis.8 The text communicates
the racist logic underlying the casting call and the studio’s decision to cast white
actors: “Asian-American faces are simply inadequate for American audiences,
even in a movie that so obviously celebrates our cultural heritage” (panel 9). The
rejection of Asian actors in lead roles by the American audience clearly seems to
be at the core of what ignited the protest movement, yet this logic is rarely made
explicit in the fan protest discourse. Instead, the fans emphasized a necessary
connection between an Asian-like world inhabited by Asian-looking actors and
thus presupposed a fixed and essential connection between culture and biological race. This argument is aimed primarily at the director and the producers who
make casting decisions.
The addressee of Yang’s argument, in contrast, is much broader. While it
could be understood as the producer’s questionable assumption about Asian
American actors’ lacking marketability, it can also be read as an accusation of
the American audience for rejecting Asian American actors at the box office,
which by extension would widen the focus of the critique from Hollywood to
encompass US society at large. If one looks at the economics of a tent-pole
summer blockbuster such as Airbender, the latter possibility appears plausible. Airbender cost $150 million to produce and had a marketing budget of
$130 million (Eller). A survey of recent box office results reveals that no film
featuring an Asian or Asian American star has approached the level of revenue
required to amortize such an investment during the past ten years. Probably
the only film with an Asian lead actor to ever break the $300 million box office
barrier was Rush Hour 2 (2001) featuring Jackie Chan at $347 million. However,

Ultimately, one can only speculate whether the track record of Asian and
Asian American stars at the American box office is due to a lack of audience
acceptance or whether it is simply a consequence of the lack of films starring
Asian and Asian American actors. In any case, it is plausible that a studio executive responsible for recouping investments at the level of Airbender would be
tempted to include race in the casting decision for the lead stars because past
performance is an important metric when estimating future return on investment. Yang hits a nerve with his webcomic by pointing at audience culpability,
a responsibility that the larger protest movement prefers to ignore. It certainly
is more convenient to single out the director or the studio than to formulate a
critique that would implicate the broader society. This blind spot of Airbender’s
critics could be due to the ethnic composition of the protest movement.
According to a survey from 2009, the supporters of the protest movement
were 48.5% white and only 24.2% Asian, and thus accusing the mainstream US
audience of racism could be a divisive issue for the fan community itself; white
fans may be less inclined to fault a predominantly white audience for its relative
rejection of Asian actors.10
In contrast to Yang’s broader socio-political argument lamenting the popular
rejection of Asian performers, the fandom’s “Asian culture” argument is primarily text immanent, as it demands “authenticity” and “consistency” from
Airbender’s fictional fantasy universe. While it is intuitively reasonable to
demand “Asian-looking” actors in a world that appears to be “Asian,” this line
of argument opens up several conceptual problems at closer inspection. One is
evident in Kim’s blog post where he writes that the Avatar universe is “built
around Asian (and Inuit) culture.” He relegates Inuit culture to secondary status
by putting it in brackets and thus inadvertently reveals that claiming Avatar
as “Asian” is an essentializing move that can easily marginalize peripheral
cultures such as the Inuit. Furthermore, the use of the term “Asian” itself is
problematic in the fans’ discourse. The work of Lopez, based on an eighteenmonth ethnography of the protest movement, confirms my impression that
the show’s Asian references were central to the fans’ argumentation (5). She
examines how fans used ethnographic photographs and other visual evidence
to prove that the characters in Avatar were Asian despite the racial ambiguity of

Given the diversity of cultures subsumed under the category Asian, any unifying
notions of “Asian culture” are destined to be lacking.
In the same way, it seems meaningless to classify the breadth of Avatar’s
references, or rather appropriations, simply as “Asian” when it includes Inuit
clothing, Japanese anime, Chinese martial arts, yoga practice, the Dalai Lama,
and anything in between. It might be more appropriate to categorize Avatar as
Asian American since in many areas such as character, narrative, dialogue, and
ideology the influences and references are not surprisingly found in American
or Western cultural history. However, the term Asian American would be a no
more stable or productive category. As Kandice Chuh has argued, a unifying
understanding of Asian American does not effectively support political arguments against the kind of institutional discrimination such as the one underlying the Airbender casting decision: “the political may be seen to be animated by
difference, not identity. Dissent, as difficult as it may be to confront, facilitates
critical unification . . .” (147). Chuh argues in favor of embracing the diversity
of the “Asian American” experience. If difference and dissent are negotiated
openly, dispute can paradoxically strengthen Asian American unity and enliven
its role in American political discourse. By contrast, conjuring up homogenous
and essentialized notions of “Asian” or “Asian American” and demanding a corresponding racialized representation perpetuates the same racial logic that led
to the domination of whiteness in Hollywood in the first place.11 Such a limiting
discourse also does not do justice to Avatar’s playful appropriation of elements
Asian and Western, which Yang carries on in Promise.
