Research statement

Martin Lepage, Ph.D.
martinlepage26@me.com

My doctoral project is a study of identity negotiations in the Montreal Pagan community, regarding
gender and sexual orientation, and practices of witchcraft and magic. Much of the research on the
topic suggests that contemporary Paganism and Wicca offer a very inclusive milieu where LGBTQ
Pagans can express the specificity of their identities through collective and individual practices and
performances whose gendered meanings and relations can be modified at will. But I demonstrate,
contrary to popular belief, that these religiosities still reproduce normativity, and that identity and
spirituality are more easily aligned when the individual is detached, at least for a time, from the
community.
To complete this research, I did an extensive exploration of the diverse relationships between
religion and sexuality, as they can be normative, as well as loci of agency. Because certain issues
around identity and power arose in my research, I also worked on discourses around sexuality, the
history of the LGBTQ movement, and different approaches to gender and sex in society. For the
most part, my approach to research is influenced by queer feminist studies, religious studies, cultural
studies, socio-anthropology of religion and sexuality. I have explored and successfully applied the
use of the life narrative methodology with participant observation, in order to contrast different
power dynamics surrounding gender. Moreover, I have articulated a broadly useful queer perspective
allowing the analysis of power relations, within individual and collective experiences, between
identity, authenticity, belonging, and social recognition.
I have served as a teaching assistant in courses about religion and sexuality, homosexuality and
gendered identities, rituals, culture and Western society, as well as Quebec culture, more specifically.
I also have spoken in many courses related to those topics, and my research was published in
different journals, both in French and in English. I have served as a research assistant for projects on
sexuality, ageing and masculinities, on gendered school rites, on youth and sexuality in Quebec, on
marginalized populations in France, and on religious diversity in Montreal. In addition, I have a
previous background in literary studies, and a basic training on the Ethics and Religious Culture
curriculum. I have spent four years doing fieldwork in the Pagan community surrounding Montreal
and some festival locations in Ontario. I have traveled to Toronto, Ottawa, and Quebec City,
interviewed experts on the topics of Paganism, magic and gender from Ontario and Quebec
universities, and created a diversified and comprehensive original dataset from ethnographies and
LGBTQ informants’ interviews.
Although I have been focused on my graduate research for several years, I have been actively
involved in conversations with students and scholars from many universities since 2011; through
organization and participation in our annual Young researchers conference; from Université de
Montréal, with the GRDU; from Université Laval, having been a part of conferences within ACFAS;
and from around the world, contributing to the Religious Studies Project, a project sponsored by the
British Association for the Study of Religion (BASR); to the online network of researchers
specialized in Pagan studies; with the International Society for the Study of Religion (ISSR), and the
American Academy of Religion (AAR). I have also discussed my works, both in French and English,
in many different scientific peer-reviewed conferences. My works were also published after
contributing to such conferences, leading to four book chapters and peer-reviewed articles at Presses

In the next few years, I am interested in developing solid methods on this interdisciplinary approach
to research, particularly combining inductive and deductive methodologies with thematic qualitative
data analysis. I also hope to develop and give courses in sociology and anthropology of gender,
religion, culture, and society in accordance with this scientific expertise. Finally, it is my intention to

softwares like Atlas.ti or NVivo.
I am interested in this position for a variety of reasons: it would allow me to investigate the
relationship between gender, culture and the transmission of social norms around religious and
embodied identities. I would be particularly interested in looking at how collective and individual
gender practices share a common set of ritual tools and performances that help negotiate with
normative tradition, modify it or carry it on. These tools, as physical and aesthetic artifacts, exist
inside and outside religious experience. They permeate every aspect of culture and come into play
with how identities are created and negotiated.
It is my wish to delve deeper into an intersectional exploration of alternative masculinities and sociopolitical movements surrounding hegemonic masculinity. As such, a broader research endeavor
would allow further the conversation between religious studies, queer studies and men’s studies, and
create tighter comparisons between a greater variety of identities occupying different (cisgender,
transgender, queer, gay, bisexual, heterosexual men, racialized men, ageing men, disabled men)
social positionings. More specifically, I would expand my previous research on contemporary Pagan
and LGBTQ communities, which pass on their material heritage in ways that are still very much
unknown. Comparing this phenomenon with the appropriation of First Nations traditions, for
example, which often meet with Pagan or LGBTQ communities and values in the context of sociopolitical activism, would certainly prove to be fruitful. These identities are certainly based on
different ideologies and practices, but, at the same time, profoundly influenced by Western thought
and way of life. As such, they all have developed different branches in the same umbrella of
sociocultural traditions, which people pass on in many alternative and innovative intersecting ways
that remain to be unveiled.
In the next few months, I will most probably be attending the 2017 Eastern International Regional

Martin Lepage’s evidence of teaching effectiveness for the Department of Sociology, Loyola University Chicago

Evidence of teaching effectiveness

Martin Lepage, Ph.D.
martinlepage26@me.com

As you can see from my resume and statement of teaching interests, I haven’t had the opportunity to
teach a course load of my own. Therefore, teaching evaluations to accompany my application are
lacking. Nevertheless, I have given multiple presentations in diverse classes at UQÀM, and
Concordia University. I was also hired by the University of Sudbury to teach the equivalent of four
course loads between September 2016 and May 2017, but they were canceled before they even
started. With that said, I have made it possible for students to comment on my teaching performances,
which is not a negligible evidence of teaching effectiveness.
The majority of comments are positive, stating the liveliness and the dedication I can convey during
a class. As a result, most of the students show great interest towards the content of the courses,
whether about gender studies, religious studies, or methods in sociology and anthropology.
Some of the comments also have to do with my stutter. Students often claim that it doesn’t hinder at
all my performance, and that the class, as well as my capacity to engage with students remain up to
par. In fact, many have mentioned they would be glad to spend a whole semester with me as an
instructor.
Other comments have to do with the quality of my courses’ visual aids, the strictness of my grading,
or the political implications of some theories around gender (when talking about gender
performativity, for example, or male/white privilege). Each of these comments are very constructive,
but they particularly speak of the ways in which students can be positively challenged or stimulated
to shift their own perspective on social issues.
In the next pages, you will find actual comments from Francophone students. Most of them agree
that my teaching allows for a great deal of ease to participate in the learning process.
