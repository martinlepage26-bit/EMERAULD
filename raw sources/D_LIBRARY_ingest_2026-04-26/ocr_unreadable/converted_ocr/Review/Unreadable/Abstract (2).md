(Neo-)Shamanism(s) and Gender: Narratives from the Montreal Pagan Community

Martin Lepage
Université du Québec à Montréal

# Abstract

This article examines how LGBTQ-identifying individuals within the Montreal Pagan community engage with neo-shamanic practices in relation to their gender and sexual identities. Drawing from life narratives and ethnographic fieldwork, the paper investigates how queer practitioners navigate gender norms within contemporary Pagan traditions and negotiate their involvement with practices commonly referred to as shamanic. It addresses the tensions that arise between personal agency, the gender binary embedded in dominant Wiccan frameworks, and the ethical concerns surrounding the appropriation of Indigenous spiritualities. Through two case studies, the article explores how queer Pagans create meaningful spiritual practices while remaining critically aware of broader power dynamics. Rather than aiming to offer a singular model of queer neo-shamanic religiosity, this study highlights the differentiated strategies individuals employ within the sociopolitical and spiritual field of contemporary Paganism.

# Introduction

Contemporary Paganism is characterized by a wide array of ritual practices and theological perspectives, many of which loosely draw from what practitioners identify as shamanism. While anthropological literature often frames shamanism as a category emerging from specific Indigenous and Aboriginal cultural contexts, Pagans—especially in urban environments such as Montreal—tend to adopt eclectic and syncretic spiritual practices. These are often more accurately described as neo-shamanic (Rabinovitch and Lewis 2002, 248–250; Davy 2007, 149; Azria and Hervieu-Léger 2010, 123–124).

This article focuses on how individuals who identify as LGBTQ experience and reconfigure gender and sexuality within the framework of Montreal’s contemporary Pagan scene. The concept of “queer negotiations” is used here to describe the strategies these individuals employ to reconcile their gender and sexual identities with religious practices that are, at times, embedded within binary frameworks or influenced by appropriated spiritual forms. While queer engagement with Paganism has been discussed in previous research (e.g., Griffin 1999; Kraemer 2012), this study contributes to the field by centering life narratives that involve shamanic or neo-shamanic practices.

# Contemporary Paganism and the Question of Shamanism

Shamanism, as used in the Western world, is a broad term encompassing various ecstatic and healing practices originating in distinct cultural contexts, particularly in Siberia, the Americas, and parts of Asia. Many scholars argue that the term itself is a Western construct that can obscure the cultural and political specificity of Indigenous practices (Azria and Hervieu-Léger 2010, 120–123; Rabinovitch and Lewis 2002, 248). While shamanic practices typically involve spirit communication, healing, and trance states, they are fundamentally shaped by the sociopolitical and cosmological frameworks of the cultures in which they are rooted.

In contrast, contemporary Pagans in Montreal engage in spiritual practices that they label shamanic, though these are largely removed from the original contexts from which they draw inspiration. Rather than structured religious systems, these practices tend to reflect a bricolage of elements from different spiritualities. Pagans often place emphasis on personal authority, ecological consciousness, and experiential engagement with the sacred. Within this framework, shamanism is not defined by lineage or cultural inheritance, but by the efficacy of ritual and symbolic coherence for the practitioner (Rabinovitch and Lewis 2002, 186; York 2003, 157).

# Gender, Performativity, and Queer Agency

Building on Judith Butler’s theorization of gender performativity (1990; 1993), this study views gender as enacted through repeated stylized acts that are socially regulated but open to subversion. Butler challenges the naturalization of gender by highlighting how gender norms are inscribed on the body and sustained through repetition. As such, deviation from these norms can expose their constructed nature. In the Pagan context, this means that rituals and gendered symbols do not merely reflect identity—they constitute and constrain it.

In the Montreal Pagan community, the persistence of Wiccan-inspired binary cosmologies creates tensions for queer practitioners. Rituals such as the Great Rite rely on a heterosexual matrix that links the divine to binary gender, naturalizing reproductive dualism and aligning gender with presumed biological sex. LGBTQ practitioners must navigate these symbolic systems, often through re-interpretation, omission, or creative resistance (Charbonneau 2002; Harper 2010; Sloan 2008).

# Intersectionality and the Ethics of Appropriation

Intersectionality offers a crucial analytic for understanding how overlapping systems of power—such as sexism, racism, colonialism, and heteronormativity—shape the lived experiences of LGBTQ Pagans (Crenshaw 2005; Bilge 2009; Brotman and Lévy 2008). Rather than isolating a single axis of identity, intersectionality emphasizes the multiplicity of positionalities that structure both marginalization and privilege.

In the context of neo-shamanic practices, intersectionality helps to illuminate how race and colonial histories are entangled with spiritual appropriation. Many of the rituals and symbolic elements adopted in contemporary Paganism originate in Indigenous contexts. While some of these practices are shared with consent and care, others are recontextualized without acknowledgment or critical reflection (Wallis 2000; 2003). This has led some practitioners to question the legitimacy of using the term “shamanism” altogether when describing practices that emerge from New Age or eclectic Pagan settings.

Yet, the situation is not reducible to a simple binary of appropriation versus authenticity. As Welch (2007) and Driskill et al. (2011) note, Indigenous agency plays a crucial role in shaping how spiritual practices are transmitted, shared, and transformed. Some rituals, such as those performed at festivals like Kaleidoscope Gathering or Wic-Can Fest, involve Indigenous practitioners who share knowledge and practices in a context of mutual respect and intercultural exchange. These occasions complicate rigid critiques of appropriation and point to the importance of context, intent, and relationality.

# Case Studies: Queer Negotiations in Practice

The case studies of Helena and Sam illustrate how queer practitioners engage with neo-shamanic practices through strategies of adaptation, critique, and redefinition. Their narratives reflect differentiated approaches to spirituality that challenge both heteronormative ritual frameworks and the uncritical adoption of Indigenous elements. These accounts do not aim to generalize, but rather to offer grounded insights into how subjectivity, agency, and ethical discernment emerge in lived religious practice.
