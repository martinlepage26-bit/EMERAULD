## **Introduction**

Before the headline, the regulator’s notice, or the court’s written reasons, there is usually a quieter breakdown: a system begins to act on people at scale while the organization lacks the evidentiary infrastructure to notice, contest, or correct what it is doing. Consider the now-canonical hiring example in which a résumé screener learned to penalize the word “women’s” (as in “women’s chess club captain”), reproducing the gendered structure of historical hiring data long before any public scandal forced the failure into view. The governance failure in such cases is temporally prior to the public failure, and that sequencing matters because it locates the primary object of inquiry upstream of “model performance” and downstream of institutional choice. In this sense, AI governance is best understood as a politics of visibility: a set of organizational practices that decide which decision pathways become legible, which harms remain structurally unobservable, and which affected parties retain meaningful capacity to challenge outcomes that classify, score, allocate, and deny. The stakes are already institutionalized in the present regulatory moment, where risk-based governance frameworks are consolidating alongside intensified enforcement, including voluntary but operationally detailed guidance such as NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework and binding regimes such as the EU AI Act. This paper argues that legitimacy under these conditions behaves less like a reputational claim and more like an evidence-backed asset: it is produced, or depleted, by whether an organization can show what happened, why it happened, and what changed after harm.

The research is framed by an explicit governance stance: AI governance is not a technical program adjacent to deployment, but a liability-bearing institutional practice that must be governed with the same rigor as any other enterprise risk. That stance refuses two accommodations that routinely normalize harm. First, it refuses an asymmetric arrangement in which individuals become increasingly legible through data traces while the mechanisms acting on those traces remain structurally opaque - an arrangement treated here not as an implementation detail but as an architecture of power. Second, it refuses the quieter violence of effacement, where training gaps and measurement choices convert statistical absence into practical discrimination. In their place, the paper advances an auditable standard for “maturity” in AI governance: traceability, useful transparency, verifiable equity, contestability, supplier governance, and incident readiness - requirements that must be evidenced in artifacts and practices rather than asserted as principles.

The paper is also written at a time when AI systems are being introduced as cognitive infrastructure inside organizations, mediating analysis, drafting, deliberation, and decision. On this view, deployment is an epistemic act as well as a technical one: a system inherits the questions an organization stopped asking, the perspectives it systematically discounted, and the edge cases it preferred not to surface. What automated outcomes do to others - who becomes legible, who is misrecognized, who is erased - also reveals something about the institutional cultures that produced, procured, and normalized the machinery. The result is a deliberately entangled framing of governance as simultaneously an external accountability problem (who can contest automated outcomes, through what pathways, with what evidence) and an internal reckoning (what automated outcomes disclose about institutional assumptions, silences, and power distributions that governance had not previously been asked to inspect).

A politics-of-visibility frame has a long theoretical genealogy, and naming it precisely is a condition of using its tools correctly. Surveillance studies insists that institutions do not merely observe; they govern through regimes of perception, classification, and exposure, while simultaneously shielding their own mechanisms behind procedural opacity. Browne’s account of racializing surveillance demonstrates how modern surveillance practices are genealogically continuous with racialized regimes of control that rendered Black life hypervisible while concealing institutional authority (Browne, 2015). Mirzoeff complements this analysis by framing visuality as authority-making and countervisuality as a demand for “the right to look” (Mirzoeff, 2011). In governance terms, the right to look becomes a right to inspect and contest the systems that classify, allocate, and deny. Put differently, the political problem is not that institutions see; it is that they see without being equally seeable, producing an asymmetry that converts classification into a one-way exercise of power. Algorithmic systems intensify this condition by making perception programmable and scalable across domains that now share a common technical substrate, from labor recruitment to welfare administration to customer service and biometrics. The implication for governance is specific: visibility cannot remain a metaphor for openness; it must become a capacity to inspect, contest, and force justification, proportionate to the system’s power to impose consequences.

Two recurring governance failures structure the empirical terrain, and defining both before pairing them is necessary to avoid collapsing distinct harm mechanisms. By asymmetric visibility, this paper means the condition in which individuals become hypervisible through data traces while the decision mechanisms acting on those traces remain structurally opaque and hard to challenge with evidence. By invisibilization, this paper means the complement: groups rendered statistically absent or distorted by training and measurement gaps become systemically misrecognized or excluded, with harms that are repeatable and difficult to litigate because they can arise without discriminatory intent. Together, these failures describe a structural double movement: some people are made intensely legible as governable objects, while others are made functionally unreadable as full participants in the institution’s category system. The empirical logic of invisibilization is visible in audits such as Buolamwini and Gebru’s work documenting intersectional performance disparities in commercial gender classification and showing how benchmark composition and evaluation scope can harden into uneven error burdens (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018). The legal-structural point is that disparate impact can emerge through ostensibly neutral proxies and institutional pipelines, meaning harm can be produced even when no actor can plausibly be described as harboring discriminatory intent (Barocas & Selbst, 2016). Consequently, “fairness” cannot be treated as a property of a model abstracted from its deployment context. The fairness-and-abstraction critique shows how modular thinking and abstraction boundaries can render technical interventions ineffective, or even dangerously misguided, once they enter the sociotechnical systems that allocate opportunity and punishment (Selbst et al., 2019). Read together, these literatures reframe bias incidents as governance diagnostics: they disclose where institutional evaluation practices, procurement incentives, and monitoring thresholds failed to make exclusion legible before it became consequential.

The conceptual hinge that follows is a proportionality claim. Reciprocal visibility names the requirement that systems become inspectable in proportion to their power to classify and allocate: if systems see the world, affected publics must be able to see systems enough to secure accountability. The key point is operational rather than rhetorical. Reciprocal visibility is not a demand for full transparency or unrestricted access to model internals; it is a demand for sufficient inspectability to support notice, contestation, and correction. In this paper, reciprocal visibility is specified as three rights held by affected persons and three organizational capacities that institutions must maintain. The rights are (1) notice that AI meaningfully intervened in a consequential decision, (2) meaningful contestation via an accessible pathway that is actionable rather than merely formal, and (3) correction when error is demonstrated. The capacities are (1) proof, understood as the ability to reconstruct what happened and why through durable evidence trails, (2) explanation that is usable for action rather than only technical narration, and (3) rapid remediation, meaning the operational ability to fix systems and document what changed after failure. Reciprocal visibility therefore functions as a governance standard: it translates legitimacy from a claim into an evidence-backed asset.

The framework then extends the same proportionality logic to voice. Voice is treated in two coupled senses: voice as listening (how institutions capture speech, convert it into features, and operationalize it as risk, trust, or eligibility), and voice as institutional speech act (how AI systems speak on behalf of organizations in ways that can bind them in disputes, regulatory proceedings, and courts). These senses are analytically distinct yet operationally entangled because a single conversational interface can misrecognize inputs while also misrepresenting policy. That entanglement motivates reciprocal audibility as the sibling concept to reciprocal visibility: if institutions deploy algorithmic listening, affected people must have enforceable routes to verify what the system heard, how it converted speech into decision-relevant features, and how errors are corrected. Reciprocal audibility matters because conversational interfaces can convert error into silent allocation: a misheard sentence in a screening call, a clinical dictation error, or a customer-service transcript that never faithfully registers the complaint is not merely “quality” drift; it is governance drift, particularly when no durable evidentiary trace exists to make the drift contestable. The empirical anchor for the disparity dimension is established by Koenecke and colleagues (2020), who document large racial gaps in word error rates across major commercial speech recognition systems - gaps large enough to convert transcription error into an inequality channel when deployed in hiring workflows, education platforms, and clinical documentation.

This is where governance machinery becomes the framework’s central sociotechnical concept. Governance machinery is defined as a repeatable operational sequence whose links are empirically inspectable: inventory, controls, assessments, evidence, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting. The concept refuses two common reductions. It refuses a checklist reading where governance becomes box-ticking without causal leverage, and it refuses a principles-only reading where governance becomes value statements without enforceable infrastructure. Instead, governance machinery names the organizational equipment that turns rights (notice, contestation, correction) into capacities (proof, explanation, remediation) at the speed and scale of automated decisions. When that machinery is missing, the institution may still deploy systems, but it cannot credibly claim they are governed, because it cannot show what happened, why it happened, and what changed afterward in a way a third party could audit.

Interface laundering is the framework’s mechanism claim for conversational and voice-facing AI. It is defined as the process through which conversational interfaces shift consequential institutional decisions into informal interaction registers that appear transactional and low-stakes, thereby evading the evidentiary infrastructure accountability requires. The mechanism operates outward-facing when customer chatbots communicate policy without adequate policy-fidelity controls, escalation routes, version management, logging, and remediation timelines; it operates inward-facing when employees route sensitive work through external conversational tools because policy lags practice and sanctioned pathways are absent. In both directions, the mechanism is identical: governance opacity converts technical convenience into accountability liability by preventing reconstruction of what was said, by which version, under what prompts, and with what correction pathway. Importantly, interface laundering is testable, because it points to specific artifacts whose presence or absence can be verified: logs, versioning records, escalation documentation, and correction timelines.

These failures are not best addressed by treating governance as a checklist adjacent to technical work; they require treating accountability as an evidentiary project that must be operationalized end-to-end. Diakopoulos (2015) frames algorithmic accountability in terms of making computational power investigable, emphasizing that without reliable routes to observation, both public oversight and internal self-audit collapse into narrative conflict. Kroll and colleagues (2017) sharpen the institutional stakes by arguing that automated decision systems implicate rule-of-law values and require technical and procedural tools that allow compliance with fairness and due process constraints to be verified rather than asserted. Raji and colleagues (2020) translate this orientation into organizational practice through an end-to-end framework for internal algorithmic auditing that treats documentation, evaluation, and monitoring as durable governance artifacts rather than optional add-ons. The documentation substrate is further specified by proposals such as datasheets for datasets and model cards, which formalize what must be recorded about training data, intended use, limitations, and subgroup performance for systems to remain contestable across time and organizational turnover (Gebru et al., 2018; Mitchell et al., 2019). Contestability becomes operational when affected persons can obtain actionable explanation and feasible recourse; recent work in data protection and AI governance argues that explanation is not merely a technical summary but a governance-facing instrument that supports challenge and correction (e.g., Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021). The integration gap this paper targets is that these streams are often treated as parallel conversations: visibility as critique, fairness as metrics, documentation as best practice, and voice as a separate technical domain. A visibility-plus-audibility framework positions them as interlocking governance requirements that rise and fall together because they are produced by the same machinery.

Against this backdrop, this paper advances two linked contributions, one conceptual and one operational. Conceptually, it reframes AI governance as governance of algorithmic perception and institutional voice, specifying reciprocal visibility and reciprocal audibility as proportionality principles: if systems see and listen in ways that impose consequences, then affected parties and oversight bodies must have enforceable capacity to inspect what the system did, contest how it did it, and trigger correction when it fails. Operationally, it treats governance machinery as the primary unit of analysis: a repeatable sequence of inventory, controls, assessments, evidence integrity, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting that converts values into inspectable decisions.

Methodologically, the paper implements this framework through comparative qualitative “governance reconstruction” across seven heterogeneous cases spanning labor gatekeeping, biometric data provenance, capital allocation, administrative due process, vendor-mediated risk scoring, internal conversational leakage, and customer-facing institutional speech. The cases include the Amazon recruiting tool, Clearview AI, Zillow Offers, the Dutch childcare benefits scandal (Toeslagenaffaire), COMPAS, Samsung’s ChatGPT-related leakage episode, and the Air Canada chatbot dispute, enabling cross-case pattern matching of where governance chains broke and how those breaks propagated into harm. The paper proceeds as follows: the literature review maps the streams and names the integration gap; the conceptual framework specifies reciprocal visibility, reciprocal audibility, governance machinery, and interface laundering; the methods section operationalizes the coding scheme and evaluative indices; the case analyses reconstruct governance failure across the seven dossiers; and the discussion and conclusion synthesize governance requirements for preserving legitimacy as an evidence-backed asset rather than a performance claim.

**From the Politics of Visibility to the Politics of Voice in AI Governance**

The introduction positions AI governance as a politics of visibility: legitimacy tracks whether institutions can render consequential decision systems inspectable, contestable, and correctable, rather than merely accurate. The relevant literature largely agrees with this wager, yet it often distributes the problem across separate conversations: surveillance as critique, accountability as oversight, fairness as metrics, documentation as best practice, and voice as a technical subdomain. Read together, however, these streams describe a single evidentiary problem. Governance rises or collapses with an institution’s capacity to produce durable traces of what a system did, why it did it, and what changed when it harmed.

Surveillance scholarship supplies the foundational move: visibility is not neutral observation, it is power arranged as perception. Browne’s genealogy of racializing surveillance shows how modern institutions repeatedly render Black life hypervisible while protecting institutional mechanisms from reciprocal inspection (Browne, 2015). Hypervisibility functions here as exposure without standing. It multiplies data capture while thinning the routes through which the captured can contest the capture, or even name the mechanism that acted on them. Surveillance becomes, in this sense, a governance template: asymmetries in legibility harden into asymmetries in authority.

Mirzoeff’s account sharpens the hinge between perception and legitimacy by treating visuality as authority-making and countervisuality as a demand for “the right to look” (Mirzoeff, 2011). The right to look is not a romantic call for transparency. It is a claim about jurisdiction over interpretation: who gets to see, to name, and to dispute what counts as the scene of decision. Transposed into AI governance, countervisuality becomes an insistence that those subjected to classification retain enforceable capacity to inspect and contest the classificatory apparatus itself, not only its outcomes. The stakes are legal and institutional: without a right to look back, error and discrimination become administratively naturalized.

These theoretical contributions matter because algorithmic systems operationalize perception as a programmable pipeline that scales across domains. When institutions convert a person into a vector, a score, or a risk label, they do not merely represent; they authorize downstream allocation. This shift intensifies what can be called asymmetric visibility: individuals become increasingly legible through data traces while decision pathways remain structurally opaque, difficult to reconstruct, and therefore difficult to challenge with evidence. In that arrangement, “transparency” becomes a reputational keyword rather than an operational capacity. Put differently, the governance question is not whether institutions see; it is whether they can be seen seeing, in forms that permit contestation.

Algorithmic accountability research reframes this asymmetry as an evidentiary conflict, not a communication gap. Diakopoulos defines algorithmic accountability in terms of investigability: the ability to observe computational power structures through methods that can withstand institutional incentives to obscure (Diakopoulos, 2015). This is an infrastructural claim. If oversight lacks observation routes, disputes collapse into competing narratives, while the system continues to allocate. In practice, investigability requires stable traces: logs, records of design choices, and evaluative artifacts that survive personnel turnover and organizational forgetting.

Kroll and colleagues press the normative stakes by locating automated decision systems inside rule-of-law expectations, including due process and the ability to verify compliance claims rather than merely assert them (Kroll et al., 2017). On this view, performance metrics do not exhaust legitimacy because legitimacy also depends on whether affected parties can contest, and whether institutions can demonstrate, that constraints were respected. Raji and colleagues operationalize that stance through internal auditing as an end-to-end practice, where documentation and monitoring are treated as governance artifacts rather than optional engineering hygiene (Raji et al., 2020). The shared implication is blunt: accountability is not a post hoc explanation, it is an evidentiary infrastructure that must be built into the system lifecycle.

Documentation research specifies the substrate through which reciprocal inspection becomes possible. Datasheets for datasets propose standardized records of dataset composition, collection conditions, intended uses, and limitations (Gebru et al., 2018). Model cards extend the same logic to trained models, requiring disclosure of evaluation context, performance characteristics, and subgroup behavior (Mitchell et al., 2019). These artifacts matter because they stabilize memory and responsibility. A dataset without a datasheet and a model without a model card are not only opaque; they are institutionally deniable, since the organization can no longer reliably reconstruct what it built, what it tested, or what it knew. Documentation, in this sense, is not a clerical add-on. It is the material form of contestability.

Fairness research reveals how invisibilization emerges when governance fails to treat representation and measurement as enforceable constraints. Buolamwini and Gebru’s audit of commercial gender classification systems documents intersectional error disparities, with the heaviest error burden falling on darker-skinned women, and links these outcomes to benchmark composition and evaluative scope (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018). The concrete anchor here is familiar: a face analysis API deployed at scale becomes a pipeline of misrecognition when “who is in the dataset” is treated as a technical detail rather than a governance decision. Invisibility is not the absence of data; it is the institutional choice to let absence become outcome.

Legal scholarship clarifies why this mechanism is structurally resilient. Barocas and Selbst show how data-driven systems can produce disparate impact through ostensibly neutral proxies embedded in institutional pipelines, even absent discriminatory intent (Barocas & Selbst, 2016). The harm channel does not require an explicit bias variable; it requires a system that learns correlations inside historically structured data and then routes those correlations into allocation. This makes governance harder, not easier, because intent is not the primary evidentiary threshold. The question becomes whether institutions can surface, test, and constrain proxy effects before deployment makes them consequential.

Selbst and colleagues extend the critique by targeting abstraction as a governance trap: fairness framed as a property of a model can fail once the model is embedded in a sociotechnical system that reorganizes incentives, feedback loops, and institutional decision rights (Selbst et al., 2019). Abstraction boundaries can become liability boundaries. When organizations modularize responsibility (data team, model team, deployment team, vendor), the system’s harms can become structurally unowned. In this sense, fairness collapses into governance: it is sustained by procurement discipline, evaluation design, monitoring thresholds, and recourse pathways, not by metrics alone.

Contestability scholarship makes explicit what reciprocal inspection must enable: action, not merely understanding. Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell propose counterfactual explanations that tell an individual what would need to change to obtain a different outcome, without requiring access to the full model internals (Wachter et al., 2018). The governance relevance is that explanation becomes usable, hence legally and administratively meaningful. Ustun, Spangher, and Liu tighten this further through actionable recourse: it is not enough to specify a counterfactual; the required changes must be feasible within the constraints of a person’s life and the institution’s rules (Ustun et al., 2018). Explanation without feasible recourse can become procedural theater, a pathway that looks like due process while preserving substantive domination.

Within European data protection discourse, Kaminski and Malgieri argue for algorithmic impact assessments that generate multi-layered explanations calibrated to different audiences (individuals, regulators, auditors), treating explanation as a governance instrument rather than a single technical statement (Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021). This matters because accountability is plural. A user needs a route to contest; a regulator needs a route to verify; an auditor needs a route to reconstruct. A single explanatory mode cannot satisfy these layers unless governance is designed as an evidentiary system from the start.

Voice and speech technologies extend these problems into audibility, where the perceptual medium is not the image but the utterance. Koenecke and colleagues document significant racial disparities in word error rates across major commercial automatic speech recognition systems (Koenecke et al., 2020). The anchor is mundane: a call-center transcript, a clinical dictation, a hiring screen, an education tool. When recognition error distributes unevenly, error becomes allocation. In that moment, “quality” functions as governance. A misheard sentence can become an absent complaint, an incorrect note in a medical record, or an eligibility denial that cannot be contested because the system did not preserve a faithful trace.

Conversational systems intensify the stakes because they do not only listen; they speak. When an interface produces policy-like outputs, it can operate as institutional speech in the eyes of a user who reasonably treats the interaction as authoritative. This shifts governance from perception to representation: what the system says can bind the organization in disputes, yet conversational output often escapes the evidentiary discipline imposed on formal written channels. The governance demand that follows is reciprocal audibility: if institutions operationalize listening and speaking through automated systems, affected parties must have enforceable routes to verify what was captured, how it was processed, and how errors were corrected. In operational terms, this requires logging, versioning, escalation routes, and remediation timelines that treat conversational events as auditable institutional acts.

Across these literatures, the pattern is consistent. Surveillance theory explains why asymmetries in perception stabilize power (Browne, 2015; Mirzoeff, 2011). Accountability scholarship specifies that legitimacy requires investigability and verification, not narrative reassurance (Diakopoulos, 2015; Kroll et al., 2017; Raji et al., 2020). Documentation practices provide the transportable artifacts that make reconstruction possible (Gebru et al., 2018; Mitchell et al., 2019). Fairness research shows how invisibilization emerges from measurement choices and benchmark politics (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018), while legal analysis shows why disparate impact persists through proxy pipelines even absent intent (Barocas & Selbst, 2016), and sociotechnical critique shows why abstraction routinely mislocates responsibility (Selbst et al., 2019). Contestability work clarifies that explanation must support recourse (Wachter et al., 2018; Ustun et al., 2018) and that governance requires multi-layered accounts that travel across audiences and institutions (Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021). Speech research shows that audibility failures reproduce the same invisibilization logic through unequal recognition burdens (Koenecke et al., 2020).

The integration gap, then, is not the absence of tools; it is the tendency to treat these as parallel solutions to adjacent problems. A visibility-plus-audibility frame instead treats them as interlocking requirements produced by the same governance machinery. Documentation enables inspection; inspection enables contestation; contestation disciplines fairness evaluation; fairness monitoring exposes invisibilization; audibility controls prevent conversational interfaces from becoming accountability voids. Under this view, legitimacy is not secured by declarations or aggregate performance. It behaves like an evidence-backed asset: it is produced when institutions can reconstruct decisions and demonstrate correction, and it is depleted when they cannot.

## **Conceptual Framework**

This project conceptualizes AI governance as a struggle over who gets to perceive, record, interpret, and contest consequential decisions - shifting the usual focus from “model performance” to the politics of visibility and audibility. The framework begins from the premise that visibility is not a neutral good but a power relation: institutions can make people legible through data capture while keeping decision processes comparatively illegible. Browne (2015) shows how modern surveillance regimes historically intensify racialized legibility - producing hypervisibility of Blackness - while insulating institutional practices from reciprocal scrutiny. Mirzoeff (2011) complements this by framing visuality as an authority-producing regime and “the right to look” as a claim to contestable perception. Together, these accounts motivate a governance requirement: when automated systems classify, allocate, or constrain opportunities, affected people must have practical capacity to “look back” at the system as a decision-making apparatus, not merely receive a result.

From this theoretical base, the framework identifies two recurring failure modes that translate perceptual power into institutional harm. First is asymmetric visibility, where individuals are rendered increasingly measurable (profiles, scores, logs, embeddings) while the institutional mechanisms acting on them remain opaque, difficult to investigate, and difficult to challenge with evidence. Algorithmic accountability research treats this not as a communications problem but as an investigability problem: accountability requires methods and records that make computational power structures observable in durable, contestable ways (Diakopoulos, 2015). Legal scholarship further insists that automated systems must be compatible with verification-oriented norms (e.g., due process-like expectations), where it is not enough to claim compliance - institutions must be able to demonstrate it through traceable procedures and auditable controls (Kroll et al., 2017). Second is invisibilization, where omissions, distortions, or proxy structures in data and measurement become systematically unequal outcomes. Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) show how intersectional performance disparities can be produced and normalized when benchmark composition and evaluation scope are treated as incidental. Barocas and Selbst (2016) explain why this persists even without discriminatory intent: “neutral” proxies can reproduce disparate impact because data-driven systems learn correlations embedded in historically structured contexts. Selbst et al. (2019) adds a key sociotechnical warning: abstraction can misplace responsibility by treating fairness as a model property rather than a system property shaped by deployment context, incentives, and feedback loops. In this framework, invisibilization is therefore not simply “bias in the algorithm,” but a governance failure to treat representational decisions (what is measured, who is represented, what counts as error) as enforceable constraints.

The framework then extends the visibility problem into an audibility problem for speech and conversational systems. Where vision-based AI governs through images and visual classification, voice-facing AI governs through listening and speaking - capturing utterances, transforming them into machine-usable representations, and sometimes producing outputs that users interpret as authoritative institutional speech. Koenecke et al. (2020) demonstrates that automated speech recognition can impose unequal burdens through differential word error rates across racial groups; when speech recognition is embedded in high-stakes settings, recognition error becomes an allocation mechanism. This creates what the framework treats as a parallel governance demand: reciprocal audibility. If institutions deploy automated listening or conversational interfaces in consequential contexts, affected people must have meaningful ways to verify what was captured, how it was processed, and how errors are corrected - otherwise “voice” becomes an accountability void where harms are difficult to evidence and contest.

To turn critique into operational requirements, the framework specifies two proportionality standards: reciprocal visibility and reciprocal audibility. Reciprocal visibility requires (1) *notice* that an automated system meaningfully intervened, (2) *contestability* through procedures that can actually change outcomes, and (3) *correction* backed by evidence and documented remediation. Reciprocal audibility requires the same triad but applied to speech-mediated interactions: the person must be able to establish what was heard (or misheard), how that input influenced downstream decisions, and what remedy exists when the system’s listening/speaking introduces consequential error. These standards align accountability with enforceable capacities rather than aspirational transparency: legitimacy depends on whether the institution can produce durable traces, reconstruct decision pathways, and make disputes resolvable through evidence rather than narrative.

Operationally, the framework treats governance as an end-to-end evidentiary infrastructure rather than a single policy statement. Following auditing and accountability approaches that emphasize lifecycle coverage, internal responsibility, and institutionalized monitoring (Raji et al., 2020; Kroll et al., 2017; Diakopoulos, 2015), the framework requires a “governance machinery” capable of: maintaining an inventory of systems and use contexts; establishing access, escalation, and supplier controls; conducting impact assessment processes that generate records usable by multiple audiences; preserving decision-relevant logs and version histories; implementing deployment gates and rollback triggers; monitoring for drift and disparity; and producing audit-ready artifacts that remain stable across personnel turnover. Crucially, documentation is not treated as optional “good practice,” but as the medium of contestability. Datasheets for datasets formalize provenance, intended use, and limitations in ways that allow stakeholders to evaluate whether a dataset is fit for purpose (Gebru et al., 2018). Model cards likewise institutionalize disclosure about evaluation context and subgroup performance, enabling external and internal scrutiny of how a model behaves and where it is likely to fail (Mitchell et al., 2019). In this framework, these documentation practices are governance instruments: they stabilize institutional memory and make responsibility traceable.

Finally, the framework specifies that explanation must be action-enabling, otherwise it risks functioning as procedural theater. Counterfactual explanations provide one route to meaningful contestation by describing what would need to change for a different decision outcome without requiring full model transparency (Wachter, Mittelstadt, & Russell, 2018). But the framework distinguishes “explanation” from “recourse”: changes must be feasible within real constraints, not merely theoretically possible (Ustun, Spangher, & Liu, 2018). To support governance across stakeholders, explanation must also be multi-layered - producing different kinds of accounts for individuals, auditors, and regulators - consistent with the logic of algorithmic impact assessments that generate audience-appropriate justification and evidence (Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021).

In sum, the conceptual framework links surveillance and visuality theory (Browne, 2015; Mirzoeff, 2011) to algorithmic accountability and rule-of-law expectations (Diakopoulos, 2015; Kroll et al., 2017; Raji et al., 2020), and then grounds fairness harms in the interaction of data, proxies, and sociotechnical embedding (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018; Barocas & Selbst, 2016; Selbst et al., 2019). It extends these concerns to speech systems through demonstrated performance disparities (Koenecke et al., 2020) and treats documentation and auditing as the practical substrate of contestability (Gebru et al., 2018; Mitchell et al., 2019). The governing claim is that legitimacy is produced when institutions can reconstruct decisions, demonstrate constraints, and deliver actionable recourse - and is depleted when automated perception and speech operate without durable evidence trails, usable explanations, and correction pathways.

## **Methods and Materials**

The study uses a comparative, qualitative multiple-case design organized as governance reconstruction, treating each incident as a trace of (i) algorithmic perception (visibility, voice, or hybrid), (ii) the coupling between model output and institutional action, and (iii) governance capacity under stress. The methodological wager is that public failures are rarely “first failures.” They are late-stage visibility events that render earlier governance breakdowns reconstructible, which makes incidents analytically valuable not as spectacles of error but as windows onto what an institution could not evidence, monitor, or correct before harm surfaced. This orientation aligns the method with the paper’s politics-of-visibility frame: institutional power concentrates where systems can see, listen, and speak without producing reciprocal evidence that would allow affected parties, auditors, or regulators to look back (Browne, 2015; Mirzoeff, 2011).

Inference proceeds through two complementary strategies: within-case process tracing and across-case pattern matching. Process tracing reconstructs the sequence connecting model outputs to institutional actions and downstream consequences, with the explicit goal of identifying the earliest break in governance machinery and tracking how that break propagated into public failure or harm. This is an accountability method in Diakopoulos’s sense: it treats the key problem as investigability, meaning the ability to produce an inspectable account of computational power in operation, not merely an after-the-fact narrative (Diakopoulos, 2015). It also tracks with rule-of-law expectations that automated decision systems must be governable through verifiable procedures rather than asserted compliance (Kroll et al., 2017). In practical terms, each case is reconstructed as a governance chain: what was built or procured, what evidence existed about its intended use and limits, how outputs were operationalized in decisions, what monitoring existed (or did not), what contestation pathways were available to those affected, and what forms of remediation were actually enacted once failure became visible.

Pattern matching then tests whether observed failures cluster around recurring missing components and whether voice-mediated systems exhibit higher rates of “interface laundering” when evidence controls are not enforced. In construct-validity terms, the conceptual framework supplies the expected pattern: if governance functions as evidentiary infrastructure, breakdowns should be visible as missing artifacts and broken operational links rather than as isolated technical surprises. This comparative logic is designed to avoid the abstraction trap identified by sociotechnical fairness research, where analysis isolates “the model” from the institutional system that deploys it (Selbst et al., 2019). Here, the comparative unit is not architecture but governance function: inventory, accountability assignments, documentation, evaluation scope, deployment gates, monitoring, incident response, and recourse pathways that convert harm into correction rather than into institutional denial.

The design is theory-disciplined through explicit boundary conditions. The framework is applied to systems coupled to institutional consequence, meaning material allocation and enforceable decisions in domains such as employment, welfare eligibility, liberty, capital deployment, or consumer redress, where contestability and evidentiary durability are not optional goods but conditions of legitimacy. This focus operationalizes a central legal point: disparate impact can be produced through ostensibly neutral proxies embedded in institutional pipelines, even when discriminatory intent is absent (Barocas & Selbst, 2016). Accordingly, the method does not treat discrimination as a rare anomaly detectable only through intent; it treats it as a predictable governance risk that must be surfaced through evaluation scope, subgroup testing, and monitoring practices comparable to what intersectional auditing work makes visible (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018).

Case selection follows purposeful, maximum-variation sampling across consequence domains and modalities. The aim is analytic leverage rather than representativeness: the corpus is constructed to stress-test the framework across systems that primarily “see” (classification and visual inference), systems that “listen” (speech-to-text and voice capture), and systems that “speak” (conversational interfaces whose outputs may function as institutional speech acts). This modality-sensitive selection is substantively motivated. Speech technologies can extend invisibilization into the auditory domain via unequal recognition burdens, as evidenced by documented racial disparities in automated speech recognition performance (Koenecke et al., 2020). Conversational systems, meanwhile, create a distinct accountability vulnerability because the interaction surface can appear low-stakes while still binding organizational commitments; this is precisely the condition under which interface laundering becomes plausible and testable through missing logs, versioning records, and escalation traces.

“Materials” in this study are treated as governance artifacts and traces rather than as experimental inputs. Each case is assembled as a documentary dossier oriented toward reconstructability: what the institution publicly claimed, what external oversight bodies recorded, what technical descriptions were available about data and model behavior, and what changed after failure. This evidentiary posture aligns with end-to-end auditing approaches that treat documentation, evaluation, and monitoring as durable governance artifacts rather than optional engineering hygiene (Raji et al., 2020), and it treats accountability as a requirement for verifiable observation rather than reputational reassurance (Diakopoulos, 2015; Kroll et al., 2017). Where artifacts are missing, that absence is not treated as a neutral gap. It is treated as an empirical signal about governance capacity: an institution that cannot produce durable traces cannot credibly claim it governed a system whose outputs were coupled to consequential action.

Operationalization is oriented by the paper’s governance requirements for reciprocal visibility and reciprocal audibility. Documentation practices such as datasheets and model cards function as method-relevant objects because they specify what must be recorded for systems to remain inspectable across time, organizational turnover, and regulatory scrutiny (Gebru et al., 2018; Mitchell et al., 2019). In parallel, the method evaluates explanation not as a public-relations genre but as a governance mechanism: whether the accounts produced are usable for contestation, and whether they support feasible recourse rather than procedural theater (Wachter et al., 2018; Ustun et al., 2018). Multi-layered impact assessment logic further disciplines what counts as “explanation” by requiring accounts that can travel across audiences and institutional roles, including individuals, auditors, and regulators (Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021). Put differently, the method treats explainability as an accountability instrument whose adequacy depends on its capacity to change outcomes under contestation, not on its rhetorical clarity.

Analysis produces two linked outputs for each case. First, a within-case reconstruction that identifies the earliest governance break and maps how that break propagated through deployment, drift, escalation, and remediation, with attention to whether fairness harms resemble disparate impact through proxies (Barocas & Selbst, 2016), whether measurement and evaluation scope produced intersectional error burdens (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018), and whether sociotechnical embedding reorganized responsibility in ways that prevented effective correction (Selbst et al., 2019). Second, a cross-case pattern synthesis that tests whether failures cluster at predictable evidentiary choke points, especially around gating, evidence integrity, monitoring, audit-ready reporting, and modality-specific accountability controls for voice and conversational systems. This comparative claim is consistent with the paper’s closing discipline: governance reconstruction treats inventory, gates, evidence integrity, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting as empirically checkable links, and it anticipates that high-profile failures cluster at these points rather than emerging as random technical surprises.

## **Analysis**

This study treats AI governance as a politics of visibility and audibility: organizational practices decide which decision pathways become legible, which harms remain structurally unobservable, and which affected parties retain practical capacity to challenge consequential outcomes. The analytic wager therefore sits upstream of public scandal and downstream of institutional choice, where evidentiary infrastructures either enable or prevent noticing, contesting, and correcting harm before it hardens into routine allocation. Put differently, the study treats legitimacy as an evidence-backed asset, produced or depleted by whether an organization can reconstruct what happened, why it happened, and what changed after harm, in a form that can survive dispute (Diakopoulos, 2015; Kroll et al., 2017; Raji et al., 2020).

The analysis proceeds from public case dossiers assembled across heterogeneous document types, treated as perspectival traces rather than transparent accounts. Court filings, regulator findings, corporate statements, investigative reporting, and technical audits are read as documents with incentives, omissions, and strategic silences; their value lies less in any single authoritative account than in what becomes triangulable across genres. This choice aligns with the paper’s theoretical frame: surveillance and visuality scholarship insists that institutions can render people legible while insulating institutional mechanisms from reciprocal inspection (Browne, 2015; Mirzoeff, 2011), and governance analysis must therefore treat conflicted narratives and partial disclosure as predictable features of the accountability landscape rather than as methodological noise.

Within this evidentiary stance, conflicts among sources are coded as data. Divergence is treated as a signal of contested accountability, organizational boundary work, or missing governance artifacts, particularly when institutions face incentives to limit disclosure once harm becomes visible (Diakopoulos, 2015). Documentary absence is likewise treated as data. When a dossier lacks audit artifacts, monitoring evidence, ownership records, contestation documentation, or version histories, the absence is coded as an empirically meaningful deficit in inspectability. The analytic consequence is explicit: a system can be widely deployed while remaining governance-poor, precisely because the organization lacks the durable traces required for reconstruction and contestation (Kroll et al., 2017; Raji et al., 2020).

The study implements comparative qualitative “governance reconstruction” across seven heterogeneous cases spanning labor gatekeeping, biometric data provenance, capital allocation, administrative due process, vendor-mediated risk scoring, internal conversational leakage, and customer-facing institutional speech. The comparative claim is deliberately constrained. It does not assume shared technical architectures; it assumes that governance machinery can be compared across heterogeneous settings because consequential automation repeatedly requires the same operational links: inventory, controls, assessments, evidence integrity, gates, monitoring, and audit-ready reporting (Raji et al., 2020). This focus also resists the abstraction trap identified in sociotechnical fairness work, where analysis isolates “the model” from the institutional system that deploys it, thereby relocating responsibility into a technical module that cannot actually hold the harm (Selbst et al., 2019).

The primary unit of analysis is the governance break: the earliest identifiable point in the machinery chain where a missing link plausibly enabled downstream harm, or prevented meaningful correction once harm began to surface. Operationally, the method adapts process tracing to documentary environments. Each case is reconstructed as a temporally ordered chain connecting model outputs to institutional actions and downstream consequences, with a repeated diagnostic question: at what point did the organization lose the capacity to produce an inspectable account of the decision pathway, and what downstream effects followed from that evidentiary loss (Diakopoulos, 2015; Kroll et al., 2017)?

Coding translates the conceptual framework into dossier-observable features across five dimensions: modality, coupling, asymmetry, recourse architecture, and governance machinery completeness. Modality codes whether a system primarily “sees” (classification and visual inference), “listens” (speech capture and transcription), or “speaks” (conversational interfaces that users can reasonably treat as institutional speech), because modality shapes what evidence must exist for accountability to be possible. Speech is treated as governance-relevant perception rather than as a narrow quality problem, given documented racial disparities in automated speech recognition that can convert transcription error into unequal allocation when embedded in consequential workflows (Koenecke et al., 2020). Coupling codes how tightly model output binds institutional action, because high coupling steepens the harm gradient: small documentation or monitoring deficits can become systemically consequential once outputs routinize into eligibility, denial, or sanction. Asymmetry codes the extent to which people become measurable while mechanisms remain opaque, operationalized by whether affected parties and oversight bodies have sufficient access to assemble a contestable account of system operation (Browne, 2015; Mirzoeff, 2011). Recourse architecture codes notice, meaningful contestation, and correction as practiced rather than as declared, treating explanation as a governance instrument only when it can support action and outcome change (Wachter et al., 2018; Ustun et al., 2018). Governance machinery completeness codes whether the dossier evidences the operational chain from inventory through audit-ready reporting, with missing links treated as primary comparative findings (Raji et al., 2020).

To discipline interpretation and prevent drift into principles-only rhetoric, the analysis applies two evaluative indices as audit heuristics rather than psychometric measures. The Reciprocal Visibility Index (RVI) assesses whether affected parties and oversight bodies can inspect decision pathways sufficiently to contest and correct outcomes, distinguishing formal declarations from operational evidence. The Reciprocal Audibility Index (RAI) assesses whether conversational and voice-mediated systems generate durable evidence and enforceable accountability through logging, versioning, escalation, and remediation timelines, consistent with multi-layered explanation logic that must travel across audiences and institutional roles (Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021). Both indices use a three-point rubric (0 absent, 1 partial, 2 robust), applied to dossier evidence rather than organizational self-reporting, and are used as interpretive anchors rather than stand-alone quantitative outputs.

The indices also function as fairness discipline. Disparate impact can be produced through ostensibly neutral proxies embedded in institutional pipelines, including in settings where discriminatory intent is absent or legally irrelevant (Barocas & Selbst, 2016). Accordingly, the analytic demand is not simply that organizations report aggregate performance, but that they maintain evidentiary capacity to detect and constrain proxy effects and subgroup burdens before deployment stabilizes them. This demand is sharpened by empirical work documenting intersectional error disparities in commercial gender classification and showing how evaluation scope and benchmark composition can harden into unequal error burdens (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018). Documentation proposals such as datasheets for datasets and model cards are treated here as governance-relevant objects because they specify what must be recorded for systems to remain inspectable across time, turnover, and dispute (Gebru et al., 2018; Mitchell et al., 2019). In this sense, documentation is not clerical hygiene; it is the material substrate of contestability and the precondition for actionable explanation and feasible recourse (Wachter et al., 2018; Ustun et al., 2018).

Finally, analysis proceeds in two stages. Within-case reconstruction identifies the first governance break and maps how that break propagated through deployment, drift, escalation, and remediation, with attention to whether harms resemble disparate impact through proxies (Barocas & Selbst, 2016), whether measurement scope produces intersectional error burdens (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018), and whether sociotechnical embedding reorganizes responsibility in ways that block correction (Selbst et al., 2019). Cross-case synthesis then tests whether failures cluster at recurring evidentiary choke points, especially around gating, evidence integrity, vendor boundary controls, and modality-specific accountability for voice and conversational systems, where the absence of durable traces can collapse disputes into narrative conflict rather than evidence-backed adjudication (Diakopoulos, 2015; Kroll et al., 2017).

## **Results**

Across the seven cases, governance breaks concentrate where organizations fail to convert consequential automation into durable, inspectable artifacts. The cross-case synthesis indicates recurring deficits in gating, where systems move from experimentation to consequential deployment without enforceable sign-off; in evidence integrity, where records are incomplete, non-versioned, or non-reconstructable; in vendor boundary controls, where audit rights and documentation access are constrained; and in recourse architecture, where notice, contestation, and correction remain aspirational rather than operational. This clustering reinforces the study’s central claim that governance machinery, not model performance alone, is the primary analytic object: missing machinery links convert foreseeable risks into administratively untraceable harms (Kroll et al., 2017; Raji et al., 2020).

The pattern is legible as an accountability failure in Diakopoulos’s sense: investigability collapses when oversight cannot reliably observe how computational power structures operate in practice, and disputes therefore default to competing narratives rather than reconstructable evidence (Diakopoulos, 2015). In rule-of-law terms, the cases show that compliance claims remain non-verifiable when institutions cannot demonstrate, through traceable procedures and auditable controls, how constraints were respected or violated (Kroll et al., 2017). The result is a governance failure that is temporally prior to the public incident: the institution loses the capacity to know what it did at scale, then later discovers that it cannot prove what happened when asked to account.

Documentation deficits emerge as a repeatable mechanism that links missing evidence to institutional deniability. Where dossiers lack durable records of dataset provenance, intended use, evaluation scope, and subgroup performance, institutions also lack stable memory of what was built, what was tested, and what was known at the time of deployment. The recurring absence of documentation artifacts that would be recognizable as datasheets for datasets or model cards is therefore not incidental; it is a structural precondition for accountability gaps, particularly when staff turnover and vendor layering fragment responsibility across organizational boundaries (Gebru et al., 2018; Mitchell et al., 2019; Raji et al., 2020).

Fairness-related harms appear less as isolated technical surprises than as governance consequences of measurement choices, proxy pipelines, and abstraction boundaries. The cases support the legal-structural point that disparate impact can be produced through ostensibly neutral proxies embedded in institutional systems, even absent discriminatory intent, which means that governance cannot treat intent as the relevant threshold for pre-deployment constraint (Barocas & Selbst, 2016). At the same time, the empirical logic of invisibilization remains legible: when evaluation scope is narrowed and benchmark composition is treated as a technical detail rather than as an enforceable governance decision, error burdens can distribute unevenly across intersecting social categories (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018). This is also where the fairness-and-abstraction critique becomes operational: in several cases, responsibility is modularized across data, model, deployment, and vendor teams, and the resulting abstraction boundary becomes a liability boundary, producing harms that are structurally unowned and therefore slow to correct (Selbst et al., 2019).

Cases involving voice-facing or conversational interfaces exhibit a distinct and recurring failure mode: interface laundering. Even when end users experience the interaction as an institutional commitment, the evidentiary substrate may be too thin to reconstruct what was said, by which system version, under what prompts, and with what escalation path. This result aligns with evidence that speech systems can impose unequal burdens through differential word error rates, which means that audibility failures can function as an inequality channel when speech recognition is embedded in hiring, customer service, education, or clinical documentation (Koenecke et al., 2020). In these cases, governance failure is not limited to misrecognition; it includes the absence of durable traces that would allow a misheard utterance, or a policy-misstated output, to be contested and corrected.

Recourse remains the most fragile link where coupling is high. High-coupling settings repeatedly show low reciprocal visibility: affected persons cannot assemble contestable accounts of decisions, and oversight bodies cannot reliably inspect the pathway from input to outcome. Explanation practices, when present, frequently fail the action test. Counterfactual explanation becomes governance-relevant only when it provides usable information about what would change the outcome (Wachter et al., 2018), and actionable recourse requires that the demanded changes are feasible within a person’s constraints rather than formally imaginable (Ustun et al., 2018). Across the cases, recourse is often rendered procedural rather than effective, which helps explain why harms persist even after public visibility events.

A final cross-case implication concerns multi-audience accountability. Where dossiers include impact-assessment-like artifacts, they often fail to generate multi-layered explanations that can travel across individuals, auditors, and regulators in ways that support both contestation and verification. This gap matters because accountability is plural: a user needs an actionable pathway to challenge; an auditor needs reconstructability; a regulator needs verifiable evidence of compliance and remediation (Kaminski & Malgieri, 2021). The cases suggest that when organizations treat explanation as a communications genre rather than as an evidentiary instrument, they preserve narrative flexibility at the cost of auditability, and legitimacy becomes a reputational claim rather than an evidence-backed asset (Diakopoulos, 2015; Kroll et al., 2017; Raji et al., 2020).

## **Discussion**

The seven-case synthesis suggests that what gets labeled an “AI incident” is often the late-stage visibility of an earlier governance breakdown: institutions begin allocating opportunities, burdens, and remedies through automated systems before they have built the evidentiary capacity to notice, contest, and correct what those systems are doing at scale. Read through the paper’s politics-of-visibility and politics-of-audibility frame, the recurring failure is not that models sometimes err, but that organizations repeatedly fail to scale *reciprocal inspection* alongside scaled perception and institutional speech. In practice, that asymmetry concentrates power at the point where the institution can see (and increasingly speak) without being equally seeable in the form that accountability requires. (Diakopoulos, 2015).

A first interpretive implication is that “governance” becomes analytically usable only when treated as machinery rather than as principle. Across cases, the same functional chain determines whether harm is containable: inventory and ownership; enforceable gates that prevent quiet drift from pilot to high-coupling deployment; evidence integrity (logs, versioning, lineage, and decision traces); monitoring that is calibrated to the *consequence domain* rather than generic model-health signals; and audit-ready reporting that can survive dispute. This also reframes organizational maturity: maturity is not the presence of fairness language, explainability claims, or broad “responsible AI” commitments; it is whether a third party could reconstruct what happened, why, and what changed afterward. (Raji et al., 2020).

A second implication is that reciprocal visibility operates as a proportionality standard that clarifies why the same technical error looks “small” in low-coupling settings and catastrophic in high-coupling settings. The cases align around a simple amplifier: when output binds action (eligibility, sanctions, hiring screens, redress), thin notice and weak contestation turn ordinary error into a durable allocation regime. Importantly, the proportionality claim does not require full transparency or disclosure of internals. It requires sufficient inspectability to make contestation real: affected people must be able to learn that an automated system meaningfully intervened, challenge the decision through an accessible pathway, and obtain correction when error is demonstrated.

That proportionality logic also sharpens the paper’s fairness argument: disparity is not only a measurement outcome but a governance outcome. Disparate impact can be produced through seemingly neutral proxies embedded in institutional pipelines, which means intent is a poor operational threshold for intervention (Barocas & Selbst, 2016). The cross-case pattern is consistent with the paper’s “invisibilization” mechanism: when evaluation scope is narrow, subgroup monitoring is absent, and documentation is treated as optional, representation gaps and proxy effects become structurally stabilized rather than discovered early enough to constrain. In other words, inequity persists less because organizations are unaware that inequity is possible, and more because governance machinery does not force the work of *making inequity legible* before deployment routinizes it (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018).

The study’s distinctive contribution, however, is the extension of these insights from vision to voice and conversational interfaces. The cases that involve listening and institutional “speech” reveal a specific vulnerability: conversational systems can migrate consequential decisions into an interaction layer that feels informal, ephemeral, and low-stakes - exactly the register in which organizations are least likely to enforce durable evidence trails. The paper names this interface laundering, and the mechanism is operationally testable: when logs, version histories, escalation pathways, and correction timelines are missing or weak, the organization cannot reliably reconstruct what was said, by which version, under what prompts, with what guardrails, and with what remediation. The result is an accountability gap that is not merely technical but institutional: disputes become narrative conflicts because the event itself is not preserved as auditable institutional action. This matters even more when speech recognition or transcription mediates downstream action, given documented performance disparities that can transform “quality drift” into unequal allocation (Koenecke et al., 2020).

This voice-centered finding expands the scope of responsible AI governance beyond models and datasets to include communications governance. If a chatbot functions as institutional speech, it should be governed like institutional communications infrastructure, not like a marketing widget: policy-fidelity controls, logging as default, version control that is externally reconstruable, escalation procedures that are visible and usable, and remediation timelines that convert detected error into documented correction. Similarly, internally deployed (or internally used) conversational tools need governance that anticipates “shadow AI” dynamics: sanctioned pathways that match real work, enforceable controls on sensitive content, and incident-ready escalation routes - because audit voids are most likely where practice outruns policy.

Methodologically, the paper’s governance-reconstruction approach strengthens what counts as evidence in accountability research. Treating dossiers as perspectival traces (rather than as neutral accounts) makes disagreement and documentary absence analytically meaningful: contested narratives and missing artifacts are not noise; they are part of how accountability is negotiated and resisted. The method’s comparative discipline - comparing governance functions across heterogeneous domains - also helps avoid the sociotechnical trap in which “the model” is abstracted from procurement, organizational incentives, and decision coupling. Still, the approach carries predictable limitations: public dossiers distribute visibility unevenly, and cross-case synthesis risks flattening domain specificity. Those constraints should be read as part of the phenomenon itself: when institutions can control what becomes reconstructible, they can also control what becomes governable.

The results therefore support a practical takeaway with modest ambition but strong operational teeth: build the conditions of reconstructability. The recurring missing artifacts are not exotic: enforced gates, durable logs, version histories, ownership clarity, subgroup-aware evaluation and monitoring, supplier governance that does not outsource auditability, and incident readiness that treats remediation as a documented machinery change rather than a reputational promise. The voice-facing cases add one more: treat conversational events as consequential records by default, because accountability cannot be retrofitted once the trace is gone.

Finally, the study points to three focused research extensions. First, paired comparison between scandal cases and “contained” cases could identify which machinery links are most predictive of early detection and rapid remediation. Second, inside-organization qualitative work could map how gates are negotiated, bypassed, or redefined under deadline pressure and vendor constraints. Third, a dedicated empirical program on interface laundering could operationalize audibility controls as measurable governance requirements across sectors, allowing stronger claims about which conversational deployments are structurally audit-ready versus structurally evasive.

## **Conclusion**

This paper argues that AI governance is best understood as a politics of visibility and audibility: institutions increasingly deploy automated perception and automated speech in consequential settings while often failing to build the reciprocal evidentiary capacity that makes those deployments contestable, correctable, and therefore governable. The seven-case synthesis suggests that high-profile “AI incidents” cluster at predictable choke points - gating, evidence integrity, vendor boundary controls, and recourse design - where missing governance machinery turns foreseeable risk into administratively untraceable harm.

The conceptual contribution is to specify reciprocal visibility and reciprocal audibility as proportionality standards: as systems gain power to classify, allocate, and speak authoritatively, affected parties must gain practical capacity to receive notice, contest decisions meaningfully, and secure correction - supported by institutional capacities for reconstruction, usable explanation, and documented remediation. The operational contribution is to treat governance maturity as reconstructability: whether an organization can show what happened, why, and what changed afterward in a form that can withstand dispute.

The closing implication is deliberately disciplined: responsible AI is not secured by principle statements or aggregate performance claims. It is secured when organizations can preserve auditable traces, enable actionable contestation, and convert failure into documented changes in the machinery of deployment.
