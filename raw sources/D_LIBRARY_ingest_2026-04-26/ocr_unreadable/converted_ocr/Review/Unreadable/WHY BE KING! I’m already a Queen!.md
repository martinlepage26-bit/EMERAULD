**WHY BE KING! I’m already a Queen!**

**Sacred Subversions: Queer Becoming in Montreal’s Neo-Pagan Circles**

"It is difficult to speak of action in the world without simultaneously suggesting that our actions are the expression and product of a self that endures behind each of its roles."

— *Erving Goffman (1991: 287)*

"What we know is not 'the world in itself' nor 'the world for us,' but the way in which we construct, through action, our knowledge of the world. The empirical world, in both its material and ideal dimensions, is a constructed and signified given."

— *Alex Mucchielli (2005: 31)*

This is an essay about witches. But not the kind that hide in fairy tales or flicker briefly across Halloween decor. These witches are real, complex, laughing, wounded, fierce. They are queer and trans, urban and solitary, lineage-bound and iconoclastic. They invoke spirits, call down the moon, chant against pipelines and patriarchy. They gather in basements, in forests, on Zoom. They know the world is broken, and they cast spells not to escape it, but to touch it differently.

Contemporary Paganism does not speak in dogma but in gesture, affect, and symbol. It is less a single religion than a spiritual cartography—a terrain of overlapping mythic maps, revived pantheons, seasonal rites, and sensual devotions. In Montreal, this terrain takes on a particular contour: one shaped by linguistic hybridity, queer presence, and post-secular yearning. Here, neo-Paganism is not merely inherited; it is conjured, claimed, stitched together from fragments of folklore, feminist politics, ecological urgency, trauma legacies, and digital subculture. It is both ancient and future-facing, fiercely embodied and relentlessly fluid.

And yet, within these expansive, enchanted spaces, tension persists. Binaries endure: Goddess and God, male and female, the chalice and the blade. Sacred texts are often unwritten, but their scripts are deeply gendered. Ritual form, even when improvised, can reproduce the heteronormative logics of lineage, polarity, and mythic balance. These moments—of misalignment, of discomfort, of reconfiguration—are not anomalies. They are sites of knowledge. They are the queer sacred made visible.

How, then, do queer and trans practitioners move within such frames? How do they craft livable, magical lives at the margins of tradition? How do they refuse not the sacred, but the terms under which it is usually understood? This essay explores those movements—the ritual negotiations, symbolic reworkings, and embodied subversions through which LGBTQ neo-Pagans in Montreal queer the sacred, and are themselves reconfigured by its force. But more than that, it explores the epistemological stakes of taking such practices seriously. It asks what happens to the category of “religion” when those who have been cast as magical, heretical, or excessive begin to narrate their own theologies.

**A Community in Motion**

Between October 2010 and December 2014, I entered this world not only as a researcher, but as a participant-witch. My fieldwork unfolded across full moon circles in basement temples, public rituals in community art spaces, and sweat-laced Beltane dances beneath open sky. I was present at initiation ceremonies, seasonal festivals, political spell-castings, and moments of conflict and reparation. I interviewed twenty practitioners in depth, but learned from many more—in whispered conversations around fire pits, through zines passed from cloak to cloak, in workshops on consent magic, ancestor reverence, and trans deity invocation. The bodies and stories in this essay are drawn from these encounters: textured, fragmentary, and radically alive.

Some were solitary practitioners crafting rituals in bedrooms strewn with tarot cards and hormone vials. Others were high priestesses of longstanding covens, carrying scars and pride from decades of activist witchcraft. Many moved between lineages, traditions, and roles. They were animists and agnostics, kinksters and caretakers. Some came to Paganism through feminist reading groups; others through Tumblr aesthetics, queer trauma, ayahuasca ceremonies, or inherited Celtic spirituality.

They were not always welcome. Some were misgendered during circle, asked to perform roles that denied their bodies, or pushed out of lineages that clung too tightly to binary gods. But they stayed, or they returned, or they built new circles. They invoked trickster spirits, rewrote the Wheel of the Year, dressed as gods no one had yet named. They brought the sacred back from the edge, and refused to let it exclude them.

**A Tradition Recast**

My research situates itself within and against the growing literature on contemporary Paganism. Seminal works by Hutton (1999), Adler (1979/2006), Magliocco (2004), and Clifton (2006) have traced the historical and aesthetic genealogies of modern Witchcraft. Others, like Pike (2001) and Berger (1998), have begun to ask how Paganism interacts with gender, sexuality, and power. But few have placed queer and trans embodiment at the heart of their inquiry. This essay does not simply add LGBTQ voices to an existing frame; it recenters the frame around the ritual lives of those whose bodies, desires, and cosmologies do not fit.

I draw from queer theory not aBs a topical lens but as a method of attunement. I am indebted to Butler’s performativity (1990, 1993), Sedgwick’s affective epistemologies (1990), Halberstam’s notion of queer failure (2011), and Muñoz’s call for queer futurity (2009). I also engage the anthropology of lived religion (Ammerman 2007; McGuire 2008), recognizing that sacred life happens not in creeds but in breath, touch, and rhythm. Feminist ethnography (Behar 1996; Abu-Lughod 1993) anchors my commitment to partiality, intimacy, and the politics of representation.

**Embodied Reflexivity**

I write from within. My own queer, Pagan body is not outside the text, but one of its instruments. I cast circles, called quarters, invoked deities I did not always believe in. I shared meals, heartbreaks, and ecstatic dances with my interlocutors. Sometimes I was scholar; other times, initiate, ritualist, confidant. This positionality is not a methodological flaw but a condition of knowing. It shaped not only what I was allowed to witness—but also how I was transformed by it.

This doubleness allowed me to trace the micro-politics of sacred space: the moments when someone’s voice cracked during invocation, not from error but from transition; when gendered metaphors collided with lived ambiguity; when a participant sat quietly outside the circle, not from disrespect, but from dissociation. These moments are not disruptions. They are the material of this essay.

**Questions and Commitments**

This essay asks:

* How do queer and trans neo-Pagans in Montreal express gender, embodiment, and cosmology through ritual?
* In what ways do they reproduce, resist, or reinvent sacred scripts?
* How does queering the ritual also queer the very category of religion?
* What does it mean to treat magic not as superstition, but as method, as knowledge, as resistance?

Rather than impose coherence, I map contradiction. I attend to both the healing and the harm, the spells that liberate and the liturgies that constrain. I write with love, with ache, and with a deep sense of debt to those who trusted me with their stories.

**Structure of the essay**

section One situates Montreal’s Pagan landscape and the traditions most central to this study: initiatory Wicca, Reclaiming Witchcraft, and their local adaptations. section Two outlines the theoretical scaffolding, with attention to queer embodiment, performativity, lived religion, and the politics of normativity. section Three details the methodological approach, grounded in reflexivity, affective accountability, and ritual participation.

sections Four and Five offer the core ethnographic material. section Four examines how queer identity is performed, affirmed, and negotiated within ritual. section Five explores how power operates in these spaces—who speaks, who leads, who is seen, and who is rendered peripheral.

section Six widens the circle. It queers the very category of “magic,” positioning it as an embodied, decolonial, and gender-dissident epistemology. It argues that queer neo-Paganism is not only a ritual genre but a spiritual method—a cosmology of care.

**Conjuring an Open Circle**

The conclusion does not seal the spell. It breaks it open. It reflects on what it means to think, to write, and to listen from within queer enchantment. It insists that to study magic is to be touched by it—and to allow that touch to change the terms of knowledge itself.

This is not a conclusion, but an invocation. Let the altar remain unset. Let the story remain unfinished. Let the queer sacred speak.

### **1. Queer Cartographies: Mapping Montreal’s Pagan Landscape**

Montreal’s ritual landscape is neither cohesive nor static. It pulses with the rhythms of the moon, flares with sabbats, and reconstitutes itself in borrowed halls, whispered blessings, queer flirtations, and flickering candlelight. This section situates that terrain within broader currents of contemporary Paganism, with a focus on initiatory Wicca, Reclaiming Witchcraft, and their local reinventions. It also re-reads this terrain through the lens of queer geography and spiritual bricolage.

To walk into a Montreal Pagan ritual is to step into a layered space. One might find a reclaimed church basement adorned with rainbow prayer flags, a park in the Plateau where a genderqueer priestess leads a Beltane dance, or a queer-inclusive shop on Saint-Laurent where workshops on ancestor reverence and trauma magic spill into conversations about hormones and heartbreak. The city’s Pagan landscape is not defined by temples, but by networks—of people, herbs, zines, playlists, gestures. It is improvised, relational, enchanted.

What follows is an attempt to map that enchantment—not as a fixed territory, but as a shifting constellation of practices, memories, and political commitments. This map is queer not only in content, but in form: it resists linearity, embraces contradiction, and centers those at the margins of both religious and academic legitimacy.

**1.1 The Object of Study: More Than Religion**

Contemporary Paganism in Montreal presents itself not as a codified religion, but as a plural, layered ecology. It is a spiritual milieu that holds together those who call themselves witches, priestesses, druids, animists, brujas, faeries, and sometimes nothing at all. It gathers those who believe in many gods, no gods, or gods they invent themselves. Most participants I met during fieldwork were less concerned with doctrine than with texture—how the sacred felt, how it smelled, what it allowed them to do or become.

Rather than doctrine, what unites this field is practice. A transmasculine witch might host full moon baths for trauma release, mixing spellwork with therapy. A femme astrologer might open a ritual space with Beyoncé lyrics and offer honey to Venus. A two-spirit circle might blend Cree cosmologies with Wiccan form. What holds all this together is not a shared belief system, but a shared insistence: that the sacred is not dead, and that it can be touched.

**1.2 What Is Paganism, Queered?**

The term “Paganism” has long been a site of projection—first a Christian category of exclusion, later reclaimed by countercultural movements. In its modern form, Paganism is often used to describe spiritualities rooted in pre-Christian traditions, ecological attunement, polytheism, or Goddess worship. But in Montreal, Paganism becomes something else again: a queer archive, a speculative cosmology, a living bricolage.

For some, Paganism is a return. For others, it is invention. Most practitioners I met held multiple truths at once: they honored Celtic ancestors and watched anime, invoked Lilith and practiced Reiki, wore sigils inked on skin and quoted queer theory during ritual planning. They knew the charge of the Goddess and the politics of the pronoun. For them, Paganism was not a category, but a field of play—a place to try on power, to ritualize grief, to make the invisible tangible.

The use of the word “neo-Pagan” in this essay signals not just a temporal break but a methodological one: a shift from assumed lineage to intentional creation. These practitioners are not simply reviving ancient ways—they are remixing them, queering them, weaving them into queer lives that demand more than the binaries offered by both mainstream religion and mainstream LGBTQ politics.

**1.3 Ritual Bricolage as Queer World-Making**

If there is one ritual logic that defines this community, it is bricolage. Rituals are not inherited whole; they are pieced together from memory, internet threads, dreams, and community needs. A Samhain ritual might include Wiccan casting of the circle, Reclaiming-style collective singing, a moment of silence for missing and murdered Indigenous women, and a spontaneous dance to Florence and the Machine. The sacred emerges not from fidelity to a form, but from the charge of presence.

Zara, a nonbinary Reclaiming witch, told me:

“My altar is a mess—Kuan Yin next to Aphrodite next to glitter pinecones I found in the Plateau. It’s all sacred. It’s all me.”

This eclecticism is not arbitrary. It is situated, affective, and politically alive. Rituals often respond to the needs of the moment: to grief after a hate crime, to celebration after a name change, to grounding before surgery, to honoring queer ancestors lost to AIDS, suicide, exile. Sacred practice is not separate from social life—it is one of its modalities.

**1.4 The Tensions of Tradition**

While many groups in Montreal embrace innovation, others maintain inherited structures. Wiccan covens often retain a symbolic polarity rooted in Goddess and God, chalice and blade. Even when the practitioners are queer, trans, or polyamorous, the ritual form can echo heteronormative logics. This can be deeply affirming for some and deeply alienating for others.

Lou (she/they), a trans femme practitioner, reflected:

“I love the structure. It helps me feel held. But sometimes, I wish the Goddess had a penis, too.”

This tension between form and fluidity recurs throughout the essay. Some respond by rewriting scripts; others step outside the structure altogether. A few groups have abandoned the Great Rite or reimagined the elements as emotions rather than genders. Others work within tradition, queering it gently but insistently—invoking Hecate as a crone-god in transition, or calling in the directions not with binaries, but with verbs: to protect, to grieve, to open.

**1.5 Queer Geography: Montreal as Ritual Terrain**

Montreal is not just a backdrop—it is a participant in this story. The city’s linguistic and cultural hybridity, its activist legacy, its Catholic ghosts and queer pulses, all shape the form of ritual life. Anglophone and Francophone groups mingle but do not always overlap. Some circles are explicitly bilingual; others code-switch within ritual. Postcolonial tension is felt in the land acknowledgements, the use of smudging, and the invocation of deities from non-European pantheons.

Spaces of ritual are often makeshift: artist studios, living rooms, parks, yoga spaces, anarchist bookstores. These spaces carry their own auras, constraints, histories. The sacred must be set up, and taken down. Altars are mobile. The circle is never permanent.

This geography is queer not only in its inhabitants but in its structure: unstable, partial, under construction. Montreal’s Paganism is not a unified religion—it is a constellation of practices bound by breath, memory, and improvisation.

**1.6 Conclusion: Mapping the Unmapped**

This section has traced the outlines of a field that resists finality. Montreal’s neo-Pagan landscape is a ritual commons—messy, brilliant, frustrating, alive. Its traditions are both inherited and invented. Its forms both constrain and inspire. Within this field, queer practitioners do more than seek inclusion: they change the very conditions under which sacred life can be imagined.

The next section turns to theory: to the conceptual tools needed to read ritual not only as performance, but as power, as resistance, as world-making. In doing so, it continues the work of mapping a terrain that is always in flux—and always becoming.

**2. Theory as Compass: Performativity, Power, and Queer Knowledge**

To understand the sacred worlds described in the previous section, we need more than definitions—we need tools. This section lays out the theoretical scaffolding of the essay: a set of conceptual instruments drawn from queer theory, ritual studies, feminist anthropology, and the sociology of knowledge. These tools do not simply illuminate ritual; they reveal the politics embedded within it. They allow us to read a chant as both invocation and refusal, a circle as both boundary and breach.

Theory here is not cold or abstract. It is warm-blooded. It comes from the body, the field, the altar. It does not explain away magic—it lets it speak. The thinkers I draw upon—Butler, Sedgwick, Bell, Fraser, Muñoz, TallBear—are not distant authorities but companions in this work of queer world-making. They help trace how identity is not discovered but enacted, how power is not absent in spiritual communities but often disguised, and how magic itself might be an epistemology.

**2.1 Lived Religion and Queer Embodiment**

Nancy Ammerman and Meredith McGuire have argued for a shift in how we study religion: away from doctrine and toward practice. For them, religion is not primarily what people say they believe, but what they do—how they make the sacred felt in kitchens, bedrooms, protests, and spells.

In the Montreal Pagan circles I studied, this insight is vital. Most of my interlocutors did not articulate a coherent theology. Instead, they burned candles, rearranged altars, whispered prayers in the bath. Their rituals were often hybrid: a tarot draw mixed with affirmations from therapy, a Wiccan script modified to include nonbinary deities. Sacred life was made not through rules, but through rhythm.

Lived religion, then, provides a lens through which we can understand queer Paganism as embodied and improvised. It gives us permission to take seriously the sacredness of repetition, the meaning in small gestures, the deep knowledge that emerges from intuitive acts.

**2.2 Performativity and the Ritualization of Gender**

Judith Butler’s theory of performativity reshaped how we understand gender: not as a stable identity but as a repeated doing—a stylized set of acts that create the illusion of coherence. In ritual, this is especially poignant. To perform “the High Priestess,” to call down the Goddess, to wear a robe or wield a blade—these are not neutral acts. They draw on histories, expectations, desires.

In Pagan ritual, gender is everywhere: in the polarity of chalice and blade, in the gendered deity pairings, in the metaphors used to call the elements. For trans and nonbinary practitioners, these scripts can be sites of rupture and reworking. Some change the language. Others shift the choreography. A few refuse the script entirely, building new liturgies from scratch.

Butler’s framework allows us to see these ritual adjustments not as accidents, but as generative interruptions. They show us where the sacred cracks, and where queerness floods in.

**2.3 Recognition and the Sacred Gaze**

Nancy Fraser and Axel Honneth have written extensively on recognition: the social act of being seen, validated, made legible. In ritual, recognition is more than social—it is spiritual. Who is allowed to invoke the God? Who is seen as a “real” witch? Who is given a title, a seat at the altar, a chance to lead?

Many of the queer practitioners I spoke to described moments of misrecognition: being asked to take on gendered roles that erased their identities, being passed over for leadership, being welcomed as symbols but not as subjects. And yet they also described moments of profound visibility: being asked to call a direction, to bless the circle, to speak their truth into being.

Recognition in this context is not a matter of politeness—it is a sacred act. It is the difference between being tolerated and being believed.

**2.4 Ritual as Power, Not Just Performance**

Catherine Bell urges us to think about ritual not only as a set of repeated forms, but as a practice that enacts and reinforces power. Ritual is not outside the world—it is one of the ways the world is made.

In Pagan settings, ritual carries authority. It sets rules, assigns roles, determines who can speak and how. These dynamics are not always equitable. While Paganism often positions itself as anti-hierarchical, hierarchies still emerge: between elders and novices, between initiated and uninitiated, between those with recognized lineages and those without.

For queer practitioners, ritual can both liberate and constrain. Some experience it as a space of affirmation, where fluid identities are welcomed. Others find it replicates the same norms they fled in mainstream religion. Reading ritual through Bell’s lens helps us see these ambivalences clearly.

**2.5 Queerness as Method**

Following José Esteban Muñoz and Kim TallBear, I approach queerness not simply as a subject, but as a method: a way of thinking, feeling, researching, and relating. Queerness, in this sense, is a refusal of closure. It stays with contradiction. It honors the messy, the liminal, the not-yet.

To queer ritual is to open it—to uncertainty, to laughter, to ghosts. To queer scholarship is to write with emotion, to take seriously stories that don’t fit the mold. My interlocutors were not data points; they were guides. They showed me that ritual is not just a text to be analyzed, but a performance to be felt.

This essay does not aim to define queer Paganism. It aims to move with it. To trace its spirals. To dwell in its ruptures.

**2.6 Conclusion: Theoretical Threads for Enchanted Analysis**

Theory is often imagined as the opposite of magic. This section has tried to undo that binary. The thinkers drawn upon here offer a vocabulary for understanding the queer sacred—not as fantasy, but as embodied truth. They help us read rituals not only as cultural performances, but as epistemological events: ways of knowing and unknowing.

In the sections that follow, these tools become companions. They help interpret the chants, the silences, the rituals gone wrong. They allow us to read enchantment not as escape, but as critique.

What we need now is not only interpretation, but presence. The next section turns to methodology: to what it means to research from within the circle—and to be transformed by it.

### **3. Embodied Ethnography: Fieldwork as Ritual, Reflexivity as Spell**

This section details the methodological underpinnings of this study—but it also tells a story of intimacy, vulnerability, and enchantment. It is the story of how I entered this field not just as a researcher, but as a queer, practicing witch. The lines between observer and participant were never stable. I was invoked as often as I was invisible. This is not a flaw. It is the ground of my knowledge.

What follows is not simply a list of methods. It is an account of methodological entanglement. It is about what it means to do research in a space where every circle called is also a mirror, every chant a form of affective labor, every ritual a potential undoing.

**3.1 Entering the Circle: Access and Invitation**

Fieldwork for this study took place between October 2010 and December 2014 in Montreal and its surrounding regions. My entry into the field was not secured through formal letters or institutional endorsements. It happened through presence: showing up, staying quiet, asking questions, being seen. Access came through enchantment.

I attended open rituals in community spaces, private ceremonies in cramped apartments, festivals in wooded landscapes. I helped carry crates of candles, offered rides, and learned how to smudge without appropriating. I was invited not because I had credentials, but because I carried myself with care. Trust was the true method.

In these spaces, I was rarely just an ethnographer. I was asked to call quarters, to cleanse the space, to help rewrite ritual texts. When someone cried, I held their hand. When someone asked about my practice, I answered honestly. This proximity was not a compromise of rigor—it was the condition of insight.

**3.2 Ritual Ethnography: Methods That Feel**

My methodology combined semi-structured interviews, participant observation, and sensory fieldnotes. But those words feel too clinical for what unfolded. I prefer to call it ritual ethnography.

* **Interviews** were not conducted. They happened—often after ritual, while sharing food, walking to the metro, or smoking under the stars.
* **Observation** was not detached. It was embodied. I smelled the incense. I trembled in trance. I forgot where I was and came back crying.
* **Fieldnotes** were written on napkins, in notebooks, and in dreams. They recorded what was said, but also what was felt, unsaid, or refused.

I also collected ritual scripts, altar photos, chants, digital ephemera, and stories passed between bodies. These artifacts were not treated as data, but as fragments of cosmology.

**3.3 Queer Positionality: Writing From Within**

I write this essay as a queer, white, settler researcher, fluent in both English and French, shaped by feminist thought, transfeminist kinship, and post-secular longings. My queerness shaped how I was seen and what I could see. I was not an outsider, but I was not neutral either.

In some circles, I was welcomed as kin. In others, I had to earn trust slowly. My body carried its own ambiguities: gender-nonconforming, shifting, both marked and assumed. These ambiguities opened doors, but also made some rituals difficult to inhabit. I never stopped negotiating.

Following Donna Haraway, I embrace situated knowledge. Following Kim TallBear, I refuse the fantasy of detached expertise. My voice is in this essay not to claim authority, but to trace the conditions of my seeing.

**3.4 Ethics Beyond the Form**

Ethics were not administered through forms. They were practiced—ongoing, situated, imperfect. All interviews were conducted with consent, and names were changed unless participants requested otherwise. In a few cases, I destroyed notes upon request.

But the deeper ethics were affective. I did not include everything I saw. I withheld stories that felt too raw, too sacred, or too unresolved. I respected the boundary between what was offered and what was shared.

I also practiced what I now call “ritual confidentiality”: a refusal to expose the most fragile, potent parts of ritual life to academic scrutiny. Some spells should not be written. Some circles should remain unbroken.

**3.5 The Community: Constellations, Not Categories**

The twenty formal participants ranged in age from 22 to 58. They identified across a spectrum of genders, sexualities, and spiritual lineages. Some were initiated in Wiccan traditions; others identified with Reclaiming, Druidry, eclectic paths, or no lineage at all. A few had left lineages after conflict or exclusion.

They included trans women, genderqueer faeries, cis lesbians, agender witches, polyamorous priestesses, and nonbinary brujos. They came from diverse class backgrounds and were mostly white, though some identified as Indigenous, Métis, or mixed-race. Many were bilingual, and their rituals reflected Montreal’s linguistic and cultural hybridity.

They did not speak with one voice. They argued, improvised, contradicted each other. Their unity was not in doctrine, but in devotion—to living lives that made room for enchantment.

**3.6 Methodology as Magic**

This section has offered a portrait of a fieldwork process rooted in presence, responsiveness, and queer attunement. But it is also a spell. To do this kind of research is to be changed by it.

I began with questions about gender and ritual. I left with an altar of my own, a dozen friendships, a deep sense of accountability, and a grief I cannot fully name. Some of what I learned can be cited. Some can only be carried.

This essay is not just an account of queer neo-Paganism in Montreal. It is a testimony to what it means to witness, to be seen, and to believe that magic—real, aching, complicated magic—can shape not just the ritual, but the research.

The next section brings us into that ritual space. We begin to see how gender is not just expressed, but performed, enchanted, and remade in sacred time.

**4. Ritual Performance and Queer Identity: Practices of Gender, Practices of Spirit**

This section traces how queer neo-Pagan practitioners in Montreal embody and negotiate their identities within ritual space. Through life narratives, participant observation, and sensory fieldnotes, we enter a world where gender and spirituality are not merely identities—they are verbs. They are acts of crafting, of world-building, of sacred experimentation. They are lived, rehearsed, and at times ruptured within the potent temporal space of the circle.

Ritual is not a neutral ground. It is a container shaped by cosmology, power, and affect. For queer practitioners, it is also a site of becoming—where embodiment is affirmed, questioned, and made otherwise. This section explores how LGBTQ practitioners navigate sacred roles, symbols, and gestures. It attends to both celebration and discomfort, affirmation and friction.

**4.1 Sacred Stagings of the Self**

Rituals are never just rituals. They are mirrors, magnifiers, and portals. When queer and trans people step into a circle, they bring with them not only intention but history. They carry the weight of having been misread, misnamed, misrecognized. Within ritual, the hope is not simply to be included, but to become visible in ways the outside world rarely permits.

Mira, a nonbinary femme witch, once said to me after calling the West:

"It’s not that I believe the water spirits are real. It’s that they believe I am."

For many, ritual is not about metaphysical belief—it’s about relational recognition. It is the one place where the voice that cracks, the body in flux, the pronoun that changes, are not mistakes but magic.

Ritual becomes autobiography with symbols. It is where the sacred and the social converge.

**4.2 The Queer Choreographies of Ritual Form**

Traditional Pagan rituals, especially in Wicca and Reclaiming, are built around repeated gestures: the casting of the circle, the calling of quarters, the drawing down of deity. These structures offer grounding and containment. But they also carry gendered expectations.

The calling of elements is often gender-coded (Air as masculine intellect, Water as feminine emotion). The Great Rite symbolizes heterosexual union through the penetration of blade into chalice. Deities are invoked in pairs: Goddess and God. Roles are often split by gender: High Priest and High Priestess.

For queer practitioners, this choreography can be both stabilizing and stifling. Julien (they/he), a genderqueer practitioner, told me:

"I like the rhythm of the rites. But I always end up rewriting the lines in my head while I say them."

Others take a more radical approach: rewriting scripts, queering roles, disrupting polarity. One group replaced the four classical elements with states of being: Grief, Desire, Pleasure, and Rage. Another rewrote the Charge of the Goddess to include trans ancestors and femme dyke elders.

These interventions are not just about language—they are about cosmology. They rewire the ritual’s symbolic system. They conjure new maps.

**4.3 Gender as Spell, Not Category**

Queer identity in ritual is not stable—it shapeshifts. Participants often described feeling “more real” during ritual. But what that meant varied: more embodied, more fluid, more powerful, more mythical.

Eli (he/they), a transfeminine butch witch, described it this way:

“In ritual I’m not Eli the trans guy. I’m the Keeper of the West. I’m the Vessel. I’m the snake. That’s not escapism—it’s expansion.”

Others found that ritual gave them room to test out pronouns, energies, gendered archetypes. Sometimes they would try on “priestess” one moon, “godform” the next. These were not contradictions—they were initiations.

Gender in these spaces functions like a spell: a repeated, affective, performative act that generates its own truth through embodiment and belief.

**4.4 Friction and Misrecognition in Sacred Space**

Not all experiences were transformative. Many participants described moments of pain, exclusion, or exhaustion. One nonbinary person was asked to “stand with the women” during a ritual. Another was corrected for using the term “they” in a group invocation. Others were told their gender made them ineligible to lead or invoke.

These were not always acts of overt exclusion. Often, they were the product of unconscious norms—scripts inherited without examination. But they left wounds. One fieldnote reads:

“He sat outside the circle the whole time. No one asked why.”

Some responded with withdrawal. Others with ritual refusal—stepping out of roles, changing words mid-spell, bringing in alternative symbols. Many began to form their own circles, where gender was not assigned but conjured.

**4.5 The Queer Sacred as Improvisation**

Improvisation is central to queer ritual. Not as a lack of preparation, but as a form of openness: to the body’s needs, to the spirits that arrive, to the unexpected grief that rises.

Rituals often begin with a script, but rarely end with one. Someone forgets the words and sings instead. Someone offers a poem instead of a call. Someone bursts into tears and the circle shifts to hold them. These are not failures of form—they are expressions of the sacred.

For queer practitioners, this improvisational ethic reflects lived experience. As Zara said:

“We’re always making it up. That’s not chaos—it’s magic.”

The queer sacred is not found in perfection. It is found in the glitch, the slip, the gasp.

**4.6 Conclusion: Becoming Sacred, Becoming Possible**

This section has traced how queer and trans practitioners inhabit, disrupt, and reinvent ritual space. Their performances are not only expressions of identity—they are experiments in what sacredness can hold. Ritual becomes not just a container for the self, but a crucible for becoming.

Gender is not erased—it is expanded. Tradition is not abandoned—it is transformed. The sacred is not denied—it is rewritten, whispered, worn differently.

What emerges is not a single model of queer ritual, but a constellation of practices: tentative, bold, fractured, ecstatic. These performances do not resolve the contradictions of sacred space—they live within them.

The next section turns toward power. If ritual is a site of sacred becoming, it is also a space shaped by hierarchy, legitimacy, and structural tension. What does it mean to speak, to lead, to be believed in the circle? And what happens when the invitation to belong is not evenly extended?

**5. Power in the Circle: Authority, Tension, and the Limits of Inclusion**

If the previous section showed how queer and trans practitioners perform identity in sacred space, this section turns toward structure. Rituals are never only aesthetic—they are also political. They are sites where legitimacy is negotiated, authority is asserted, and exclusions are enacted. This section examines how power moves in Montreal’s queer Pagan spaces: who leads, who is heard, who is believed, and who is silenced or made peripheral.

Contrary to popular perception, alternative spiritualities are not free from hierarchy. In fact, the refusal of institutional structure can sometimes mask more insidious dynamics—those rooted in charisma, tradition, or implicit normativity. Queer practitioners are often praised for their creativity, but denied real authority. They are asked to speak their truth, then gently redirected when it disrupts the flow.

This section does not aim to expose hypocrisy, but to trace the contradictions that persist within even the most well-meaning circles. It is a section about complexity, discomfort, and the courage it takes to name power where others see only light.

**5.1 The Altar as Stage: Leadership and Legitimacy**

The sacred circle is not a blank space. It is a choreography of roles: caller of quarters, keeper of time, invoker of deity, holder of space. These roles are often passed down, earned through initiation, or conferred by social recognition. Titles like “High Priestess” or “Elder” carry symbolic weight—even in communities that claim to resist hierarchy.

But who gets to lead?

Many queer and trans participants described being invited to participate but rarely asked to guide. They were told their insights were welcome, yet found themselves relegated to supportive roles. They were praised as visionaries, but denied the microphone. When they questioned gendered metaphors or invoked different cosmologies, they were sometimes accused of being disruptive, political, or “too intense.”

Simone, a nonbinary Reclaiming priestx, told me:

“They say they want change. But when I bring it, they freeze. I’m allowed to be mystical—but not authoritative.”

The altar, it turns out, is a contested site. Authority is spiritual, but also gendered, racialized, and embodied. Charisma matters—but so does familiarity. Being seen as “serious,” “stable,” or “initiated” is often coded in cisnormative and classed ways.

**5.2 Inclusion Fatigue: The Labor of the Marginal**

Inclusion is often framed as a value—but it is also labor. The labor of translating one’s experience into palatable terms. The labor of educating others mid-ritual. The labor of absorbing microaggressions for the sake of harmony.

Jay, a genderfluid witch, described the exhaustion of being the only visibly trans person in a coven:

“They said they wanted to learn. But every time we met, I had to teach. I wasn’t there to teach. I was there to call Fire.”

For many participants, the promise of inclusion came with strings attached: the expectation to represent, to explain, to soften. One practitioner described “performing gratitude” just to stay in the group. Another said, “I always felt like a guest, never a priestess.”

This is not to say that all queer practitioners were excluded. Many led rituals, held titles, and found deep belonging. But even in those cases, the effort it took to claim space—to be seen not just as symbolic, but as sovereign—was often invisible to others.

**5.3 The Ghosts of Tradition**

Even in communities committed to fluidity, tradition haunts the circle. The gender binary is woven into foundational rituals, deity pairings, and elemental associations. Some groups have begun to adapt these forms, but many continue to rely on templates where the Goddess and God must dance in harmony, the chalice and blade must meet.

Marc, a gay elder in a traditional Wiccan lineage, reflected:

“I love the rite. But it always assumes straightness. I’ve been reimagining the Great Rite for years—and I’m still not sure I’ve found a way to make it feel like mine.”

Others described moments of rupture: being asked to embody a gendered energy that misaligned with their reality, being excluded from sex-based mysteries, or having to “prove” their connection to archetypes coded as cisgender.

These moments are not merely aesthetic mismatches. They are theological fractures. They force queer practitioners to choose between authenticity and belonging, between silence and disruption.

**5.4 When the Circle Fails: Exclusion and Exit**

Not all tensions are resolved. Some become untenable. Several participants shared stories of leaving groups after being repeatedly misgendered, sidelined, or spiritually invalidated. These exits were often painful—filled with grief, rage, and a sense of betrayal.

One priestess told me:

“I left because I couldn’t breathe in circle anymore. It was supposed to be sacred, but it became surveillance.”

Others didn’t leave, but stopped speaking. They performed the chants, nodded at the right moments, but no longer brought their full selves to the altar. This withdrawal is not apathy—it is survival.

And yet, many kept practicing. They found new circles, started their own covens, or crafted solitary rituals that reflected their truths. Power, after all, is not only held—it is made. And queer practitioners, even when cast out, often become architects of new cosmologies.

**5.5 Queering Power: Authority Reimagined**

Despite these frictions, many queer practitioners are finding ways to reimagine leadership. Some groups use consensus decision-making. Others rotate roles or refuse fixed titles. A few reject the binary entirely, invoking deities that shift, blur, or defy embodiment.

Zara described their ideal circle as:

“A place where no one is above, but everyone is radiant.”

These experiments are not always smooth. They require time, trust, and a willingness to sit with discomfort. But they point toward new models of sacred authority—ones rooted in listening, adaptability, and mutual recognition.

Power, in these spaces, is no longer about who calls the quarters. It’s about who feels held. Who gets to arrive fully. Who is allowed to transform the ritual, and who is allowed to be transformed.

**5.6 Conclusion: The Sacred, Complicated**

This section has traced how power circulates in Montreal’s neo-Pagan communities—not only through titles or lineage, but through tone, gesture, absence. Even in spaces that claim to be liberated, the sacred can reproduce the social. Rituals that aim to heal can still wound. Circles that welcome can still exclude.

But to name these tensions is not to dismiss the work. It is to honor it. Queer practitioners are not naive. They know that inclusion is never a guarantee. But still they gather. Still they call, invoke, remember.

Their practices do not erase power—they queer it. They offer new ways to lead, new ways to listen, new ways to cast and be cast. The circle, like the world, remains imperfect. But it also remains possible.

The next section lifts us beyond the circle—toward a queering of magic itself. It asks not only how queer bodies fit into ritual, but how queerness rewrites the very grammar of enchantment.

**6. The Queering of Magic: Epistemologies of Enchantment and the Politics of the Irrational**

This final section is not a conclusion, but a detonation. It moves from ritual to cosmology, from the structure of the circle to the ground it is drawn upon. It takes the queer sacred seriously—not as a site of inclusion within existing religious frameworks, but as a form of insurgent knowledge that destabilizes the very distinction between religion and magic, between sense and nonsense, between reality and the real.

This section contends that to queer magic is not simply to decorate the altar with rainbows or expand deity pronouns. It is to contest the intellectual, colonial, and theological assumptions that have long rendered certain spiritualities illegible. It is to say: magic is not primitive, nor feminine, nor naïve. It is a technology of queer survival. It is a method. It is a way of knowing.

**6.1 Magic as Queer Epistemology**

What is magic, if not the refusal of fixed categories? What is queerness, if not the embrace of fluidity, contradiction, becoming?

For many practitioners in this study, magic is not a metaphor. It is real. But not in the way that scientific realism defines reality. Magic is real because it works—it transforms. It creates change. It alters perception. It makes the impossible momentarily tangible.

Maria, a nonbinary medium, explained:

“Magic is how I survived trauma. It gave me a framework that said: you are not broken, you are porous. You are not lost, you are in between worlds.”

Magic in queer Pagan life functions as an epistemology—an embodied theory of how the world works. It is grounded not in proof, but in presence. It values intuition, affect, synchronicity. It welcomes the nonverbal, the messy, the uncanny.

To embrace magic is to embrace the irrational—not as failure, but as resistance to normativity.

**6.2 The Colonial Afterlife of the Magic/Religion Divide**

Anthropology, theology, and sociology have long policed the boundary between magic and religion. Religion was said to be collective, rational, ethical; magic individual, manipulative, primitive. These binaries did not emerge in a vacuum—they emerged through colonial encounters, racial hierarchies, and gendered ideologies.

As scholars such as Styers (2004), Taves (2011), and Tambiah (1990) have argued, the very structure of “religious studies” has been predicated on the exclusion of magic. The witch, the cunning woman, the ecstatic, the animist—all have been cast as “other.”

Queer practitioners today inherit this exclusion. When they speak of deities that shift gender, spells cast with hair and spit, or altars built in drag, they are often dismissed as unserious. Yet it is precisely these practices that generate new ways of relating—to spirit, to land, to self.

To queer magic is to recover it—not as a lost tradition, but as a suppressed epistemology. One that values aesthetics over logic, desire over discipline, becoming over being.

**6.3 Techne and Transformation: Magic as Method**

Magic is not only a belief system—it is a methodology. A praxis. A set of techniques for navigating the unknowable.

In this sense, queer ritualists are not simply spiritual subjects. They are theorists. They are experimenting with the alchemy of metaphor and the architecture of desire. They are crafting cosmologies that resist totality.

Helena, a transfemme witch, described her altar as “a lab for reality hacking.” For her, a sigil was not a symbol—it was a force. A charged glyph that made a different future momentarily accessible.

These practices demand a scholarly response that matches their fluidity. To study magic is to be enchanted by it—to risk transformation. It is to write in ways that bend genre, rupture linearity, and invite the reader into the circle.

**6.4 Aesthetics of the Unknowable: Glamour, Camp, and Ritual Excess**

Queer magic is often spectacular. It is glitter and smoke, wigs and feathers, runes drawn in eyeliner. It does not hide its theatricality—it revels in it.

Critics might call this superficial. But for practitioners, glamour is sacred. Camp is consecration. The aesthetics of ritual excess are not ornamental—they are ontological.

In a Beltane rite I attended, a drag priestess invoked Venus in full sequin regalia while a butch drummer called down Mars. The circle erupted in laughter, then tears. For a moment, the erotic and the divine were indistinguishable.

This is not parody. It is liturgy. It is ritual as art, and art as spell.

The sacred here does not come from solemnity. It comes from affective intensity—from the ability to make meaning shimmer.

**6.5 Enchantment as Political Refusal**

In a world structured by disenchantment, magic becomes a refusal. A way of saying: not everything can be explained, tamed, capitalized. Queer enchantment is not escapism—it is insurgency.

By casting spells, building altars, invoking deities who cross binaries, queer practitioners assert a different kind of relationality. One that is not governed by productivity or coherence. One that makes room for mystery, pleasure, grief, and joy.

Ritual becomes protest. Glamour becomes strategy. Magic becomes not what is unreal, but what remains unruled.

**6.6 Conclusion: Magic, Queerness, and the Future of Knowledge**

This section has argued that magic, far from being marginal, is a central mode of queer world-making. It is not an addition to religion—it is a challenge to its foundations. It is not a soft form of belief—it is a sharp tool for critique.

To queer magic is to queer knowing. It is to value the felt, the fabulated, the fabulous. It is to insist that knowledge can emerge from the margins—from ritual, from drag, from desire.

If earlier sections asked how queers navigate sacred space, this section asks how queerness remakes the sacred altogether.

The circle does not close here. It expands. The altar is not cleared—it multiplies. The spell is not finished—it continues.

This is not a conclusion. It is a glamour.

A glamour that shimmers with the possibility of a sacred yet to come.

**CONCLUSION**

**The Circle Remains Open: Toward a Queer Sociology of the Sacred**

This essay began as an ethnography of ritual and identity and, in its unfolding, became something larger: a sustained meditation on the sacredness of queer world-making and the methodological implications of taking enchantment seriously. Through sustained fieldwork in Montreal’s neo-Pagan communities, deep engagement with feminist, decolonial, and queer theories, and a commitment to ritual not just as object but as epistemology, we have followed how LGBTQ+ practitioners do more than adapt spiritual frameworks—they reshape the very grammar of the sacred.

What emerged is not a story of easy inclusion. Nor is it a tale of radical rupture. It is a cartography of friction. Neo-Pagan spaces—particularly those structured by Wicca and Reclaiming Witchcraft—offer potent tools for gender affirmation, symbolic play, and cosmological healing. But they also reproduce the very binaries and hierarchies they seek to overcome. In that contradiction lies the ritual labor of queering: not purification, but improvisation. Not transcendence, but transformation.

This essay thus contributes to three scholarly conversations: pagan studies, the sociology of religion, and queer studies. In each case, it does so not by merely adding queer subjects to existing paradigms, but by rethinking what counts as knowledge, what constitutes ritual, and how power animates spiritual life.

**In Dialogue with Pagan Studies**

Within Pagan studies, this essay insists on the centrality of queer voices—not as anecdotal or exceptional, but as constitutive. Much of the existing literature on Paganism has focused on its revivalist, feminist, ecological, or esoteric dimensions. While crucial, these analyses have often sidelined questions of gender fluidity, sexual politics, and racialization. This work expands the field by demonstrating how queer practitioners are not simply participants in neo-Paganism, but its theorists, critics, and remakers.

By attending to moments of misrecognition, ritual refusal, and symbolic reimagination, this ethnography complicates overly celebratory narratives of inclusivity in Pagan traditions. It asks difficult questions about sacred legitimacy, theological authority, and the inheritance of binary metaphors. And it foregrounds practitioners who don’t just cast spells—they queer them, revise them, and sometimes reject the very cosmologies they were trained in.

The essay also offers a methodological intervention. It argues for a Pagan studies that is not only descriptive but transformative—one that treats ritual as theory, and enchantment as knowledge.

**Contributions to the Sociology of Religion**

To sociology of religion, this work offers both data and critique. It documents a contemporary spiritual movement that exists outside institutional frames, doctrinal orthodoxy, and census categories. It traces how queer practitioners assemble meaningful spiritual lives through improvisation, aesthetic labor, and embodied experimentation.

But more fundamentally, it challenges the epistemological boundaries of the discipline. Classical sociology—from Durkheim to Weber—has long drawn a line between religion and magic, belief and performance, legitimacy and deviance. These distinctions were never neutral. They were structured by colonialism, patriarchy, and rationalist bias.

By centering magic as a queer epistemology, this work destabilizes the very categories through which religion has been sociologically known. It demands that we take seriously the knowledge produced in ritual—not only as affect, but as analytics. It insists that religion is not just institutional—it is intimate, fragmented, affective, and sometimes flamboyant. It invites sociologists to listen differently: not only to beliefs, but to gestures, glitches, and glitter.

**Enriching Queer Studies**

In the context of queer studies, this essay insists that the spiritual is not an afterthought. Too often, queer theory has foregrounded critique of normativity, embodiment, and futurity while leaving religion and spirituality to the sidelines—or treating them with suspicion.

This essay offers an alternative: a queer studies that is not afraid of the altar, the chant, the trance. It treats ritual not as superstition, but as performance theory in motion. It explores how queer and trans practitioners use magic not as metaphor but as method: to rework kinship, to heal trauma, to mark transition, to grieve the ungrievable.

It contributes to queer thought by expanding its terrain—from the bedroom and the archive to the sacred circle. It does not abandon critique, but it pairs it with curiosity. What if queer world-making is not just about disruption, but also about invocation? What if the future is not only imagined, but summoned?

**Ritual as Method, Magic as Critique**

Throughout this essay, we have followed rituals in their most intimate and most theatrical moments. We have witnessed sacred scripts rewritten, roles reimagined, and symbols queered. We have seen how drag can be a liturgy, how misgendering can fracture a rite, and how sacred space can be a site of both healing and harm.

The work of queer Paganism, as this essay shows, is never just expressive. It is cosmological. It is ontological. It is political.

And for scholars, it is also methodological. We must now ask: How do we research what resists language? How do we write rituals that weren’t made to be transcribed? How do we stay with the trouble—not to resolve it, but to learn from it?

Queer Pagan ritual is not a performance of resistance. It is resistance as performance. It does not aim to be coherent—it aims to be real. In the cracked voice of an invocation, in the trembling hand holding a candle, in the laughter after the glitch—something sacred happens. Something that deserves to be studied, not as anomaly, but as revelation.

This is not a conclusion. It is an opening. The circle, as many rituals proclaim, is cast beyond time and space. It does not close—it transforms. The altar is not cleared—it is multiplied. The spell is not broken—it is shared. So let us return—again and again—to the sites of queer enchantment. Let us study them not only with theory, but with breath. Let us let the ritual mark us, unsettle us, inspire us. The queer sacred is not an object. It is a relation. Let the relation remain. Let the circle remain open. Let the queer sacred speak.
