Abstract

Witchcraft and witch hunting formed a social and intellectual tradition in early
modern Europe. Arguments persist as to what extent that tradition was
invented. This article reviews the schools of thought and directions within the study
of European witchcraft in the period between 1450 and 1750. The contributions
of modern scholars from the nineteenth century up to the present are reviewed.
Key arguments, important publications, and representative research, together with
teaching resources are covered. This historiographical review focuses on three issues:
the understanding and exercise of power across the social, political, and religious
cultures of early modern Europe; the prevailing problem of inconsistency hindering
the development of a single explanatory model; and thus the advantage of
micro-history.

In 1961 one expert on the history of European witchcraft suggested ignoring
much of what had previously been written on the subject.1 That advice was
corroborated.2 With some exceptions a considerable amount of unreliable
work had appeared. Moreover far too much investigation depended upon
secondary sources rather than acquaintance with the archival primary sources.
Basic flaws in historical methodology crept into the early period of writing
on the subject.3 Happily, these deficiencies were largely reversed from the
1970s. A tendency to focus almost exclusively on witch trial records had
been adjusted. Assumptions that late medieval and early modern Europeans
were grossly superstitious underwent modification as a result of careful
analysis. Recent trends have shown more clearly essential differences between
witchcraft and witch trials.4 Despite these advances, the history of witchcraft
is still plagued by misperceptions.5
The early modern European phenomenon of witch hunting took shape
against the background of one of the most creative and dynamic periods in
the history of western civilization. In the midst of progress and human
advancement arose another more sinister reality producing pandemic fear
and persecution.6 In the year 1600 an experienced witch trial judge
hyperbolically commented:

There are times in the history of humankind when demons appear to
flourish.8 This reflects in part the ethos of the sixteenth and seventeenth
centuries. The witch hunts were on. An argument can be advanced
supporting a logical foundation for the rise of demonism and witchcraft.9
Efforts to suppress sorcery may also be considered a factor.10
One basic concern for witch hunters was the definition of a witch and
witchcraft though that eventually proved to be rather elastic. In 1580 Jean
Bodin defined a witch as “one who knowingly tries to accomplish something
by diabolical means.”11 Belief in the presence of the Devil appears
fundamental for witchcraft in its European dimensions. The early modern
European witch cannot exist without the Devil for witchcraft is the Devil’s
work. Without the concept of the Devil there can be no witches.12 Some
modern approaches limit the definition of a witch to individuals entering
into formal agreements with the Devil, sealed by sexual intercourse, the
possibility of aerial flight, the observance of the Sabbat, and the practice of
maleficia – harmful magic.13 Such definitions mirror majority views in parts
of early modern Europe.
Approaches to the study
Of considerable importance for the study of this subject is an inquiry into
the essential mental assumptions making witchcraft possible.14 One of the
crucial areas has been the identification of an apocalyptic mood at all levels
of society.15 The study of witchcraft and witch hunting in the early modern
period involves most of Europe: from Iceland to the Balkans, Russia to
Portugal, Scandinavia to the Mediterranean.16 Witchcraft historiography
considers numerous topics including, but not limited to, history,
anthropology, magic, popular superstitions, religion, theology, law,
psychology, sexuality, gender, sociology, medicine, politics, language, popular
beliefs, folklore studies, and popular culture.
Anthropology has been especially useful bringing to the study the caution
that cultural relativism is important and may be more useful than interpreting
historical events from modern perspectives.17 Privileging anthropology
uncritically however is no asset. The notions of conflict, social change, and
stereotyping are emphases contributed from sociology.18 Beyond this, avenues
of psychoanalytic outlook have been adopted from psychiatry and
psychology.19 While there are clear advantages from such perspectives, undue
reliance can produce unfortunate distortion.20
There is further a semantic approach to the understanding of this historical
phenomenon wherein particular attention is given to the language of the
narratives – how things were said and became part of a discourse – and the

In this respect, linguistic and literary perspectives are relevant.
One approach to the study of witches is to submit witchcraft beliefs to
empirical verification and see if they correspond to the activities of real
people in normal circumstances. The answer to that inquiry is often
negative. The existence of “witchcraft” is deemed a non-entity, on the
grounds it has no referential in the real world. Therefore, witchcraft is
dismissed as irrational or mistaken. That approach begs the question. One
might examine witchcraft and witchcraft beliefs, and ask why such ideas
were held, often rather firmly, in the face of obvious unlikelihood. In terms
of a scholarly discipline, there are several approaches in witchcraft
historiography and these are shaped to a certain extent by the peculiarities
evident in different geographical areas. For example, in France the idea of
demonic possession seems to prevail as a central motif.23 In Spain, the
profusion of popular magic and the lack of intensive interest in the matter
on the part of the Inquisition seem crucial.24 In England, the presence of
harmful magic – maleficia – must be stressed along with the apparent absence
of diabolism and the Sabbat.25 In places such as Finland and Iceland the
predominance of male witches present other challenges.26
Several intellectual approaches to the subject are apparent. The Rationalists
form one school of thought dealing with the subject. Adherents of this
perspective regard witchcraft beliefs as the result of delusions. Embedded in
this approach are convictions that witchcraft as reality constitute the artificial
invention of those who stand to profit from its prosecution. Such invention
takes root in the misuse or misunderstanding of Scripture and a
commensurate development of aspects of scholastic theology (both exegetical
and ontological). This foundation receives facilitation and support from
inquisitorial fanaticism and a Christian Church unwilling to deny the powers
of Satan in the world. Numerous challenges to this narrow line of thinking
have appeared in the past thirty years.
A second school of philosophical thought – the Romantics – contend
that witchcraft amounts to actual occurrence, even though the matter may
be presented in distorted fashion. These possible occurrences are viewed
either as persistent non-Christian motifs or endemic resistance and dissent
within Christendom. Considerations of social order, power, and control are
at issue. Of course, there had to be individuals and groups in late medieval
and early modern Europe fervently believing the charges against so-called
witches were absolutely and literally true.27
Thirdly, one can identify another approach typified by the social scientists
who perceive the history of hunting witches as a product of enormous strain
and stress. These factors were often endemic in early modern societies
struggling to deal with the significant implications of social changes brought

After the 1960s the prevalence of rationalism – espoused by H.
R. Trevor-Roper in his seminal essay and extending all the way back to
Henry Charles Lea and Joseph Hansen (each discussed below) – began to
yield to numerous explanatory models – some rather ambitious. A tendency
began to emerge which regarded societies engaging in witch hunting and
the persecution of witches as fundamentally flawed. The 1970s witnessed
the development of a deep interdisciplinary assessment of the matter with
disciplines like social psychology, anthropology, sociology, and historical
methodology joining hands around the mysteries of the witches.
Interpretation slowly took shape from these interdisciplinary efforts.
Serious considerations of culture entered the fray. At the same time advances
in cultural anthropology began to discount earlier rational explanatory views.
Scholars started entertaining the possibility and probability that late medieval
and early modern European societies operated under a different cultural
rationality. These were contrasted with impulses governing societies in the
twenty-first century. In other words, the thinking of one age is not
necessarily subject to the same rules as the mentality of another.28 This
sentiment echoed a half century ago began to resonate with new clarity. The
unthinkable in historical records – in this case witch hunting – presents itself
as a possible point of entry into an alien mentality.29 E. P. Thompson’s
epigrammatic caution urges scholars to make every effort to avoid the
“enormous condescension of posterity.”30 That challenge remains valid.
Since the 1960s, witchcraft historiography has been revolutionized.
Tremendous advances can be observed in the scholarly knowledge of the
subject, in specific understanding, useful synthesis, parameters of
interpretation, and the use of source materials. Even geographical and
chronological dimensions have been expanded.31 The foundations for these
new approaches of interpretation have been built partly by multi-member
interdisciplinary teams. There are several notable examples. The “Witchcraft
and Sorcery in the Netherlands” group met from 1982 for about a decade.32
In 1985, the “Arbeitskreis Interdisziplinäre Hexenforschung” began at
Stuttgart, Germany and continues to function. In Hungary, a witchcraft
research team has operated for about twenty years. In France, the project
“Histoire critique de la sorcelliers” associated with the École Normale
Supérieure de Fontenay/Saint-Cloud has been working since the 1990s. At
the University of Lausanne in Switzerland a series of publications within
the Cahiers Lausannois d’histoire Médiévalé have appeared. There are other
active workshops in Belgium, Denmark, Luxembourg, Norway, and
Scotland. In the United States, Pennsylvania State University Press, in
conjunction with the Societas Magica, have been producing a series titled
“Magic in History” edited by Richard Kieckhefer while the University of
Pennsylvania Press has published a valuable and relevant multi-volume
work.33 In Britain, Manchester University Press regularly publishes

Modern historiography
The academic study of witchcraft in its modern dimensions dates to the
mid-nineteenth century. Two publications mark a scholarly watershed. Wilhelm
Soldan’s (1803 –69) history of the process against witches became the first
major history of the subject. Johann Grässe’s (1814 – 85) great annotated
bibliography marked the first effort to assemble a modern bibliography of
witchcraft materials.35 The scholarly work of Jules Michelet (1798 –1874),
Joseph Hansen, and Henry Charles Lea can be linked to these publications.
Hansen and Lea are discussed below. Michelet regards witches as heros and
heroines of folk medicine or victims of feudal oppression. He asserts peasant
devil worship (falsely so-called) was in fact a sublimated form of social revolt
suppressed by the religious power of the Church.36 Michelet’s work must
be understood against the rise of renewed Jesuit activity in nineteenth-century
France, his personal Huguenot heritage and avowed anti-clerical philosophy.
His views on witchcraft reflect his aversion to organized Christianity and is
an attempt to portray witches as a unified protest against an overbearing
Roman Catholicism. By the turn of the twentieth century a burgeoning
field of historiography known as “witchcraft” had emerged.37
Two generations later,American scholars began the work of summarizing
the achievements and challenges of the inquiry into witchcraft and its
subfields.38 There are several works which elevate the field of witchcraft to
the strata of historical consciousness on a broad level: Trevor-Roper, Baroja,
Robbins, Mandrou, and Manselli are good examples.39 All of these were
nevertheless subjected to withering critique and excoriated on several levels.
For example, Trevor-Roper was widely ridiculed while Mandrou’s thesis
endured critique for virtually bristling with fallacies.40 The latter’s work
constituted a premature attempt at synthesis. Notwithstanding this, by the
late twentieth century, witchcraft was firmly ensconced as a serious field of
study within the historical discipline with an ever-widening scope of
coverage.41
Witchcraft historiography achieved an early benchmark in the work of
Joseph Hansen (1862 –1943), an archivist at Cologne, who stood squarely
within the rationalist historiographical tradition. Committed to pre-World
War I cultural optimism, Hansen identifies the accumulated beliefs of sorcery
and heresy as comprising witchcraft in its fifteenth-century concept. He
established what was then the historiographical axiom that the Malleus
Maleficarum functioned as the pivotal text for the rise of witchcraft. This had
the effect of suggesting most witch hunters were motivated by this single
text. That assumption has been refuted. His combined efforts shaped the
direction for substantial research on witchcraft in the twentieth

Other German researchers during the Third Reich made unexpected

of the SS called the Hexen-sonderkommando.This unit eventually accumulated
more than 30,000 dossiers of German witch trial summaries. The materials
survived the war and are useful in several respects.43
On the other side of the Atlantic, steeped in the rationalist tradition
established in Europe and represented principally by Hansen, the Americans
Henry Charles Lea (1825 –1909), George Lincoln Burr (1861 –1942), and
Rossell Hope Robbins (1912–90) produced influential studies. Each regarded
witchcraft as a theologically conceived cognitive construct rooted in heresy
and hegemonically imposed upon society by corrupt religious authorities,
especially the Inquisition. The Church controlled the exercise of power.
From this perspective witchcraft is viewed as the result of superstitious fear
and the use of torture to elicit confessions.44 Lea particularly understands
witch trials as an outgrowth of medieval superstitions coupled with the
widespread use of judicial torture. He based his opinions on a deep reading
of the demonological texts and trial records. He became convinced
prosecuting authorities were seeking power over others at worst, or at best,
were gullible in accepting the ideas of witches working malevolent deeds
aimed at the subversion of Christian society. The contribution of these
scholars, for all its irrefutable strength, suffers from the disadvantage of relying
almost exclusively on documentary evidence – texts, treatises, and trial
records – without recourse to the social contexts or the contributions of
other disciplines, especially sociology and anthropology. This myopic
approach produced results which can neither be legitimately nor meaningfully
applied across the discipline. It is impossible to dissent from the view that
Lea, for example, was unable to meld vastly different sources from many
perspectives into a coherent explanatory matrix.45 Robbins’s sceptical account
of witch hunting can be understood partly against the background of
McCarthyism at mid-century.
Ironically, just as the rationalist paradigm was taking root on both sides
of the Atlantic, a proverbial monkey wrench was thrown into the study of
witchcraft. The Egyptologist Margaret Murray (1863 – 1963) turned her
attention to Europe arguing witchcraft was a continuation of pagan cults
originating in Egypt surviving throughout Christian history before coming
to prominence in the seventeenth century. By contrast Christianity was a
religion of the minority suppressing the majority. The influence of Michelet
is apparent. Witches were really members of an ancient pre-Christian fertility
cult, originating in Egypt and surviving as a dominant religion in Europe
until the seventeenth century. This fertility cult devoted to a horned god

Any serious objective analysis of her work reveals uncritical use of the

Montague Summers (1880–1948) also presents an alternative
understanding. By most accounts he was a religious fanatic obsessed by
thoughts of the Devil and constantly finding evil and the servants of Satan
under every rock and bush of history. Horrified, yet fascinated by stories of
witches, Satan-worship, orgies, and tales of the most alarming conspiracies,
Summers wrote prolifically on the subject of witches and translated
witch-hunting manuals when he was not preoccupied with vampires,
werewolves, the Marquis de Sade, and other prurient and scholarly interests.
Summers held stories of the witches to be entirely factual and deeply
disturbing. He steadfastly maintained witches were real, did what they were
accused of doing, were organized, devoted to evil and working to subvert
Christianity. The particular form of Catholicism espoused by Summers –
more medieval than twentieth century – is a critical factor for understanding
his work. Witchcraft was “real” and a manifestation of demonic worship.49
Summers cannot be regarded as a historian in any meaningful sense of the
term. Both he and Murray wrote in reaction to the rationalist perspective.
While it cannot be said that either the ideas of Murray or Summers made
any deep impact on the scholarly mainstream of witchcraft historiography
it is definite they established popular conceptions. The same cannot be said
for the work of the Spaniard Julio Caro Baroja (1914 –95) whose interest
centers mainly in social history and anthropology. Concerned with
understanding the nature of reality, his study became an epistemological
investigation. The result offered a penetrating analysis of the social and
intellectual worlds of witches and accusers. He argues different societies at
various times operate under different ways of thinking. Therefore, by
extension, an alternative concept of reality governs witch-hunting societies.
His work outlines the long and complicated theological process whereby
pagan and popular beliefs were demonized.50
In 1967 the study of witchcraft received a major impetus while reaching
a historiographical apogee. In that year the British historian H.
R. Trevor-Roper (1914–2003) published his long essay on “The European
Witch Craze” offering a social explanation for the witch hunts positing a
social psychology based upon thin air in mountainous regions causing
hallucinations which effectively reduced witch hunting to a “crusade against
the Alpine peoples.”51 That point did not find acceptance though it should
be noted Trevor-Roper, unlike Baroja, was not particularly interested in
popular beliefs. He argued for organized witch hunting related to the wars
of religion in Europe which declined when new ideas about the world and
nature took hold. Trevor-Roper was the first to point out the witch craze

paradigm. Clearly wrong on many issues, his pioneering essay nevertheless
did much to stimulate interest in the subject and it elevated the importance
of witchcraft and witch hunting for the consideration of central issues in

Coming on the heels of Trevor-Roper, the work of British scholars Keith
Thomas (1933–) and Alan Macfarlane (1941–) became an exercise in applying
functionalist social anthropology and the African anthropological literature
to European witchcraft.52 According to their findings, witchcraft as doing
harm was endemic to communities and society. Their careful research reveals
that beliefs in sorcery and witchcraft-related activities were key elements in
the imaginative world of popular culture. Witchcraft found definite roots
in social conflict and in the struggle for order and power. Thomas perceives
witchcraft accusations as falling into patterns consistent with social tensions.
His imposing study is a significant achievement in the social history of ideas.
For his part, Macfarlane does not attempt to explain why people believed
in witchcraft but concentrates instead on how witchcraft functioned in
society once the basic assumptions about witches were established and
accepted.
Both Thomas and Macfarlane incorporate principles from the important
work of the Oxford anthropologist Edward E. Evans-Pritchard (1902 –73)
who drew a distinction between sorcery as a learned skill and witchcraft as
innate power. Both works effectively link witch hunting to communal
conflicts stemming from a refusal to extend charity and a result of the trauma
in the transition from feudalism to capitalism. Having prevailed for a
generation, that view is now under question for being somewhat too narrow
as an explanation for the witch hunts. Research into witch trials in Essex
and Kent suggests much broader explanations than previously allowed by
the Macfarlane-Thomas model.53 Moreover, recent research, especially that
of James Sharpe, calls into question the assumption England is an exception
to the European model of witch hunting. That notion has been entrenched
in the historiographical traditions. Now it appears in considerable danger of
being permanently dislodged.54
By the early 1970s, the diversity of witchcraft and its prosecution had
become so well established scholars began to recognize the need for
concentrated micro-historical studies. Thomas and Macfarlane limited their
inquiries geographically and chronologically. One of the earliest and best
regional studies into the problem of witch hunting in Europe was that of
H. C. Erik Midelfort (1942–) who deals with the matter in terms of a
confessional interpretation of witch trials in the German lands by investigating
Baden-Württemberg.55 Midelfort maintains the phenomenon of witch
hunting was not used confessionally, that is by Catholics against Protestants
or vice versa. This holds true for Midelfort’s area of investigation but it can
be shown to be inapplicable for other German areas. By extension,
Midelfort’s study suggests less disparity between Catholic and Protestant

structures are partly to blame for witch hunts. Nevertheless, his work proved

In the same year Midelfort’s work came to light, Jeffrey Burton Russell
(1934 –) began to argue for witchcraft as a form of heresy amounting to an
organized anti-Christian religion.56 Issues of religious power are crucial for
Russell. The first part of the argument has merit but the disadvantage in the
streamlining approach Russell champions tends to create distortion since
the subject of witch hunting is so multifaceted that attention to a central
theme – however useful – cannot but distort. He is the first to mount a full
scale treatment of the subject. A number of his conclusions do not hold up
well after thirty years and he was taken in by the forgeries exposed so
convincingly by Norman Cohn just after Russell’s book appeared. Closer
inspection reveals a number of inconsistencies in the effort to articulate a
grand theory. His understanding of the relationship between witchcraft and
heresy remains valid though the forgeries exposed by Cohn (below)
weakened that linkage. Nonetheless, it is clear that religion, Reformations,
and widespread religious conflict created social upheaval and the initiatives
against the diabolical conspiracies of witches – carried out by Protestants
and Catholics – served to strengthen prevailing orthodoxy. Only when
ecclesiastical and civil authorities came to terms with pluralism in religion
did a commensurate decline in witch hunting occur.57 Notwithstanding this,
the Reformations helped to redefine both the understanding and exercise
of power in Europe.
Considerable residual ambiguity was summarily swept aside when Norman
Cohn (1915–) turned his penetrating attention to the subject. Demolishing
the Margaret Murray thesis entirely, Cohn cites the rise and political uses
of demonology linked with legal developments and the zeal of witch
hunters.58 For Cohn, belief in witches is rather like conspiracy theories.
Popular culture could neither devise nor sustain witch hunting. The whole
history is holocaust; a form of social pathology based on fantasy. There was
nothing usual, ordinary, positive or helpful about it. Cohn goes further
suggesting modern scholars writing in the twentieth century consciously or
otherwise project their own contemporary cultural views onto their subject
matter. His book eroded all credibility in certain hitherto sources exposing
the frauds perpetrated by Étienne-Léon Lamothe-Langon (1786 –1852)
debunking earlier ideas about witch hunting. Even the eminent Joseph
Hansen had accepted the fraudulent materials as genuine and many scholars
uncritically followed. Cohn’s book is not above reproach on several matters.

Working at the same time as Cohn, Richard Kieckhefer (1946–) tackles
the problematic relationship between the learned literary clerical class (those
who wrote the books) and the others (those allegedly holding nefarious
views and perpetrating “exceptional crimes”). With this “crimen exceptum”
in view as the focal point, Kieckhefer attempts to correlate the mental worlds
of both sides. He argues perceptions of witchcraft held by peasants and social
elites diverged widely. The result of his investigations produced a cautious
but essential study for understanding the early period and his work on the
witch trials in late medieval Europe goes some distance to establishing the
chronological patterns of witch hunting.61 Independent of Cohn, Kieckhefer
demolished the alleged evidence supporting major witch trials in early
fourteenth-century France. This had the effect that the scholarly repository
of sources was reduced and the subtraction of these sources altered prevailing
knowledge about witch trials. This meant it became quite impossible to
claim medieval Europe had any popular witch cult. The result forced a clear
“chronological revolution” in the history of witchcraft, one which forced
the problem out of the Middle Ages altogether and into the Renaissance
and early modern Europe.62
Accordingly,William Monter argues for linkages between witch hunting
and social changes brought on by the Reformations, the Counter
Reformation, and political upheaval. Responding to the work of Keith
Thomas and Erik Midelfort which describe very different witchcraft worlds,
Monter charts a course between the two in an effort to address that middle
ground.63 Agreeing with Mandrou that the French had their own particular
preoccupation with witchcraft, Monter’s exhaustive survey of the Jura region
concludes it does not fit with any of the western European paradigms. The
advantage of micro-history is apparent. In opposition to thriving trends in
the 1970s, Monter resists utilizing non-European comparative material
convinced it is useless. “All things considered, non-western social
anthropology provides keys that do not fit continental European locks.”64
Later, Monter did look more seriously at non-European materials.65

The careful work of Monter, Kieckhefer, and Cohn bore fruit
elsewhere. The work of the Scottish sociologist Christina Larner (1934 –83)
supports the groundbreaking efforts initiated by Cohn. Exploiting the
differences in social structure, law, and the Church as battlefields of power
and control, Larner explores the problem of witchcraft prosecutions. She
rejects the functionalist position which conceived of witchcraft as an
outgrowth of social strain. Instead, she argues for an intermingling of popular
and elite cultures. She rejects the impulses of the new feminist critique
concluding “witchcraft was not sex-specific but it was sex-related.”67 Instead,
she prefers to see accusations of witchcraft as effective tools of social control
illustrating the understanding and application of power.68 Against Thomas,
she reasserts the validity of studying witchcraft from above as opposed to its
purely social dimensions. She attributes witchcraft persecutions to the
politicizing of religion in general in the rise of confessionalism perceiving
Midelfort’s conclusions inapplicable to Scotland. It was Larner who
introduced the idea of national witch hunts. She accomplished this by
focusing on trials rather than actual executions. The query remains: were
these events ever truly national in scope? A close inspection of the evidence
suggests the national witch hunts proposed by Larner were often confined
to particular areas.69 Her untimely death precluded her work from reaching
maturity. Her contributions nonetheless were both influential and important.
Recent work on Scotland reveals the strengths and limitations of her
investigations.70
Elsewhere, effectively ignoring the work of Cohn and Kieckhefer, the
Italian scholar Carlo Ginzburg (1939 –) offers a surprising modification of
Murray’s thesis. He concludes his work with the assertion that there can be
no reasonable doubt on the matter of the existence of an “underlying
Eurasian mythological unity” existing and developing over centuries.71 He
sees the demonological stereotype of diabolic witchcraft based on “a solid
core of facts.”72 Ginzburg argues for elements of truth in witchcraft
theory. The benandanti (“good walkers”) of the mountain regions of northeast
Italy prove – in Ginzburg’s mind – witchcraft has ancient roots. Ginzburg’s
work refocuses the agendas of the demonologists and instead of creating
fantastic fiction, argues they were engaged in the task of attempting to
decipher (and in the process demonize) basic folk beliefs held from time
immemorial. What emerges from Ginzburg’s research is the undeniable
pattern of inquisitors misinterpreting popular ideas and distorting them –
consciously or otherwise – to fit their own preconceived ideas about
witchcraft and diabolism. Furthermore, the contest for control of knowledge
and power once again emerges. The rise of witchcraft is the result of
