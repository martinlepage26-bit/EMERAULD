Statement of teaching interests

Martin Lepage, Ph.D.
martinlepage26@me.com

After completing a master’s degree in literary studies, I entered the field of religious studies because
I wanted to work closer to those interested, at different levels, in the complexity of the way culture
and religion intersect. Scholars and religiously involved people have different things to say about
very diverse kinds of beliefs and practices. In crossing over fields of studies, I needed to figure out,
and very fast, how students were taught fundamental concepts about those very omnipresent issues
of life without them thinking that these were religious truths. As such, I was wildly interested in the
many ways in which different narratives are constu(ct)ed in diverse contexts (religion, gender,
education, identity) around objects of knowledge and power. It is a very amazing thing to talk about
religion as an aspect of culture, and it is a tremendous challenge to bring students to that place where
they can detach themselves from their faith and collaboratively think like mature students and even
future scholars.
My passion for learning is coupled with the desire to pass on what I have learned. In fact, it is a
matter of pride for me to teach others, in a very short and effective fashion, what and how I have
learned. University courses and other speaking opportunities, during which it was crucial to
communicate socio-scientific theories, methodologies, and necessary information in a short amount
of time, have allowed me to put this into practice using interactive methods and lively discussions
with and among the audience.
In fact, something else came into this willful need to communicate knowledge while making sure I
was understood. Indeed, ever since I could talk, I always had a stutter. More pronounced in my
younger years, it has now become a very important element for me to deal with whenever I face an
audience, whether composed of students or scholars. It is important because it allows me to be
honest with the audience, as struggles may occur during communication. Was I understood correctly?
What can I do to make sure I was? I found out with experience that it is always best to see students
as part of the communicative process. Certainly they are the ones being tested, but the exercise is
also mine, and, as such, it is my duty to make sure that every aspect of my classes engages everyone
in a learning relationship based on reciprocity.
I also found out it was often easier for me to communicate in English, as French pronunciation can
sometimes be a bit treacherous. That being said, I cherish my ability to navigate between two
languages, as I’m sure a lot of students in Montreal often have to do. It is also part of what allows me
to understand and adjust to different kinds of audience and levels of expertise.
For all of those reasons, I am continuously interested in looking into and talking about issues of
normativity. The queer approach I adopted for my doctoral thesis led me to question whether or not
certain topics deserved to be taught in a certain way, and others in other ways. Queer theory is a
constructivist approach to gender that questions one of the very first aspects of our identities as
individuals: are we man or woman? In fact, my goal is to bring students to ask questions like “How
can we speak of something without creating a norm?”, “How have our scientific categories
influenced the way we talk about certain matters in our schools today?”, or “How do I take part in
the stereotypes surrounding these important matters?”. Culture is not something that should only be
seen as mere entertainment, but mostly as a reflection of our everyday “mundane” life, as a lens with

In that sense, I often reflect on questions like “What does it mean to be a good teacher?” or “What is
a good class?”. I had the chance to meet great professors who modeled alternatives to traditional
lectures, and valued the teaching as much as the learning process. There is a great number of tools
available to help students be more involved in their learning (visual aids, music, cinema, social
media, performances, among many), and each class is a renewed opportunity for me to use them
properly, and at the right time, to allow the learning to flourish and the students to become more and
more autonomous.
In sum, I always aim to develop pedagogical strategies dedicated to teaching several principles from
diverse fields of studies in a dynamic and concrete way. I am also convinced that these strategies will
equally help students in other areas of academia and in a very day-to-day manner, inasmuch as they
learn to encounter new experiences with open eyes and critical but respectful words.
