**Stuttering through the Institution: On Queer Knowing, Academic Containment, and Refusal**

**Abstract**

This autoethnographic essay traces the layered exclusions faced by a queer scholar navigating academic life. Through moments of institutional betrayal, failed mentorship, public silencing, and symbolic containment, I examine how voice, stutter, and unconventional knowledge are policed in higher education. My defense became a site of both vulnerability and power. I reclaim my stutter not as a flaw, but as a pedagogical method. Questioning which forms of expression are considered legitimate, I argue that the university rewards fluency, conformity, and containment, while punishing disruption. To remain, to speak, and to resist, is to refuse erasure on one’s own terms.

**Keywords:** Autoethnography; Institutional Betrayal; Queer Methodology; Academic Containment; Epistemic Exclusion

**Word Count:** 7129

**Introduction**

In the official records, I received my doctorate without controversy. The defense was passed, the degree conferred, the institutional boxes checked. But the reality behind that paper trail was far more complex. I spent seven years in a university that prided itself on justice, equity, and critical inquiry, yet my presence, my research, and my voice were never fully trusted. I worked on contemporary Paganism and queer identity: topics well established in anglophone religious studies, but treated in my francophone department as strange, marginal, even unserious. My scholarship was rigorous, interdisciplinary, and grounded in lived experience. But it was met with discomfort, not celebration. I was allowed to remain, but never invited to belong.

This article is a reckoning with that experience. It offers a critical autoethnography of symbolic containment: the practice by which institutions tolerate marginalized scholars just long enough to extract value, while withholding access to power, authorship, and continuity. I trace how I was effectively denied a future in the university, not for lack of merit, but because of how I carried my knowledge. My queerness was too vivid. My research was too embodied. And my voice, literal and metaphorical, did not perform the smooth, neutral authority that academic culture rewards.

I stutter. I always have. In daily life, and especially under pressure. And in academic contexts, where fluency is often read as competence, my speech became an obstacle in the eyes of others. I could feel it in meetings, in presentations, in the tight smiles of professors who assumed hesitation meant unpreparedness. But my stutter was never a weakness. It was, and is, a mode of teaching. In the classroom, it slows things down. It asks people to listen differently. It lets me repeat what matters. It makes space for care. Yet institutions mistake disfluency for failure, for instability. My voice didn’t reassure them. It made them uneasy. And rather than question that unease, they wrote me out.

I was invited to speak in classrooms, but never offered a course to teach. I proposed syllabi that were later implemented, without my name attached. I was asked to contribute to a departmental journal, only to be publicly disavowed in a footnote that undermined my paper’s legitimacy. Other queer scholars, more palatable in presentation, were offered the very opportunities I had made visible. I was visible, but unendorsed. Completed, but not carried forward.

Even my doctoral defense mirrored this paradox. The first part was difficult. I stumbled over nearly every word. I felt the audience hoping for my survival. But in the second half, the part where your committee asks questions, challenges the foundation of your work, I came alive. I answered with clarity, conviction, and depth. I saw the room shift. I watched my friends and family realize what I had built. For a brief moment, my voice, in all its broken cadence, carried everything. Still, I have never watched the recording. I don’t need to. That moment was real. I don’t want to give myself one more excuse to doubt.

What I offer here is not a story of misunderstanding. It is a theory of how institutions manage difference. The university I inhabited performed liberal values outwardly, while preserving structural conservatism inwardly. It welcomed difference in rhetoric, but policed it in practice. It did not fail to see my potential, it saw it clearly, and chose not to foster it.

This article unfolds in three sections: first, I describe the ways my work, and my body, were marked as “not quite academic”; second, I recount key scenes of explicit rejection, including a departmental director’s pronouncement that I would “never teach here”; and third, I follow the afterlife of these betrayals into my current workplace, where I remain structurally underrecognized despite credentials and experience. Across these scenes, I reflect on how academic legitimacy is still built on fluency, conformity, and erasure, and on what it means to remain, to speak, and to refuse

This account is grounded in my lived experience, and I acknowledge its partiality. I was not present in the meetings where key decisions were made. I cannot access the motivations or unconscious biases of those involved. I recognize that perception is relational and affective, that I may sometimes read rejection where there is indifference, or betrayal where there is bureaucracy. I tend to see myself through others, and often interpret professional encounters with more warmth or tension than may have been intended. But these tendencies do not invalidate the patterns I trace. As Ellis, Adams, and Bochner (2011) remind us, autoethnography is not about neutrality or distance; it is about making meaning from the position of the affected. (Ellis, Adams, & Bochner, 2011, p. 276) In Holman Jones’s terms (2005), it is a method of critical storytelling that theorizes through lived contradiction (Holman Jones, 2005, p. 770). I do not claim to know the full story. I offer mine, as rigorously and reflexively as I can.

### **Methods and Framework: Speaking from the Fault Lines**

This essay emerges from a commitment to critical autoethnography as both method and mode of refusal. I do not present my story as data to be decoded, but as a form of situated theorizing, what Adams, Holman Jones, and Ellis (2015) describe as “personal narrative used to understand cultural experience.” My account is not singular, but spectral. It contains the echoes of others whose voices, like mine, have been shaped, distorted, or refused by institutions that pride themselves on progress but operate through protocols of containment.

My aim is not to generalize, but to reveal. Following Holman Jones (2005), I treat autoethnography as a critical performance of knowledge production that makes visible the affective, epistemic, and disciplinary forces that govern what, and who, counts. My narrative is composed of scenes: pedagogical exclusion, professional silencing, ambivalent recognition, and the slow sedimentation of institutional betrayal. These are not isolated events, but recursive enactments of what Smith and Freyd (2013, 530–539) term *institutional betrayal*: systemic failures of care within spaces that claim to be inclusive.

I approach this text through a queer-crip analytic, drawing especially on the work of Alexandre Baril (2020), Margaret Price (2011), and Sara Ahmed (2012). Their work enables me to name disfluency not as defect, but as epistemic form. My stutter is not simply a break in communication, it is a method. It slows down institutional time. It disrupts the smooth flow of performative competence. It resists conversion into professionalism. Through this lens, I understand my speech, and its refusal to be streamlined, as a form of embodied critique.

This essay is also shaped by the disciplinary edge of religious studies, queer theory, and critical university studies. My research on contemporary Paganism and queer ritual practice is not just the backdrop to this narrative, it is what positioned me as illegible within the academy. To study enchantment seriously, to speak from within spiritual worlds, to write with affect, was to violate unspoken codes of academic conduct. As Berlant (2011), Ferguson (2012), and Moten and Harney (2013) have argued, institutions do not simply exclude, they absorb, manage, and metabolize difference until it becomes docile. What cannot be metabolized is quietly expelled.

This paper therefore operates on multiple registers: it is testimonial, theoretical, methodological, and political. It is written from the threshold, where voice shakes, where silence accumulates, and where refusal takes shape not as rage alone, but as rigorous attention to the systems that misread us. I write not to settle accounts, but to unsettle the metrics by which accounts are usually measured.

**“Not Quite Academic”: Queerness, Magic, and Scholarly Illegitimacy**

In my first year of doctoral studies, I quickly learned that the legitimacy of a research topic in religious studies was not judged by its intellectual merit alone, but by its proximity to certain codes of seriousness. Studying queer Paganism, contemporary Wicca, goddess spirituality, techno-magic, non-binary ritual work, was, to many in my department, a niche indulgence at best and a scholarly embarrassment at worst. While the anglophone world had already embraced the ethnographic turn toward alternative spiritualities, influenced by scholars like Susan Greenwood, Sabina Magliocco, and Jone Salomonsen, the francophone academic community I was entering still viewed such material as unserious, overly experiential, or worse, self-indulgent.

What made my work especially suspect was not just *what* I was studying, but *how* I was studying it. I was not a distant observer. My methods were reflexive, participatory, and unapologetically queer. I approached ritual not as artifact, but as theory-in-practice. I drew on queer phenomenology, trans embodiment, affect theory. I dared to suggest that ritual aesthetics, gendered performance, and spiritual self-making among queer Pagans constituted serious data, not merely symbolic systems to decode, but vital forms of world-making that deserved to be studied on their own terms.

Yet, from the beginning, I encountered subtle but relentless resistance. Faculty members and students who nodded politely in seminars would later warn me to be “careful” about seeming “too immersed.” Others questioned whether my topic was “really about religion” or if it fell more under anthropology or gender studies, a disciplinary displacement that served to deflect responsibility for my supervision. The implication was clear: my work didn’t fit. It was too embodied, too emotive, too entangled with the lives it described.

This disciplinary discomfort was not just methodological. It was deeply political. To study queer Paganism as a serious religious practice, and not as a new age eccentricity, was to challenge entrenched assumptions about the nature of religion, authority, and legitimacy. It meant asserting that gender non-conforming witches, techno-mystics, and femme ritualists deserved the same scholarly attention as clerics, theologians, or monks. It meant claiming that spiritual experience could be shaped by digital culture, queer trauma, erotic magic. And it meant writing as someone who was not ashamed to be affected by these worlds, who saw in them not just data, but a future.

But that future was ultimately denied to me. Over the course of my PhD, I was rarely, if ever, encouraged to imagine myself as part of the department’s intellectual lineage. I was not invited into conversations about curriculum, departmental planning, or pedagogical development. I was never asked to co-teach or shadow a course. I was, however, regularly invited to “give a talk”, to present fragments of my work, always framed as fascinating but ultimately peripheral. I was permitted to appear, but never to anchor. I was treated as an exception: novel, perhaps even admirable, but not replicable. Not the kind of scholar one builds an institution around.

And yet, there were moments when my voice pierced through the containment. When I spoke in classrooms about Wicca and contemporary Paganism, I sometimes sang the chants I had heard during fieldwork, ritual songs passed through circles, covens, and queer spiritual gatherings. In those moments, my voice, so often read as unstable, became resonant, rhythmic, powerful. I was not just speaking about magic. I was invoking it. The atmosphere would shift. Students would lean in. Theory gave way to embodiment. The chant was a method, a citation, a situated epistemology. And for a brief moment, the classroom became a ritual site. I remember those moments not as performance, but as clarity: I knew exactly what I was doing. So did the students. Whether the faculty did, or wanted to, was another question.

When I defended my thesis, three people stood before me, each one mirroring a different relation to my work. My supervisor, a queer academic who had supported me through the long arc of this research, sat there steady and invested. Alongside them was a brilliant feminist theorist who challenged me throughout the writing process, but ultimately respected my project and recognized its scholarly value. The third, an older gay man and professor from my former university, embodied a more ambiguous energy. He appreciated my writing, even found it seductive, but his interest stopped at aesthetics. He eroticized the novelty of my work without fully understanding its politics or depth. A fourth jury member was absent, for reasons of health. His absence added a strange weight: an unseen judge whose silence did not liberate, but introduced doubt, his inability to fulfill his role became another gap in the chain of institutional recognition I was seeking to complete.

The first part of my defense was hard. I stumbled over nearly every other word. I could feel the room rooting for me, or maybe cringing with a kind of projected sympathy. I felt watched, not just academically, but existentially. As one of my friends was filming the event from the back, all I could imagine was the split between my unstable voice and the solidity of my argument. I was defending rigorous, layered, politically urgent research, but in a tone of voice that academia reads as uncertain. It felt like delivering brilliance in a language they had no patience to understand.0

But then came the second part. The part where the jury asks questions, not to trap you, but to test the foundation of your work, to engage its scaffolding. And there, I lit up. I didn’t flinch. I remember feeling like: try me. Ask me anything. I’ll show you what this work *does*. So I did. The audience shifted, I felt it. Friends and family were watching. Students, colleagues. I saw eyes widen, bodies lean forward, people suddenly understanding the *why* of it all. This wasn’t a quirky thesis about witches and queers. This was intellectual power, embodied, reasoned, sacred, political.

My supervisor smiled. The feminist theorist nodded, energized. Even the older gay professor looked, if not transformed, then at least momentarily disarmed. As for my family, they saw me shine in a way they never had before. Not despite my stutter, but through it. Not in the voice they expected, but in one that carried the weight of lived thought.

Despite that positive deployment of energy, to this day, I haven’t watched the video of my defense. I don’t need to. I don’t want to give myself one more excuse to doubt. That moment existed. I lived it. They saw me. I saw myself. It was real.

This disavowal of scholarly legitimacy is not unique to my case. It reflects a larger academic tendency to valorize certain modes of knowledge production while pathologizing others. As Sara Ahmed (2012, p. 143) argues, “the institution gives recognition only to those who do not cause trouble.” Research that troubles disciplinary norms, by centering lived experience, challenging dominant theologies, or queering methodologies, is often marked as excessive, unprofessional, or unmanageable. It becomes “too much”: too embodied, too emotional, too spiritual, too queer.

In this framework, queerness itself is not the problem, as long as it is properly aestheticized, depoliticized, or channeled into the right rhetorical register. The university will celebrate queerness when it is digestible. But when queerness becomes methodological, when it refuses to disavow its intimacy with the sacred, the erotic, or the speculative, it triggers a defensive institutional response. The queer scholar who does not know her place, who insists on collapsing the boundary between research and enchantment, theory and praxis, is rendered suspect.

In response to this phenomenon, I learned to translate myself, to code-switch in committee meetings, to use the language of “symbolism” instead of “ritual”, toeference Foucault instead of Starhawk, and to pretend I was simply describing these practices, not shaped by them. But translation is not neutral. It is a form of epistemic compromise. Each time I rephrased my work to meet institutional expectations, I felt the edges of my research fray. I was still speaking, but I was no longer heard.

Despite these accommodations, the suspicion never lifted. My doctoral work was never openly denounced, but it was never fully welcomed either. I existed in a space of perpetual ambiguity: visible, but not affirmed. Advanced, but not supported. This ambiguity, this refusal to name the discomfort for what it was, was perhaps the most violent part of all. There was no moment of open rejection, only a slow process of containment, a gradual silencing through non-invitation, non-promotion, non-recognition.

This is how the institution disciplines scholars it cannot assimilate. Not through overt censure, but through neglect. It allows you to finish, but not to flourish. It awards you the credential, but not the community. It grants the degree, but withholds the path that degree was supposed to open.

What emerges from this experience is not simply a personal grievance, but a structural insight: that the neoliberal university produces symbolic inclusion through strategic disposability. It wants your research as long as it can extract value from it, but it does not want *you*, not in your fullness, not in your unruliness, not in your ritual garments or glitchy grammar. It wants the queer gesture, not the queer demand.

To be “not quite academic” is not a failure. It is a misrecognition, imposed from above. And it is a haunting that follows many of us, queer, disabled, racialized, spiritual, emotionally vivid, through every space we enter. We carry it into our post-PhD careers, into clinical research offices, community organizing spaces, public scholarship. It becomes a wound, but also a warning system. It teaches us how to recognize institutions that say yes with their mission statements and no with their structures.

In this section, I have mapped the early contours of institutional illegitimacy as they were imposed on me during my doctoral research. In the next, I turn to the moments when that illegitimacy was made explicit, when containment became condemnation, and erasure became policy.

**“You Will Never Teach Here”: Exclusion as Policy, Not Exception**

There are moments in academic life when the unspoken rules of exclusion become briefly, brutally articulated. The dissimulation drops. What had previously been experienced as unease or opacity is suddenly made legible as policy. It is in these moments that institutional betrayal ceases to be an atmosphere and becomes an event, a crystallization of power in a single phrase, an encounter, a decision already made. For me, two such moments stand out: one disguised as hospitality, the other disguised as collegial guidance. Both revealed what the institution had never said aloud: *you are not wanted here*.

The first took the form of an invitation. The director of my department asked me to join him for lunch. At the time, I interpreted it as a sign of possible inclusion, a gesture that perhaps the institution was reconsidering my marginal status. I prepared carefully. I was nervous, but hopeful. I wanted to be taken seriously. What I did not realize was that I was not being invited into a relationship of mentorship or collaboration; I was being evaluated. The lunch was not a conversation. It was a covert audition. Sitting in the teacher’s lounge, a space already coded as exclusionary for graduate students, I became aware that I was being watched not for the content of my research, but for my comportment, my voice, my “fit.”

I obviously stuttered a lot. I often do when I’m anxious, especially in formal or high-stakes situations. But stuttering, like other forms of embodied difference, is rarely treated as a neutral fact in academia. It is misread as intellectual uncertainty, emotional instability, or worse, unprofessionalism. A voice that hesitates is a voice that does not dominate, and therefore a voice the institution does not trust. Weeks later, my supervisor told me that the director had shared his verdict with the entire faculty: *“He will never teach at this university.”*

That sentence was not a prediction. It was a decision. It transformed my stutter from a personal trait into a professional liability. It marked me as unfit, not based on my scholarship, teaching, or merit, but on a five-minute performance of social acceptability. That moment confirmed what I had suspected all along: I was not being measured by the quality of my thought, but by my ability to perform normative academic subjectivity. I failed that test, not because I wasn’t good enough, but because I wasn’t *smooth* enough, *masculine* enough, *neutral* enough. Because I was *too much* of something they couldn’t name, and didn’t want to.

But my stutter has never been an obstacle to me. It is not a failure of communication, it is a form of attunement. In the classroom, it slows things down, demands care, creates rhythm. It becomes a way to repeat what matters, to pause where emphasis is needed, to invite students into a listening that is not rushed, not transactional. It brings the body into the room. It models imperfection as pedagogy. My stutter reminds students that speaking does not require domination, that to teach is not to perform mastery, but to foster connection and vulnerability as strength. Where the institution saw liability, I rather saw method.

The second moment came when I asked a question during a student conference I had organised. At the time, a course in the department, closely aligned with my doctoral research, had no assigned lecturer that semester. It was, from my perspective, an obvious opportunity. I had expertise. I had already been invited to guest lecture on similar topics. Students had responded well. Feeling in my element, at ease in my own university, I approached the professor overseeing women’s studies, under whose jurisdiction the course fell, and asked, carefully, whether I might be considered to teach it. Her response was instantaneous and sharp: *“You’re paranoid. You don’t know what you’re talking about. Let me do my job.”*

The violence of that sentence was not in its volume, but in its finality. To be told I was paranoid was to be framed as delusional. To be told I didn’t know what I was talking about was to have my epistemic credibility invalidated. To be told to let her “do her job” was to be reminded, unequivocally, that I had no place in the decisions that shaped my own professional future. That encounter foreclosed not only that teaching opportunity, but all future ones. I was no longer seen as a viable pedagogical presence. I had become, in their eyes, a problem.

These moments were not isolated. They were institutional performances: affective and rhetorical tools used to discipline a scholar perceived as out of bounds. The lunch masquerading as mentorship and the conversation framed as correction were both tactics of containment. They allowed the institution to maintain its liberal self-image, no one was fired, no one was formally censured, while enacting exclusion through soft violence. What Wendy Brown (2015, pp. 131-133) describes as the “stealth revolution” of neoliberal rationality is visible here not in policy documents, but in the micro-relational scenes where potential is denied in the name of propriety.

In fact, what is being protected in these scenes is not academic excellence, but *academic decorum*. I was not cast out for being wrong, but for being *wrongly formed*, for failing to perform authority in a way that reassured those who wielded it. This is what Sirma Bilge (2013, p. 409) refers to as the “palatability of difference”: the way institutions claim to welcome marginalized perspectives, but only when they are articulated in ways that conform to dominant aesthetic and affective norms. My queerness, my fieldwork with magical communities, my ritual-informed writing style, my emotional intensity, and even my stutter, these were all deviations from the expected academic body.

There is a cruel irony here. The same department that treated me as marginal prided itself on its commitment to social justice. It celebrated equity in its mission statements, promoted diversity in its branding, and published work on feminist theory, race, and gender. But the liberal university often functions most insidiously precisely when it believes itself to be virtuous. As Ahmed (2012) notes, the performance of institutional goodness is often used to deflect accountability. “We are not the kind of place that discriminates” becomes the shield against recognizing how discrimination actually functions: not through explicit policy, but through the gatekeeping of emotion, speech, and visibility.

The outcome of these encounters was unambiguous. I was never invited to teach again. I was never considered for a course, a fellowship, or a postdoctoral role. My ideas were recycled in curricula without attribution. Other scholars, queer, yes, but more “professional”-seeming, were granted access to opportunities for which I had paved the way, and I was left with a degree that had no institutional home. I was credentialed, but not endorsed, marked as “completed,” but never trusted.

The betrayal continued, quietly, under the guise of recognition. After defending my PhD, I was asked, presented almost as a consolation prize, to oversee an issue of my department’s academic journal. I accepted, and in good faith wrote both the editorial introduction and contributed an article on magic: a piece that, in my eyes, operated as a sixth chapter to my dissertation. The reviewers responded with comments that veiled misunderstanding in the language of scholarly rigor. They spoke with the authority of expertise over a field, contemporary magic and queer spirituality, that I had spent years researching firsthand. I revised the piece, reshaped it carefully, preserving its essence while addressing their concerns. I submitted the final version late, but with clarity and intention. The editorial board responded: there was no time for further revision. The deadlines had the final word.

When the issue appeared, my article was accompanied by a footnote, one that read like an institutional disclaimer: *“The direction wishes to thank the contributors and reviewers for their work. The author, unfortunately, chose to maintain their article as is.”* What should have been a mark of publication became instead a public rebuke, a distancing, and bureaucratic shrug. They had decided to publish it not for its intellectual merit, but out of expediency. Not because it was right, but because it was late. Not because it belonged, but because they couldn’t justify its removal in time. It was an editorial afterthought, appended with a gesture that said: *We do not stand by this.* It was a betrayal masked as inclusion, a publication turned reprimand.

These events were not the result of one person’s bias or one bad decision. They were the articulation of a collective refusal, encoded through institutional habit. In this section, I have shown how exclusion can take the form of politeness, evaluation, and dismissal disguised as advice. In the next, I examine how these exclusions follow us beyond the university, shaping our roles, silencing our authority, and restricting our trajectories long after the final defense.

**Credentialed but Controlled: The Afterlife of Academic Disposability**

Institutional betrayal is not a closed wound. It does not remain confined to the site of its occurrence. Rather, it lingers, mutates, and embeds itself in the body’s memory, in the texture of workplace interactions, and in the rhythms of self-assessment. When a university marks you as disposable, not through expulsion, but through the quieter rituals of non-recognition, that judgment tends to follow. It trails behind your name, whispers through hiring decisions, and lives on in the ways you are seen or not seen in subsequent roles. This is the afterlife of academic exclusion: its capacity to shape not only what you *were*, but what you are *allowed to become*.

After completing my doctorate, I transitioned into clinical research. After years away from anything academic, it was a pragmatic move, a way to remain intellectually engaged, to continue working within a field that valued ethics, inquiry, and social change. In many ways, the work has been meaningful: I contribute to HIV and STI prevention studies, collaborate with dedicated clinicians, develop protocols, write reports, and coordinate ethics reviews. That kind of research is urgent and impactful, and I am good at what I do.

Yet, the hierarchy is unmistakable. Despite holding a PhD, I remain in a subordinate role, structurally beneath directors who, like me, hold only a master’s degree in literature. Unlike me, however, they were never marked as professionally unfit. Unlike me, their bodies, voices, and demeanors never threatened to exceed the bounds of acceptability. Their authority is assumed. Mine must be continually negotiated, often invisibly, with the memory of prior exclusion pressing against my voice.

There is no formal reason for this stratification. On paper, I am more qualified, but the logic of institutional containment operates through more than credentials. It operates through *reputation*, *personality*, *fit*. In the aftermath of my doctoral betrayal, I find myself once again coded as too intense, too critical, too queer, too hard to manage. The same affective misreadings that once marked me as an unsuitable teacher now frame me as a capable but unpromotable researcher, good enough to do the work, not quite the right shape to lead it.

The subtle erasures persist. In my current role, my doctoral title is often omitted from official documents, internal communications, clinical protocols, even ethics forms like informed consent documents. The abbreviation “PhD” disappears without comment, as if the degree were an exaggeration, a mistake, or worse, an inconvenience. I’ve asked for corrections. Sometimes they are made, sometimes not. What lingers is not just the oversight, but the cumulative message: that my credential is fragile, conditional, revocable. That despite my training, I remain someone whose authority is not to be formalized. The erasure is bureaucratic, yes, but it is also symbolic. It says: *we see you working, but not as a doctor.*

These hierarchies aren’t just about credentials, they’re also about how we communicate. Why is writing seen as more serious than speaking? Why is a fluent voice considered smarter than one that stumbles or pauses? If a deaf person signs their needs, are they not communicating something just as true? If someone listens to an audiobook instead of reading the printed text, are they learning less? These questions matter, because institutions often decide which forms of expression are valid, and which are not. My stutter was never a lack of knowledge. It was a different way of delivering it, with slower rhythm, a way of making sure I was understood.

As Baril (2020, p. 45) argues, institutions often uphold communicational ableism by demanding fluency, speed, and emotional control as markers of epistemic legitimacy. A stutter, in this framework, is not simply a disfluency, it is a refusal. It interrupts the tempo of academic performance. It unsettles the presumed link between clarity and authority. My stutter has always carried knowledge, not despite its breaks, but through them. It marks a different temporality of thought, one that privileges repetition, breath, and uncertainty. Following Baril’s queer-crip analytic, I understand my stutter as epistemic resistance: an embodied challenge to the norms of intelligibility the university silently enforces.

This is how institutional violence reproduces itself in non-academic spaces. It is not about one job or one missed opportunity. It is about being tethered to a narrative of failure that was written by someone else and published without your consent. It is about being kept just below where your expertise should place you, and being told, again and again, but more politely, that you must wait, grow, adapt, mature. That you must continue to prove yourself in ways others were never asked to.

The deeper wound, perhaps, is what all this containment does to one’s internal landscape. After years of institutional betrayal, it becomes difficult to trust your own voice. The feeling is not just exclusion, it is impostor syndrome cultivated by design. I question myself even when I know I’m right. I anticipate being misunderstood. I hesitate before I speak, not because I lack conviction, but because I have been trained to expect punishment for it.

This impostor feeling is not individual pathology, it is cultural and systemic. Academia breeds it, and then pathologizes those who suffer from it. In the university, mental health is rarely named unless it can be weaponized. There is little space to speak openly of depression, anxiety, fatigue, or grief, especially for those who are racialized, queer, disabled, or otherwise precarious. The unspoken rule is: if you are struggling, you are failing. Failure, in neoliberal academia, is a moral flaw.

These conditions are not equally distributed across generations. Many of the senior faculty who policed me remained in their positions for decades, protected by tenure and confident in their authority. They held onto power long past the point of relevance, often blind to the damage their presence inflicted. Meanwhile, my generation, trained under the illusion that merit, passion, and hard work would be enough, found itself locked out. We did everything right. We were told there was no room. We believed in the university longer than it believed in us.

In this sense, the betrayal of academia does not end with the degree. It metastasizes. It becomes a structure of feeling, a disposition of precarity, a chronic low-level suspicion that you are still, somehow, unworthy. This is what Ann Cvetkovich (2012, p. 13) refers to as the “archives of trauma”: the ways in which affective injury accumulates, sedimenting into the body and psyche, shaping what feels possible, speakable, or safe. Even in spaces that should offer reprieve, the damage echoes. And the memory of having once been discarded haunts every new attempt to belong.

Moreover, the neoliberal workplace, like the university, is rarely innocent. It inherits the same logics of stratification, the same obsession with affective legibility, the same aversion to disruptive knowledge. Research institutions are happy to hire queer or racialized staff so long as we do not question structural authority. Diversity is welcomed in form, not in function. Queer perspectives are valued when they enhance grants, when they are branded as inclusion, when they stay in their place. But when those same perspectives demand accountability, call attention to epistemic violence, or critique the top-down logic of institutional authority, they are once again treated as threats.

This dynamic is particularly evident in how “leadership” is constructed. In my current role in clinical research, those deemed leaders are not necessarily those with the most insight or skill, but those who conform to a certain affective profile: confident but not loud, assertive but not confrontational, personable but not too idiosyncratic. They speak fluently in the language of sponsors, ethics boards, and public health forums. They do not stutter, hesitate, or make the room uncomfortable.

Despite having once trained to be a professor, despite having written and defended a complex, theoretically rich dissertation, despite having contributed to academic and public discourse on gender, religion, and queerness, I find myself in a subordinate role to people who, structurally and intellectually, I should be alongside, if not above. This is not bitterness, it is recognition. It is the result of being told, early and often, that my voice is *too much*, and learning to navigate the world as if that were true.

In conversations about professional development, the same refrains return: “You’re doing great work.” “We need people like you.” “Just be patient.” But patience has its own violence when it is only asked of the marginalized. Patience becomes a synonym for endurance. It asks us to stay in place while others are lifted. It asks us to keep proving what should already be self-evident.

What I have come to understand is that institutional betrayal is not an isolated act. It is a continuum. It begins with the silencing of queer, spiritual, or disabled scholars in the university. It continues through the soft barriers to leadership in adjacent fields. It crystallizes in every promotion denied, every meeting redirected, every idea attributed elsewhere. Mostly, it survives through silence, because to name it is to seem bitter, paranoid, difficult. The exact traits that disqualified you in the first place.

But I refuse that silence. I am naming this structure for what it is: a system of containment disguised as professionalism. A mechanism that rewards legibility over insight, loyalty over imagination, smoothness over truth.

In the final section, I will return to the university, not as a site of lost belonging, but as a system whose contradictions demand exposure. I will argue that the betrayal I experienced was not personal, but structural, an intentional disavowal of scholars who cannot or will not make themselves palatable to the institution. I will offer, not a solution, but a theorization of how symbolic containment functions as a mode of academic governance, and what it might mean to speak, to write, and to refuse in spite of it.

**Conclusion – They Gave Me a PhD to Shut Me Up: On Containment as Institutional Method**

This article has charted the trajectory of a scholar who was never meant to belong. From the subtle early markers of exclusion in graduate school, through explicit statements of rejection, and into the muted repetitions of underemployment in professional research settings, I have mapped the recursive operations of what I call symbolic containment, a mode of institutional governance that disciplines bodies, ideas, and affects that exceed the narrow bounds of academic legibility. Symbolic containment is not about outright dismissal. It is about the slow, methodical neutralization of those who dare to bring unruly knowledge into polite spaces.

In telling this story, I have deliberately blurred the boundaries between scholarship and testimony, autoethnography and critique. Not as a literary device, but as a refusal to separate knowledge from embodiment, theory from pain, research from politics. What happened to me, what is still happening to me, is not a matter of unfortunate circumstance or miscommunication. It is the predictable result of institutions designed to extract brilliance from the margins while refusing to share power with those who dwell there.

Throughout this piece, I have referred to institutional betrayal 0 not simply as a psychological phenomenon but as a structural method. It is a betrayal that occurs not in violation of norms, but in their fulfillment. I was betrayed *not despite* my institution’s stated commitment to equity, but *because of it*. The performance of liberal values, diversity, inclusion, openness, functions as a cover for the maintenance of entrenched hierarchies. The university I attended, like many others, welcomed my presence as a symbol of progress, but never intended to sustain my trajectory. I was brought in to diversify the room, not to reshape it. The limits of my acceptability were predetermined.

This is why containment is a more accurate descriptor than exclusion. I was never fully cast out. I was allowed to remain, as long as I remained silent, grateful, and small. My stutter, my queerness, my ritual methodologies, my insistence on theorizing the sacred alongside the political, these were not banned, but slowly edged out of relevance. I was permitted to perform, but not to plan, to contribute, but not to lead, to finish, but not to flourish.

In the most striking act of containment, I was invited to oversee a special issue of the department’s academic journal shortly after defending my dissertation. I wrote an introduction to honor my contributors and submitted a piece on contemporary magic, an extension of my doctoral work. The reviewers misread it, misnamed its framework, and approached it as though they were the experts on a domain I had built from within. Still, I revised, with care. The final version was submitted late, but with integrity. It was accepted. Yet when the issue appeared, it came with a footnote, on my article alone, clarifying that the department “unfortunately” published it as submitted. Not for its scholarly merit, but to meet a deadline. What should have been recognition became censure. The university published me with a disclaimer, a printed doubt, a betrayal bound in the very pages that should have held me.

It is important to distinguish containment from failure. I did not fail. My research was sound, my teaching effective, my writing rigorous. What failed was the institution’s ability, or rather, its *desire*, to make good on the promise of academic justice. I was denied advancement not due to incompetence, but due to what I represented: a challenge to academic neutrality, a refusal to divorce theory from flesh, a queerness that would not be softened into style.

The mechanisms of this containment were often affective. I was told I was paranoid, unprofessional, difficult. These labels served a dual purpose: they discredited my claims while preserving the institution’s innocence. This is what Sara Ahmed (2021, p. 287) has described as complaint as killjoy work, the way those who name institutional violence are reframed as the problem. In such a structure, to resist erasure is to become complicit in your own marginalization. The only safe option is silence. But silence is its own form of death.

What lingers most powerfully in the aftermath is not just anger, but fatigue. The exhaustion of constantly recalibrating oneself to become acceptable. The daily labor of assessing how much queerness, how much magic, how much voice to allow. The quiet grief of watching others, with fewer credentials but more normative presentation, move forward in structures you helped to build, using ideas you grew from seed, speaking words you once whispered in class.

My stutter, too, has remained with me, not as a wound, but as a form of method. What they mistook for hesitation was care. What they coded as weakness was presence. In every repetition, I offer students a second chance to hear. In every pause, I mark the moment as alive, unfinished, shared. The university failed to see this, failed to understand that disfluency can be a mode of pedagogy, an opening rather than a lack. As disability scholar Margaret Price argues, “slowness, silence, or halting may signal not a failure to know, but a different form of knowing” (Price, 2011, p. 27). My voice may not be smooth, but it is deliberate. It makes space. It makes meaning. It resists the smooth violence of institutional time.

If there is a method in this madness, it is the method of containment through symbolic overexposure. I was allowed to speak in classrooms, but not to teach. My work was read, but I was not trusted to lead. My difference was instrumentalized, but never nourished. This method is devastating precisely because it is deniable. There is no smoking gun. Just a steady erosion of possibilities, a slow foreclosure of futures. Yet, I remain, writing, naming, refusing to vanish.

What I offer here is not a roadmap to reform, though I believe reforms are needed, but a call for recognition without redemption. This story is not here to be fixed. It is here to be recorded. Because what happened to me is happening still: to queer scholars, disabled scholars, Black and Indigenous scholars, trans scholars, spiritual scholars. To those whose very presence demands that the institution rethink its foundations, and who are, in turn, punished for the discomfort they provoke.

In that sense, my voice is not singular. It is spectral. I speak with and through the voices of others who have been silenced, stalled, managed, and marked. We were never not good enough. We were never the problem. The problem is the institution’s inability to imagine success outside its own reflection.

They gave me a PhD to shut me up. But I am still speaking, not as a token of survival, but as a signal flare.
