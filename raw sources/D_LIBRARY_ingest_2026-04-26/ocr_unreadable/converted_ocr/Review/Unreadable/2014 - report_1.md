Gender, Identity and Material Culture: Negotiations of Tradition
St. Lawrence University
Instructor: Martin Lepage, Ph.D.
martinlepage26@me.com
Undergraduate level
100 students
Course description:
What does the latest fashion using esoteric or native designs say about Western culture? What about
television shows reinventing and marketing, year after year, traditional fairy tales? Or bodies, which can
either suffer from tradition, deflect it, transform it, subvert it, or preserve its integrity? A bearded drag
queen singer like Conchita Wurst, controversial winner of the 2014 Eurovision Song Contest, is another
striking example of the way things matter. Furthermore, in our digital era, objects and material culture
keep mutating faster and faster. Very common and trivial objects can now hold great value to certain
people, whereas others that used to be sacred or most-prized are often represented in new and less
typical ways. In fact, the understanding of material culture as a series of social constructs points directly
to power relationships between tradition and invention, heritage and adaptation, or those who define the
objects and those who use them. What can this change in the kind of relationships we engage in with
objects and material things such as books, tattoos, clothing, nature, cooking tools, ritual tools, building
tools, etc., show in regards to gender or socio-political norms? What does it show in the light of what
technology and social media allow us to do?
This course will look at the relationship between the study of material culture and matters of identity.
With a primary focus on gender, it will examine the ways in which objects take part in multiple aspects
of life, as vectors of control and subversion. If objects can be seen as living things in a cultural sense,
they are invested with great normativity, and at the same time the potential for performing profound
transformation in the way that knowledge, identity, and the sense of community can be transmitted
through generations. But why do we give meaning and value to objects? What kind of heritage can they
hold? How does gender come into play with the way we relate to and use objects? How do they affect
us, and help enact social status or the relationship between identity and community? This course will
develop answers to these questions in a three-fold way: first by showing how material culture studies
can lead to a better understanding of culture and its study in the humanities; then by going deeper into
the power relationships determining the roles and functions of objects in everyday life and in society in
general; finally by showing how objects, as ever-changing living things, impact the very foundation of
normativity around ethics, politics, and the construction of knowledge and science today. The course
will end on a reflection about the study of material culture, and the epistemologies surrounding it.

This course focuses on interdisciplinary concepts and approaches regarding gender and material culture,
as they take part in a variety of different phenomena. It introduces students to varied literature in the
fields of material culture and related social science disciplines. Bridging the too-often neglected gap
between them, and capitalizing on their diversity, it will advance the understanding of how objects and
material culture not only speak about the people who made them, and the different intent and power
relations behind their making, but also about their purpose today, and how they are transformed and/or
preserved by those who value them.
Attendance and participation:
It is expected that you attend and be on time for all classes. Failure to attend class will adversely affect
your grade, since participation in the classroom discussions are vital to the course.
Assignments and grading:
Grading will be based on: a take-home mid-term exam, covering the first half of the term (one-page
answers to three (out of five) questions); two ten-question quizzes will serve to monitor your
engagement in the reading and the course content; a cumulative final exam, (two-page answers to three
(out of five) questions); a ten-page final paper, on a topic of your choice (approved by the instructor five
weeks before the deadline). The quality of your writing will account for 20% of each grade. The final
10% of the course grade will reflect the level of your active participation in class (regular attendance,
punctuality, preparation for discussions). At any point during the semester, I can meet with you by
appointment to discuss grades or any concerns regarding assessment. Each evaluation will count as
follows:
Take-home mid-term exam: 20%
Two quizzes over the term: 20%
Final exam: 20%
Final paper: 30%
Participation: 10%
Intellectual Integrity:
The integrity of your academic work and intellectual thought is valued and respected in our class and at
St. Lawrence University as a whole. Plagiarism comes in many forms and will not be tolerated by any
definition. Please learn about the various policies and consequences on the university website. This is an
important first step in becoming a responsible member of St. Lawrence University’s academic and
social community.
