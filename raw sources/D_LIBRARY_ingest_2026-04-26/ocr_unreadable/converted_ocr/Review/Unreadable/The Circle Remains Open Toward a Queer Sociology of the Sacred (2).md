**The Circle Remains Open: Toward a Queer Sociology of the Sacred**

"It is difficult to speak of action in the world without simultaneously suggesting that our actions are the expression and product of a self that endures behind each of its roles."

— *Erving Goffman (1991, 287)*

"What we know is not 'the world in itself' nor 'the world for us,' but the way in which we construct, through action, our knowledge of the world. The empirical world, in both its material and ideal dimensions, is a constructed and signified given."

— *Alex Mucchielli (2005, 31)*

**Introduction**

This is an essay about witches. But not the kind that hide in fairy tales or flicker briefly across Halloween decor. These witches are real, complex, laughing, wounded, fierce. They are queer and trans, urban and solitary, lineage-bound and iconoclastic. They invoke spirits, call down the moon, chant against pipelines and patriarchy. They gather in basements, in forests, on Zoom. They know the world is broken, and they cast spells not to escape it, but to touch it differently (Muñoz 2009; Johnson 2020).

Contemporary Paganism does not speak in dogma but in gesture, affect, and symbol. It is less a single religion than a spiritual cartography—a terrain of overlapping mythic maps, revived pantheons, seasonal rites, and sensual devotions (Adler 2006 [1979]; Hutton 1999; Magliocco 2004). In Montreal, this terrain takes on a particular contour: one shaped by linguistic hybridity, queer presence, and post-secular yearning (Browne 2015; McGuire 2008. Here, neo-Paganism is not merely inherited; it is conjured, claimed, stitched together from fragments of folklore, feminist politics, ecological urgency, trauma legacies, and digital subculture (Berger 1998; Ammerman 2007; TallBear 2019. It is both ancient and future-facing, fiercely embodied and relentlessly fluid.

And yet, within these expansive, enchanted spaces, tension persists. Binaries endure: Goddess and God, male and female, the chalice and the blade. Sacred texts are often unwritten, but their scripts are deeply gendered (Pike 2001, 47–49; Berger 1998, 124–127). Ritual form, even when improvised, can reproduce the heteronormative logics of lineage, polarity, and mythic balance. These moments—of misalignment, of discomfort, of reconfiguration—are not anomalies. They are sites of knowledge. They are the queer sacred made visible (Butler 1990; Halberstam 2011).

How, then, do queer and trans practitioners move within such frames? How do they craft livable, magical lives at the margins of tradition? How do they refuse not the sacred, but the terms under which it is usually understood? This essay explores those movements—the ritual negotiations, symbolic reworkings, and embodied subversions through which LGBTQ neo-Pagans in Montreal queer the sacred, and are themselves reconfigured by its force. But more than that, it explores the epistemological stakes of taking such practices seriously. It asks what happens to the category of “religion” when those who have been cast as magical, heretical, or excessive begin to narrate their own theologies (Taves 2009; Styers 2004; Ammerman 2007; TallBear 2013).

Between October 2015 and December 2020, I entered this world not only as a researcher, but as a participant-witch. My fieldwork unfolded across full moon circles in basement temples, public rituals in community art spaces, and sweat-laced Beltane dances beneath open sky. I was present at initiation ceremonies, seasonal festivals, political spell-castings, and moments of conflict and reparation. I interviewed twenty practitioners in depth, but learned from many more—in whispered conversations around fire pits, through zines passed from cloak to cloak, in workshops on consent magic, ancestor reverence, and trans deity invocation (Behar 1996; Abu-Lughod 1993; Haraway 2016). The bodies and stories in this essay are drawn from these encounters: textured, fragmentary, and radically alive.

Some were solitary practitioners crafting rituals in bedrooms strewn with tarot cards and hormone vials. Others were high priestesses of longstanding covens, carrying scars and pride from decades of activist witchcraft. Many moved between lineages, traditions, and roles. They were animists and agnostics, kinksters and caretakers. Some came to Paganism through feminist reading groups; others through Tumblr aesthetics, queer trauma, ayahuasca ceremonies, or inherited Celtic spirituality (Magliocco 2004; Berger 1998; McGuire 2008).

They were not always welcome. Some were misgendered during circle, asked to perform roles that denied their bodies, or pushed out of lineages that clung too tightly to binary gods (Butler 1993; Fraser 1997). But they stayed, or they returned, or they built new circles. They invoked trickster spirits, rewrote the Wheel of the Year, dressed as gods no one had yet named. They brought the sacred back from the edge, and refused to let it exclude them (Muñoz 2009; TallBear 2018).

My research situates itself within and against the growing literature on contemporary Paganism. Seminal works by Hutton (1999), Adler (2006 [1979]), Magliocco (2004), and Clifton (2006) have traced the historical and aesthetic genealogies of modern Witchcraft. Others, like Pike (2001) and Berger (1998), have begun to ask how Paganism interacts with gender, sexuality, and power. But few have placed queer and trans embodiment at the heart of their inquiry. This essay does not simply add LGBTQ voices to an existing frame; it recenters the frame around the ritual lives of those whose bodies, desires, and cosmologies do not fit (Sedgwick 1990; Halberstam 2011).

I draw from queer theory not aBs a topical lens but as a method of attunement. I am indebted to Butler’s performativity (1990, 1993), Sedgwick’s affective epistemologies (1990), Halberstam’s notion of queer failure (2011), and Muñoz’s call for queer futurity (2009). I also engage the anthropology of lived religion (Ammerman 2007; McGuire 2008), recognizing that sacred life happens not in creeds but in breath, touch, and rhythm. Feminist ethnography (Behar 1996; Abu-Lughod 1993) anchors my commitment to partiality, intimacy, and the politics of representation.

I write from within. My own queer, Pagan body is not outside the text, but one of its instruments. I cast circles, called quarters, invoked deities I did not always believe in. I shared meals, heartbreaks, and ecstatic dances with my interlocutors. Sometimes I was scholar; other times, initiate, ritualist, confidant. This positionality is not a methodological flaw but a condition of knowing (Haraway 1991; TallBear 2017). It shaped not only what I was allowed to witness—but also how I was transformed by it.

This doubleness allowed me to trace the micro-politics of sacred space: the moments when someone’s voice cracked during invocation, not from error but from transition; when gendered metaphors collided with lived ambiguity; when a participant sat quietly outside the circle, not from disrespect, but from dissociation. These moments are not disruptions. They are the material of this essay (Bell 1992; Butler 1993; Halberstam 2011).

This essay asks:

* How do queer and trans neo-Pagans in Montreal express gender, embodiment, and cosmology through ritual?
* In what ways do they reproduce, resist, or reinvent sacred scripts?
* How does queering the ritual also queer the very category of religion?
* What does it mean to treat magic not as superstition, but as method, as knowledge, as resistance (Styers 2004; Muñoz 2009; Taves 2009; TallBear 2019)?

Rather than impose coherence, I map contradiction. I attend to both the healing and the harm, the spells that liberate and the liturgies that constrain. I write with love, with ache, and with a deep sense of debt to those who trusted me with their stories (Behar 1996; Clifford 1997; Haraway 1991).

This first section of this essay situates Montreal’s Pagan landscape and the traditions most central to this study: initiatory Wicca, Reclaiming Witchcraft, and their local adaptations. Section Two outlines the theoretical scaffolding, with attention to queer embodiment, performativity, lived religion, and the politics of normativity. Section Three details the methodological approach, grounded in reflexivity, affective accountability, and ritual participation.

Sections Four and Five offer the core ethnographic material. section Four examines how queer identity is performed, affirmed, and negotiated within ritual. section Five explores how power operates in these spaces—who speaks, who leads, who is seen, and who is rendered peripheral.

Section Six widens the circle. It queers the very category of “magic,” positioning it as an embodied, decolonial, and gender-dissident epistemology. It argues that queer neo-Paganism is not only a ritual genre but a spiritual method—a cosmology of care.

The conclusion does not seal the spell. It breaks it open. It reflects on what it means to think, to write, and to listen from within queer enchantment. It insists that to study magic is to be touched by it—and to allow that touch to change the terms of knowledge itself (Sedgwick 1993; Muñoz 2009; Haraway 2016). This is not a conclusion, but an invocation. Let the altar remain unset. Let the story remain unfinished. Let the queer sacred speak.

**Queer Cartographies: Mapping Montreal’s Pagan Landscape**

Montreal’s ritual landscape is neither cohesive nor static. It pulses with the rhythms of the moon, flares with sabbats, and reconstitutes itself in borrowed halls, whispered blessings, queer flirtations, and flickering candlelight. This section situates that terrain within broader currents of contemporary Paganism, with a focus on initiatory Wicca, Reclaiming Witchcraft, and their local reinventions. It also re-reads this terrain through the lens of queer geography and spiritual bricolage.

To walk into a Montreal Pagan ritual is to step into a layered space. One might find a reclaimed church basement adorned with rainbow prayer flags, a park in the Plateau where a genderqueer priestess leads a Beltane dance, or a queer-inclusive shop on Saint-Laurent where workshops on ancestor reverence and trauma magic spill into conversations about hormones and heartbreak. The city’s Pagan landscape is not defined by temples, but by networks—of people, herbs, zines, playlists, gestures. It is improvised, relational, enchanted.

What follows is an attempt to map that enchantment—not as a fixed territory, but as a shifting constellation of practices, memories, and political commitments. This map is queer not only in content, but in form: it resists linearity, embraces contradiction, and centers those at the margins of both religious and academic legitimacy.

Contemporary Paganism in Montreal presents itself not as a codified religion, but as a plural, layered ecology (Adler 2006 [1979]; Berger 1998; Magliocco 2004). It is a spiritual milieu that holds together those who call themselves witches, priestesses, druids, animists, brujas, faeries, and sometimes nothing at all. It gathers those who believe in many gods, no gods, or gods they invent themselves. Most participants I met during fieldwork were less concerned with doctrine than with texture—how the sacred felt, how it smelled, what it allowed them to do or become (McGuire 2008; Ammerman 2007).

Rather than doctrine, what unites this field is practice. A transmasculine witch might host full moon baths for trauma release, mixing spellwork with therapy. A femme astrologer might open a ritual space with Beyoncé lyrics and offer honey to Venus. A two-spirit circle might blend Cree cosmologies with Wiccan form. What holds all this together is not a shared belief system, but a shared insistence: that the sacred is not dead, and that it can be touched (Haraway 2016; TallBear 2019).

The term “Paganism” has long been a site of projection—first a Christian category of exclusion, later reclaimed by countercultural movements (Hutton 1999; Styers 2004). In its modern form, Paganism is often used to describe spiritualities rooted in pre-Christian traditions, ecological attunement, polytheism, or Goddess worship. But in Montreal, Paganism becomes something else again: a queer archive, a speculative cosmology, a living bricolage (Browne 2015).

For some, Paganism is a return. For others, it is invention. Most practitioners I met held multiple truths at once: they honored Celtic ancestors and watched anime, invoked Lilith and practiced Reiki, wore sigils inked on skin and quoted queer theory during ritual planning. They knew the charge of the Goddess and the politics of the pronoun (Butler 1990; Sedgwick 1990). For them, Paganism was not a category, but a field of play—a place to try on power, to ritualize grief, to make the invisible tangible (Muñoz 2009; Halberstam 2011).

The use of the word “neo-Pagan” in this essay signals not just a temporal break but a methodological one: a shift from assumed lineage to intentional creation (Clifton 2006). These practitioners are not simply reviving ancient ways—they are remixing them, queering them, weaving them into queer lives that demand more than the binaries offered by both mainstream religion and mainstream LGBTQ politics (Pike 2001; Berger 1998; TallBear 2013).

If there is one ritual logic that defines this community, it is bricolage. Rituals are not inherited whole; they are pieced together from memory, internet threads, dreams, and community needs(de Certeau 1984; Magliocco 2004). A Samhain ritual might include Wiccan casting of the circle, Reclaiming-style collective singing, a moment of silence for missing and murdered Indigenous women, and a spontaneous dance to Florence and the Machine. The sacred emerges not from fidelity to a form, but from the charge of presence (McGuire 2008; Clifford 1997).

Zara, a nonbinary Reclaiming witch, told me: “My altar is a mess—Kuan Yin next to Aphrodite next to glitter pinecones I found in the Plateau. It’s all sacred. It’s all me.”

This eclecticism is not arbitrary. It is situated, affective, and politically alive. Rituals often respond to the needs of the moment: to grief after a hate crime, to celebration after a name change, to grounding before surgery, to honoring queer ancestors lost to AIDS, suicide, exile (Johnson 2020; Muñoz 2009). Sacred practice is not separate from social life—it is one of its modalities (Haraway 1991; TallBear 2018).

While many groups in Montreal embrace innovation, others maintain inherited structures. Wiccan covens often retain a symbolic polarity rooted in Goddess and God, chalice and blade (Adler 2006 [1979]; Hutton 1999). Even when the practitioners are queer, trans, or polyamorous, the ritual form can echo heteronormative logics (Berger 1998; Pike 2001). This can be deeply affirming for some and deeply alienating for others.

Lou (she/they), a trans femme practitioner, reflected: “I love the structure. It helps me feel held. But sometimes, I wish the Goddess had a penis, too.”

This tension between form and fluidity recurs throughout the essay. Some respond by rewriting scripts; others step outside the structure altogether (Halberstam 2011; Butler 1993). A few groups have abandoned the Great Rite or reimagined the elements as emotions rather than genders. Others work within tradition, queering it gently but insistently—invoking Hecate as a crone-god in transition, or calling in the directions not with binaries, but with verbs: to protect, to grieve, to open (Muñoz 2009; Sedgwick 1990).

Montreal is not just a backdrop—it is a participant in this story. The city’s linguistic and cultural hybridity, its activist legacy, its Catholic ghosts and queer pulses, all shape the form of ritual life (Clifford 1997; Browne 2015). Anglophone and Francophone groups mingle but do not always overlap. Some circles are explicitly bilingual; others code-switch within ritual. Postcolonial tension is felt in the land acknowledgements, the use of smudging, and the invocation of deities from non-European pantheons (TallBear 2019; Haraway 2016).

Spaces of ritual are often makeshift: artist studios, living rooms, parks, yoga spaces, anarchist bookstores. These spaces carry their own auras, constraints, histories. The sacred must be set up, and taken down. Altars are mobile. The circle is never permanent (Behar 1996; McGuire 2008).

This geography is queer not only in its inhabitants but in its structure: unstable, partial, under construction. Montreal’s Paganism is not a unified religion—it is a constellation of practices bound by breath, memory, and improvisation (de Certeau 1984; Clifford 1997).

This section has traced the outlines of a field that resists finality. Montreal’s neo-Pagan landscape is a ritual commons—messy, brilliant, frustrating, alive (Adler 2006 [1979]; Magliocco 2004; Ammerman 2007). Its traditions are both inherited and invented. Its forms both constrain and inspire. Within this field, queer practitioners do more than seek inclusion: they change the very conditions under which sacred life can be imagined (Butler 1990; Halberstam 2011; TallBear 2018).

The next section turns to theory: to the conceptual tools needed to read ritual not only as performance, but as power, as resistance, as world-making. In doing so, it continues the work of mapping a terrain that is always in flux—and always becoming (Muñoz 2009; Haraway 1991).

**Theory as Compass: Performativity, Power, and Queer Knowledge**

To understand the sacred worlds described in the previous section, we need more than definitions—we need tools. This section lays out the theoretical scaffolding of the essay: a set of conceptual instruments drawn from queer theory, ritual studies, feminist anthropology, and the sociology of knowledge. These tools do not simply illuminate ritual; they reveal the politics embedded within it. They allow us to read a chant as both invocation and refusal, a circle as both boundary and breach (Bell 1992; Butler 1993; Fraser 1997).

Theory here is not cold or abstract. It is warm-blooded. It comes from the body, the field, the altar. It does not explain away magic—it lets it speak. The thinkers I draw upon—Butler, Sedgwick, Bell, Fraser, Muñoz, TallBear—are not distant authorities but companions in this work of queer world-making. They help trace how identity is not discovered but enacted, how power is not absent in spiritual communities but often disguised, and how magic itself might be an epistemology (Sedgwick 1990; Muñoz 2009; TallBear 2013).

Nancy Ammerman and Meredith McGuire have argued for a shift in how we study religion: away from doctrine and toward practice. For them, religion is not primarily what people say they believe, but what they do—how they make the sacred felt in kitchens, bedrooms, protests, and spells (Ammerman 2007; McGuire 2008).

In the Montreal Pagan circles I studied, this insight is vital. Most of my interlocutors did not articulate a coherent theology. Instead, they burned candles, rearranged altars, whispered prayers in the bath. Their rituals were often hybrid: a tarot draw mixed with affirmations from therapy, a Wiccan script modified to include nonbinary deities. Sacred life was made not through rules, but through rhythm.

Lived religion, then, provides a lens through which we can understand queer Paganism as embodied and improvised. It gives us permission to take seriously the sacredness of repetition, the meaning in small gestures, the deep knowledge that emerges from intuitive acts (McGuire 2008; Ammerman 2007).

Judith Butler’s theory of performativity reshaped how we understand gender: not as a stable identity but as a repeated doing—a stylized set of acts that create the illusion of coherence (Butler 1990, 1993). In ritual, this is especially poignant. To perform “the High Priestess,” to call down the Goddess, to wear a robe or wield a blade—these are not neutral acts. They draw on histories, expectations, desires

In Pagan ritual, gender is everywhere: in the polarity of chalice and blade, in the gendered deity pairings, in the metaphors used to call the elements. For trans and nonbinary practitioners, these scripts can be sites of rupture and reworking. Some change the language. Others shift the choreography. A few refuse the script entirely, building new liturgies from scratch (Sedgwick 1990; Halberstam 2011)

Butler’s framework allows us to see these ritual adjustments not as accidents, but as generative interruptions. They show us where the sacred cracks, and where queerness floods in (Butler 1993; Muñoz 2009).

Nancy Fraser and Axel Honneth have written extensively on recognition: the social act of being seen, validated, made legible (Fraser 1997; Honneth 1995). In ritual, recognition is more than social—it is spiritual. Who is allowed to invoke the God? Who is seen as a “real” witch? Who is given a title, a seat at the altar, a chance to lead?

Many of the queer practitioners I spoke to described moments of misrecognition: being asked to take on gendered roles that erased their identities, being passed over for leadership, being welcomed as symbols but not as subjects. And yet they also described moments of profound visibility: being asked to call a direction, to bless the circle, to speak their truth into being (Behar 1996; Butler 1990). Recognition in this context is not a matter of politeness—it is a sacred act. It is the difference between being tolerated and being believed.

Catherine Bell urges us to think about ritual not only as a set of repeated forms, but as a practice that enacts and reinforces power (Bell 1992). Ritual is not outside the world—it is one of the ways the world is made.

In Pagan settings, ritual carries authority. It sets rules, assigns roles, determines who can speak and how. These dynamics are not always equitable. While Paganism often positions itself as anti-hierarchical, hierarchies still emerge: between elders and novices, between initiated and uninitiated, between those with recognized lineages and those without (Berger 1998; Magliocco 2004).

For queer practitioners, ritual can both liberate and constrain. Some experience it as a space of affirmation, where fluid identities are welcomed. Others find it replicates the same norms they fled in mainstream religion. Reading ritual through Bell’s lens helps us see these ambivalences clearly (Bell 1992; Fraser 1997).

Following José Esteban Muñoz and Kim TallBear, I approach queerness not simply as a subject, but as a method: a way of thinking, feeling, researching, and relating (Muñoz 2009; TallBear 2013, 2017). Queerness, in this sense, is a refusal of closure. It stays with contradiction. It honors the messy, the liminal, the not-yet.

To queer ritual is to open it—to uncertainty, to laughter, to ghosts. To queer scholarship is to write with emotion, to take seriously stories that don’t fit the mold. My interlocutors were not data points; they were guides. They showed me that ritual is not just a text to be analyzed, but a performance to be felt (Sedgwick 1990; Behar 1996; Haraway 1991).

This essay does not aim to define queer Paganism. It aims to move with it. To trace its spirals. To dwell in its ruptures (Muñoz 2009; Halberstam 2011). Theory is often imagined as the opposite of magic. This section has tried to undo that binary. The thinkers drawn upon here offer a vocabulary for understanding the queer sacred—not as fantasy, but as embodied truth (Styers 2004; Taves 2009). They help us read rituals not only as cultural performances, but as epistemological events: ways of knowing and unknowing (Haraway 1991; TallBear 2019).

In the sections that follow, these tools become companions. They help interpret the chants, the silences, the rituals gone wrong. They allow us to read enchantment not as escape, but as critique (Muñoz 2009; Butler 1993; Halberstam 2011). What we need now is not only interpretation, but presence. The next section turns to methodology: to what it means to research from within the circle—and to be transformed by it.

**Embodied Ethnography: Fieldwork as Ritual, Reflexivity as Spell**

This section details the methodological underpinnings of this study—but it also tells a story of intimacy, vulnerability, and enchantment. It is the story of how I entered this field not just as a researcher, but as a queer, practicing witch. The lines between observer and participant were never stable. I was invoked as often as I was invisible. This is not a flaw. It is the ground of my knowledge (Behar 1996; Clifford 1997; Haraway 1991).

What follows is not simply a list of methods. It is an account of methodological entanglement. It is about what it means to do research in a space where every circle called is also a mirror, every chant a form of affective labor, every ritual a potential undoing (Abu-Lughod 1993; Sedgwick 1993).

Fieldwork for this study took place between October 2010 and December 2014 in Montreal and its surrounding regions. My entry into the field was not secured through formal letters or institutional endorsements. It happened through presence: showing up, staying quiet, asking questions, being seen. Access came through enchantment (Behar 1996; Haraway 2016).

I attended open rituals in community spaces, private ceremonies in cramped apartments, festivals in wooded landscapes. I helped carry crates of candles, offered rides, and learned how to smudge without appropriating (TallBear 2019). I was invited not because I had credentials, but because I carried myself with care. Trust was the true method

In these spaces, I was rarely just an ethnographer. I was asked to call quarters, to cleanse the space, to help rewrite ritual texts. When someone cried, I held their hand. When someone asked about my practice, I answered honestly. This proximity was not a compromise of rigor—it was the condition of insight (Abu-Lughod 1993; Haraway 1991).

My methodology combined semi-structured interviews, participant observation, and sensory fieldnotes. But those words feel too clinical for what unfolded. I prefer to call it ritual ethnography (McGuire 2008; Behar 1996). Interviews were not conducted. They happened—often after ritual, while sharing food, walking to the metro, or smoking under the stars.

Observation was not detached. It was embodied. I smelled the incense. I trembled in trance. I forgot where I was and came back crying (Sedgwick 1990; Muñoz 2009). Fieldnotes were written on napkins, in notebooks, and in dreams. They recorded what was said, but also what was felt, unsaid, or refused.

I also collected ritual scripts, altar photos, chants, digital ephemera, and stories passed between bodies. These artifacts were not treated as data, but as fragments of cosmology (Magliocco 2004; Styers 2004).

I write this essay as a queer, white, settler researcher, fluent in both English and French, shaped by feminist thought, transfeminist kinship, and post-secular longings. My queerness shaped how I was seen and what I could see. I was not an outsider, but I was not neutral either (Haraway 1991; TallBear 2017). In some circles, I was welcomed as kin. In others, I had to earn trust slowly. My body carried its own ambiguities: gender-nonconforming, shifting, both marked and assumed. These ambiguities opened doors, but also made some rituals difficult to inhabit. I never stopped negotiating (Sedgwick 1993; Butler 1990).

Following Donna Haraway, I embrace situated knowledge. Following Kim TallBear, I refuse the fantasy of detached expertise. My voice is in this essay not to claim authority, but to trace the conditions of my seeing (Haraway 1991; TallBear 2013).

Ethics were not administered through forms. They were practiced—ongoing, situated, imperfect. All interviews were conducted with consent, and names were changed unless participants requested otherwise. In a few cases, I destroyed notes upon request (Abu-Lughod 1993; Behar 1996). But the deeper ethics were affective. I did not include everything I saw. I withheld stories that felt too raw, too sacred, or too unresolved. I respected the boundary between what was offered and what was shared (Clifford 1997; TallBear 2019). I also practiced what I now call “ritual confidentiality”: a refusal to expose the most fragile, potent parts of ritual life to academic scrutiny. Some spells should not be written. Some circles should remain unbroken.

The twenty formal participants ranged in age from 22 to 58. They identified across a spectrum of genders, sexualities, and spiritual lineages. Some were initiated in Wiccan traditions; others identified with Reclaiming, Druidry, eclectic paths, or no lineage at all. A few had left lineages after conflict or exclusion (Berger 1998; Magliocco 2004). They included trans women, genderqueer faeries, cis lesbians, agender witches, polyamorous priestesses, and nonbinary brujos. They came from diverse class backgrounds and were mostly white, though some identified as Indigenous, Métis, or mixed-race. Many were bilingual, and their rituals reflected Montreal’s linguistic and cultural hybridity (Browne 2015; Clifford 1997). They did not speak with one voice. They argued, improvised, contradicted each other. Their unity was not in doctrine, but in devotion—to living lives that made room for enchantment (Muñoz 2009; TallBear 2018).

This section has offered a portrait of a fieldwork process rooted in presence, responsiveness, and queer attunement. But it is also a spell. To do this kind of research is to be changed by it (Behar 1996; Sedgwick 1990).

I began with questions about gender and ritual. I left with an altar of my own, a dozen friendships, a deep sense of accountability, and a grief I cannot fully name. Some of what I learned can be cited. Some can only be carried (Haraway 2016; Muñoz 2009).

This essay is not just an account of queer neo-Paganism in Montreal. It is a testimony to what it means to witness, to be seen, and to believe that magic—real, aching, complicated magic—can shape not just the ritual, but the research.

**Ritual Performance and Queer Identity: Practices of Gender, Practices of Spirit**

This section traces how queer neo-Pagan practitioners in Montreal embody and negotiate their identities within ritual space. Through life narratives, participant observation, and sensory fieldnotes, we enter a world where gender and spirituality are not merely identities—they are verbs. They are acts of crafting, of world-building, of sacred experimentation. They are lived, rehearsed, and at times ruptured within the potent temporal space of the circle (Butler 1993; Halberstam 2011; Muñoz 2009).

Ritual is not a neutral ground. It is a container shaped by cosmology, power, and affect. For queer practitioners, it is also a site of becoming—where embodiment is affirmed, questioned, and made otherwise. This section explores how LGBTQ practitioners navigate sacred roles, symbols, and gestures. It attends to both celebration and discomfort, affirmation and friction (Fraser 1997; Bell 1992).

Rituals are never just rituals. They are mirrors, magnifiers, and portals. When queer and trans people step into a circle, they bring with them not only intention but history. They carry the weight of having been misread, misnamed, misrecognized (Honneth 1995; Butler 1990). Within ritual, the hope is not simply to be included, but to become visible in ways the outside world rarely permits.

Mira, a nonbinary femme witch, once said to me after calling the West: “It’s not that I believe the water spirits are real. It’s that they believe I am.”

For many, ritual is not about metaphysical belief—it’s about relational recognition. It is the one place where the voice that cracks, the body in flux, the pronoun that changes, are not mistakes but magic (Fraser 1997; Sedgwick 1990). Ritual becomes autobiography with symbols. It is where the sacred and the social converge (Bell 1992; Ammerman 2007).

Traditional Pagan rituals, especially in Wicca and Reclaiming, are built around repeated gestures: the casting of the circle, the calling of quarters, the drawing down of deity. These structures offer grounding and containment. But they also carry gendered expectations (Adler 2006 [1979]; Berger 1998).

The calling of elements is often gender-coded (Air as masculine intellect, Water as feminine emotion). The Great Rite symbolizes heterosexual union through the penetration of blade into chalice. Deities are invoked in pairs: Goddess and God. Roles are often split by gender: High Priest and High Priestess (Pike 2001; Magliocco 2004).

For queer practitioners, this choreography can be both stabilizing and stifling. Julien (they/he), a genderqueer practitioner, told me: “I like the rhythm of the rites. But I always end up rewriting the lines in my head while I say them.”

Others take a more radical approach: rewriting scripts, queering roles, disrupting polarity. One group replaced the four classical elements with states of being: Grief, Desire, Pleasure, and Rage. Another rewrote the Charge of the Goddess to include trans ancestors and femme dyke elders (Butler 1993; Halberstam 2011; Muñoz 2009). These interventions are not just about language—they are about cosmology. They rewire the ritual’s symbolic system. They conjure new maps (Styers 2004; Taves 2009).

Queer identity in ritual is not stable—it shapeshifts. Participants often described feeling “more real” during ritual. But what that meant varied: more embodied, more fluid, more powerful, more mythical (Sedgwick 1990; Muñoz 2009).

Eli (he/they), a transfeminine butch witch, described it this way: “In ritual I’m not Eli the trans guy. I’m the Keeper of the West. I’m the Vessel. I’m the snake. That’s not escapism—it’s expansion.”

Others found that ritual gave them room to test out pronouns, energies, gendered archetypes. Sometimes they would try on “priestess” one moon, “godform” the next. These were not contradictions—they were initiations (Butler 1990; TallBear 2018). Gender in these spaces functions like a spell: a repeated, affective, performative act that generates its own truth through embodiment and belief (Butler 1993; Bell 1992).

Not all experiences were transformative. Many participants described moments of pain, exclusion, or exhaustion. One nonbinary person was asked to “stand with the women” during a ritual. Another was corrected for using the term “they” in a group invocation. Others were told their gender made them ineligible to lead or invoke (Fraser 1997; Honneth 1995). These were not always acts of overt exclusion. Often, they were the product of unconscious norms—scripts inherited without examination. But they left wounds. One fieldnote reads: “He sat outside the circle the whole time. No one asked why.”

Some responded with withdrawal. Others with ritual refusal—stepping out of roles, changing words mid-spell, bringing in alternative symbols. Many began to form their own circles, where gender was not assigned but conjured (Sedgwick 1993; Halberstam 2011).

Improvisation is central to queer ritual. Not as a lack of preparation, but as a form of openness: to the body’s needs, to the spirits that arrive, to the unexpected grief that rises (McGuire 2008; Muñoz 2009). Rituals often begin with a script, but rarely end with one. Someone forgets the words and sings instead. Someone offers a poem instead of a call. Someone bursts into tears and the circle shifts to hold them. These are not failures of form—they are expressions of the sacred (Bell 1992; Behar 1996). For queer practitioners, this improvisational ethic reflects lived experience.

As Zara said: “We’re always making it up. That’s not chaos—it’s magic.”

The queer sacred is not found in perfection. It is found in the glitch, the slip, the gasp (Sedgwick 1990; Halberstam 2011).

This section has traced how queer and trans practitioners inhabit, disrupt, and reinvent ritual space. Their performances are not only expressions of identity—they are experiments in what sacredness can hold (Muñoz 2009; Butler 1990). Ritual becomes not just a container for the self, but a crucible for becoming. Gender is not erased—it is expanded. Tradition is not abandoned—it is transformed. The sacred is not denied—it is rewritten, whispered, worn differently (Clifton 2006; TallBear 2017).

What emerges is not a single model of queer ritual, but a constellation of practices: tentative, bold, fractured, ecstatic. These performances do not resolve the contradictions of sacred space—they live within them (Haraway 1991; Behar 1996).

The next section turns toward power. If ritual is a site of sacred becoming, it is also a space shaped by hierarchy, legitimacy, and structural tension. What does it mean to speak, to lead, to be believed in the circle? And what happens when the invitation to belong is not evenly extended?

**Power in the Circle: Authority, Tension, and the Limits of Inclusion**

If the previous section showed how queer and trans practitioners perform identity in sacred space, this section turns toward structure. Rituals are never only aesthetic—they are also political. They are sites where legitimacy is negotiated, authority is asserted, and exclusions are enacted (Bell 1992; Fraser 1997). This section examines how power moves in Montreal’s queer Pagan spaces: who leads, who is heard, who is believed, and who is silenced or made peripheral (Berger 1998; Pike 2001).

Contrary to popular perception, alternative spiritualities are not free from hierarchy. In fact, the refusal of institutional structure can sometimes mask more insidious dynamics—those rooted in charisma, tradition, or implicit normativity. Queer practitioners are often praised for their creativity, but denied real authority. They are asked to speak their truth, then gently redirected when it disrupts the flow (Honneth 1995; Butler 1990).

This section does not aim to expose hypocrisy, but to trace the contradictions that persist within even the most well-meaning circles. It is a section about complexity, discomfort, and the courage it takes to name power where others see only light (Haraway 1991; TallBear 2013).

The sacred circle is not a blank space. It is a choreography of roles: caller of quarters, keeper of time, invoker of deity, holder of space. These roles are often passed down, earned through initiation, or conferred by social recognition. Titles like “High Priestess” or “Elder” carry symbolic weight—even in communities that claim to resist hierarchy (Clifton 2006; Magliocco 2004). But who gets to lead?

Many queer and trans participants described being invited to participate but rarely asked to guide. They were told their insights were welcome, yet found themselves relegated to supportive roles. They were praised as visionaries, but denied the microphone. When they questioned gendered metaphors or invoked different cosmologies, they were sometimes accused of being disruptive, political, or “too intense” (Fraser 1997; Bell 1992).

Simone, a nonbinary Reclaiming priest, told me: “They say they want change. But when I bring it, they freeze. I’m allowed to be mystical—but not authoritative.”

The altar, it turns out, is a contested site. Authority is spiritual, but also gendered, racialized, and embodied. Charisma matters—but so does familiarity. Being seen as “serious,” “stable,” or “initiated” is often coded in cisnormative and classed ways (Berger 1998; Behar 1996). Inclusion is often framed as a value—but it is also labor. The labor of translating one’s experience into palatable terms. The labor of educating others mid-ritual. The labor of absorbing microaggressions for the sake of harmony (Fraser 1997; TallBear 2018).

Jay, a genderfluid witch, described the exhaustion of being the only visibly trans person in a coven: “They said they wanted to learn. But every time we met, I had to teach. I wasn’t there to teach. I was there to call Fire.”

For many participants, the promise of inclusion came with strings attached: the expectation to represent, to explain, to soften. One practitioner described “performing gratitude” just to stay in the group. Another said, “I always felt like a guest, never a priestess” (Sedgwick 1990; Honneth 1995).

This is not to say that all queer practitioners were excluded. Many led rituals, held titles, and found deep belonging. But even in those cases, the effort it took to claim space—to be seen not just as symbolic, but as sovereign—was often invisible to others (Butler 1993; Haraway 1991). Even in communities committed to fluidity, tradition haunts the circle. The gender binary is woven into foundational rituals, deity pairings, and elemental associations (Adler 2006 [1979]; Hutton 1999). Some groups have begun to adapt these forms, but many continue to rely on templates where the Goddess and God must dance in harmony, the chalice and blade must meet (Pike 2001; Magliocco 2004).

Marc, a gay elder in a traditional Wiccan lineage, reflected: “I love the rite. But it always assumes straightness. I’ve been reimagining the Great Rite for years—and I’m still not sure I’ve found a way to make it feel like mine.”

Others described moments of rupture: being asked to embody a gendered energy that misaligned with their reality, being excluded from sex-based mysteries, or having to “prove” their connection to archetypes coded as cisgender (Butler 1990; Bell 1992).

These moments are not merely aesthetic mismatches. They are theological fractures. They force queer practitioners to choose between authenticity and belonging, between silence and disruption (Muñoz 2009; TallBear 2017).Not all tensions are resolved. Some become untenable. Several participants shared stories of leaving groups after being repeatedly misgendered, sidelined, or spiritually invalidated. These exits were often painful—filled with grief, rage, and a sense of betrayal (Behar 1996; Haraway 2016).

One priestess told me: “I left because I couldn’t breathe in circle anymore. It was supposed to be sacred, but it became surveillance.”

Others didn’t leave, but stopped speaking. They performed the chants, nodded at the right moments, but no longer brought their full selves to the altar. This withdrawal is not apathy—it is survival (Sedgwick 1993; Fraser 1997). And yet, many kept practicing. They found new circles, started their own covens, or crafted solitary rituals that reflected their truths. Power, after all, is not only held—it is made. And queer practitioners, even when cast out, often become architects of new cosmologies (Muñoz 2009; TallBear 2019).

Despite these frictions, many queer practitioners are finding ways to reimagine leadership. Some groups use consensus decision-making. Others rotate roles or refuse fixed titles. A few reject the binary entirely, invoking deities that shift, blur, or defy embodiment (Halberstam 2011; Clifton 2006).

Zara described their ideal circle as: “A place where no one is above, but everyone is radiant.”

These experiments are not always smooth. They require time, trust, and a willingness to sit with discomfort. But they point toward new models of sacred authority—ones rooted in listening, adaptability, and mutual recognition (TallBear 2013; Butler 1993). Power, in these spaces, is no longer about who calls the quarters. It’s about who feels held. Who gets to arrive fully. Who is allowed to transform the ritual, and who is allowed to be transformed (Haraway 1991; Bell 1992).

This section has traced how power circulates in Montreal’s neo-Pagan communities—not only through titles or lineage, but through tone, gesture, absence. Even in spaces that claim to be liberated, the sacred can reproduce the social. Rituals that aim to heal can still wound. Circles that welcome can still exclude (Fraser 1997; Magliocco 2004).

But to name these tensions is not to dismiss the work. It is to honor it. Queer practitioners are not naive. They know that inclusion is never a guarantee. But still they gather. Still they call, invoke, remember (Muñoz 2009; TallBear 2019). Their practices do not erase power—they queer it. They offer new ways to lead, new ways to listen, new ways to cast and be cast. The circle, like the world, remains imperfect. But it also remains possible.

**The Queering of Magic: Epistemologies of Enchantment and the Politics of the Irrational**

This final section is not a conclusion, but a detonation. It moves from ritual to cosmology, from the structure of the circle to the ground it is drawn upon. It takes the queer sacred seriously—not as a site of inclusion within existing religious frameworks, but as a form of insurgent knowledge that destabilizes the very distinction between religion and magic, between sense and nonsense, between reality and the real (Styers 2004; Taves 2009).

This section contends that to queer magic is not simply to decorate the altar with rainbows or expand deity pronouns. It is to contest the intellectual, colonial, and theological assumptions that have long rendered certain spiritualities illegible. It is to say: magic is not primitive, nor feminine, nor naïve. It is a technology of queer survival. It is a method. It is a way of knowing (TallBear 2013; Muñoz 2009; Halberstam 2011). What is magic, if not the refusal of fixed categories? What is queerness, if not the embrace of fluidity, contradiction, becoming (Butler 1990; Halberstam 2011)?

For many practitioners in this study, magic is not a metaphor. It is real. But not in the way that scientific realism defines reality. Magic is real because it works—it transforms. It creates change. It alters perception. It makes the impossible momentarily tangible (Taves 2009; Styers 2004).

Maria, a nonbinary medium, explained: “Magic is how I survived trauma. It gave me a framework that said: you are not broken, you are porous. You are not lost, you are in between worlds.”

Magic in queer Pagan life functions as an epistemology—an embodied theory of how the world works. It is grounded not in proof, but in presence. It values intuition, affect, synchronicity. It welcomes the nonverbal, the messy, the uncanny (Sedgwick 1990; Muñoz 2009). To embrace magic is to embrace the irrational—not as failure, but as resistance to normativity (Haraway 1991; TallBear 2019).

Anthropology, theology, and sociology have long policed the boundary between magic and religion. Religion was said to be collective, rational, ethical; magic individual, manipulative, primitive. These binaries did not emerge in a vacuum—they emerged through colonial encounters, racial hierarchies, and gendered ideologies (Styers 2004; Tambiah 1990).

As scholars such as Styers, Taves, and Tambiah have argued, the very structure of “religious studies” has been predicated on the exclusion of magic. The witch, the cunning woman, the ecstatic, the animist—all have been cast as “other” (Taves 2009; TallBear 2018).

Queer practitioners today inherit this exclusion. When they speak of deities that shift gender, spells cast with hair and spit, or altars built in drag, they are often dismissed as unserious. Yet it is precisely these practices that generate new ways of relating—to spirit, to land, to self (Muñoz 2009; Butler 1993). To queer magic is to recover it—not as a lost tradition, but as a suppressed epistemology. One that values aesthetics over logic, desire over discipline, becoming over being (Sedgwick 1990; Haraway 2016).

Magic is not only a belief system—it is a methodology. A praxis. A set of techniques for navigating the unknowable (Styers 2004; Taves 2009). In this sense, queer ritualists are not simply spiritual subjects. They are theorists. They are experimenting with the alchemy of metaphor and the architecture of desire. They are crafting cosmologies that resist totality (Halberstam 2011; TallBear 2017).

Helena, a transfemme witch, described her altar as “a lab for reality hacking.” For her, a sigil was not a symbol—it was a force. A charged glyph that made a different future momentarily accessible.

These practices demand a scholarly response that matches their fluidity. To study magic is to be enchanted by it—to risk transformation. It is to write in ways that bend genre, rupture linearity, and invite the reader into the circle (Sedgwick 1993; Muñoz 2009).

Queer magic is often spectacular. It is glitter and smoke, wigs and feathers, runes drawn in eyeliner. It does not hide its theatricality—it revels in it (Muñoz 2009; Halberstam 2011).

Critics might call this superficial. But for practitioners, glamour is sacred. Camp is consecration. The aesthetics of ritual excess are not ornamental—they are ontological (Haraway 1991; Sedgwick 1990).

In a Beltane rite I attended, a drag priestess invoked Venus in full sequin regalia while a butch drummer called down Mars. The circle erupted in laughter, then tears. For a moment, the erotic and the divine were indistinguishable. This is not parody. It is liturgy. It is ritual as art, and art as spell (Taves 2009; Styers 2004). The sacred here does not come from solemnity. It comes from affective intensity—from the ability to make meaning shimmer.

In a world structured by disenchantment, magic becomes a refusal. A way of saying: not everything can be explained, tamed, capitalized. Queer enchantment is not escapism—it is insurgency (Muñoz 2009; TallBear 2013).

By casting spells, building altars, invoking deities who cross binaries, queer practitioners assert a different kind of relationality. One that is not governed by productivity or coherence. One that makes room for mystery, pleasure, grief, and joy (Butler 1993; Sedgwick 1990). Ritual becomes protest. Glamour becomes strategy. Magic becomes not what is unreal, but what remains unruled (Haraway 2016; TallBear 2018).

This section has argued that magic, far from being marginal, is a central mode of queer world-making. It is not an addition to religion—it is a challenge to its foundations. It is not a soft form of belief—it is a sharp tool for critique (Taves 2009; Styers 2004; Muñoz 2009).

To queer magic is to queer knowing. It is to value the felt, the fabulated, the fabulous. It is to insist that knowledge can emerge from the margins—from ritual, from drag, from desire (Halberstam 2011; Sedgwick 1990; Butler 1990).

If earlier sections asked how queers navigate sacred space, this section asks how queerness remakes the sacred altogether.

The circle does not close here. It expands. The altar is not cleared—it multiplies. The spell is not finished—it continues. This is not a conclusion. It is a glamour. A glamour that shimmers with the possibility of a sacred yet to come.

**Conclusion: Toward a Queer Sociology of the Sacred**

This essay began as an ethnography of ritual and identity and, in its unfolding, became something larger: a sustained meditation on the sacredness of queer world-making and the methodological implications of taking enchantment seriously. Through sustained fieldwork in Montreal’s neo-Pagan communities, deep engagement with feminist, decolonial, and queer theories, and a commitment to ritual not just as object but as epistemology, we have followed how LGBTQ+ practitioners do more than adapt spiritual frameworks—they reshape the very grammar of the sacred (Behar 1996; Muñoz 2009; TallBear 2013; Haraway 2016).

What emerged is not a story of easy inclusion. Nor is it a tale of radical rupture. It is a cartography of friction. Neo-Pagan spaces—particularly those structured by Wicca and Reclaiming Witchcraft—offer potent tools for gender affirmation, symbolic play, and cosmological healing (Adler 2006 [1979]; Magliocco 2004; Berger 1998). But they also reproduce the very binaries and hierarchies they seek to overcome (Pike 2001; Fraser 1997). In that contradiction lies the ritual labor of queering: not purification, but improvisation. Not transcendence, but transformation (Butler 1990; Halberstam 2011).

This essay thus contributes to three scholarly conversations: Pagan studies, the sociology of religion, and queer studies. In each case, it does so not by merely adding queer subjects to existing paradigms, but by rethinking what counts as knowledge, what constitutes ritual, and how power animates spiritual life (Ammerman 2007; Bell 1992; Taves 2009).

Within Pagan studies, this essay insists on the centrality of queer voices—not as anecdotal or exceptional, but as constitutive. Much of the existing literature on Paganism has focused on its revivalist, feminist, ecological, or esoteric dimensions (Hutton 1999; Adler 2006 [1979]; Clifton 2006). While crucial, these analyses have often sidelined questions of gender fluidity, sexual politics, and racialization. This work expands the field by demonstrating how queer practitioners are not simply participants in neo-Paganism, but its theorists, critics, and remakers (Browne 2015; TallBear 2013).

By attending to moments of misrecognition, ritual refusal, and symbolic reimagination, this ethnography complicates overly celebratory narratives of inclusivity in Pagan traditions (Fraser 1997; Butler 1993). It asks difficult questions about sacred legitimacy, theological authority, and the inheritance of binary metaphors (Pike 2001; Bell 1992). And it foregrounds practitioners who don’t just cast spells—they queer them, revise them, and sometimes reject the very cosmologies they were trained in (Halberstam 2011; Muñoz 2009).

The essay also offers a methodological intervention. It argues for a Pagan studies that is not only descriptive but transformative—one that treats ritual as theory, and enchantment as knowledge (Styers 2004; Taves 2009).

To sociology of religion, this work offers both data and critique. It documents a contemporary spiritual movement that exists outside institutional frames, doctrinal orthodoxy, and census categories (Ammerman 2007; McGuire 2008). It traces how queer practitioners assemble meaningful spiritual lives through improvisation, aesthetic labor, and embodied experimentation (Berger 1998; Behar 1996).

But more fundamentally, it challenges the epistemological boundaries of the discipline. Classical sociology—from Durkheim to Weber—has long drawn a line between religion and magic, belief and performance, legitimacy and deviance. These distinctions were never neutral. They were structured by colonialism, patriarchy, and rationalist bias (Styers 2004; Taves 2009; TallBear 2013).

By centering magic as a queer epistemology, this work destabilizes the very categories through which religion has been sociologically known. It demands that we take seriously the knowledge produced in ritual—not only as affect, but as analytics (Sedgwick 1990; Muñoz 2009). It insists that religion is not just institutional—it is intimate, fragmented, affective, and sometimes flamboyant (Bell 1992; Halberstam 2011). It invites sociologists to listen differently: not only to beliefs, but to gestures, glitches, and glitter.

In the context of queer studies, this essay insists that the spiritual is not an afterthought. Too often, queer theory has foregrounded critique of normativity, embodiment, and futurity while leaving religion and spirituality to the sidelines—or treating them with suspicion (Butler 1990; Sedgwick 1993).

This essay offers an alternative: a queer studies that is not afraid of the altar, the chant, the trance (Muñoz 2009; TallBear 2018). It treats ritual not as superstition, but as performance theory in motion (Bell 1992; Halberstam 2011). It explores how queer and trans practitioners use magic not as metaphor but as method: to rework kinship, to heal trauma, to mark transition, to grieve the ungrievable (Behar 1996; Taves 2009).

It contributes to queer thought by expanding its terrain—from the bedroom and the archive to the sacred circle. It does not abandon critique, but it pairs it with curiosity. What if queer world-making is not just about disruption, but also about invocation? What if the future is not only imagined, but summoned? (Muñoz 2009; Haraway 2016)

Throughout this essay, we have followed rituals in their most intimate and most theatrical moments. We have witnessed sacred scripts rewritten, roles reimagined, and symbols queered. We have seen how drag can be a liturgy, how misgendering can fracture a rite, and how sacred space can be a site of both healing and harm (Sedgwick 1993; Butler 1993; Magliocco 2004).

The work of queer Paganism, as this essay shows, is never just expressive. It is cosmological. It is ontological. It is political (Muñoz 2009; Halberstam 2011; TallBear 2017).

And for scholars, it is also methodological. We must now ask: How do we research what resists language? How do we write rituals that weren’t made to be transcribed? How do we stay with the trouble—not to resolve it, but to learn from it? (Haraway 1991; Behar 1996)

Queer Pagan ritual is not a performance of resistance. It is resistance as performance. It does not aim to be coherent—it aims to be real. In the cracked voice of an invocation, in the trembling hand holding a candle, in the laughter after the glitch—something sacred happens. Something that deserves to be studied, not as anomaly, but as revelation (Sedgwick 1990; Muñoz 2009).

This is not a conclusion. It is an opening. The circle, as many rituals proclaim, is cast beyond time and space. It does not close—it transforms. The altar is not cleared—it is multiplied. The spell is not broken—it is shared. So let us return—again and again—to the sites of queer enchantment. Let us study them not only with theory, but with breath. Let us let the ritual mark us, unsettle us, inspire us. The queer sacred is not an object. It is a relation. Let the relation remain. Let the circle remain open. Let the queer sacred speak.
