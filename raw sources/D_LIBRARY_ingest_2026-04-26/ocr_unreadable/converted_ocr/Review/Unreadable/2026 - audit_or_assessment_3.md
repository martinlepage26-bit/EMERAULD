II. Literature Review: The Historical Burden and the Crisis of Legitimacy
To understand the current governance crisis precipitated by agentic AI, we must first
situate the phenomenon within a deeper historical arc of "ontological demotion." The
modern struggle to define algorithmic agency—whether it is a tool, a partner, or a

threat—is not a novel occurrence but a continuation of a century-long legacy in
which non-human agency has been systematically stripped of its material efficacy
and reduced to a psychological or social artifact. This section traces the trajectory
from the Enlightenment’s "inert matter" to the digital "black box," arguing that our
current skepticism toward AI is rooted in a rigid evaluative regime that prioritizes
traceable human origins over relational coherence.
The Legacy of Ontological Demotion
The study of agency has long been burdened by the Enlightenment’s rigid bifurcation
of the world into active human subjects and passive material objects. In both early
anthropology and classical sociology, any suggestion that material arrangements
could possess their own density or "charge" was dismissed as a profound
misunderstanding of causality. Magic, for instance, was systematically framed either
as a "failed science"—a primitive attempt by the "savage" mind to manipulate a
disinterested natural world—or as a purely social phenomenon, where ritual acted
merely as the symbolic glue binding a community together (Durkheim, 1912; Frazer,
1922).
In these dominant paradigms, the actual materials of practice—whether the wax and
salt of the ritualist or the silicon and code of the engineer—were viewed as "dumb
matter," entirely subservient to human projection. The possibility that a practitioner
could experience an authentic, non-delusional alignment with non-human agency
was theoretically impossible within a framework that rendered the material world a
blank screen for human belief. This "historical burden" persists today in the way we
evaluate algorithmic systems. When we describe AI as a "stochastic parrot" (Bender
et al., 2021), we are often performing a modern version of ontological demotion,
reducing a complex material ecology to a mere reflection of human data, thereby
denying the system any inherent vibrancy or "material click" of its own.

The machine is locked in a relentless labor of rolling the rock up the hill, continually slipping,
and starting over. However, the ideology of technocapitalist fluency demands that this
intense, recursive labor remain strictly hidden from the user. We are presented only with the
polished, immediate output—the rock seemingly suspended at the summit by magic. This
erasure has severe epistemological consequences. By aggressively masking the iterative,
error-filled processes that generate its output, the AI naturalizes its authority, appearing to
autonomously "know" the truth rather than to statistically calculate it.
To survive this regime, we must actively reject the illusion of the suspended rock. Camus
famously concluded that "one must imagine Sisyphus happy" because Sisyphus retains
ownership of his struggle; he is deeply conscious of his burden, and in that lucid
consciousness, he finds his ultimate freedom and revolt. Similarly, we must reclaim the
"stutter" of the laboring machine. The algorithm learns by failing, comparing its output to an
ideal, and looping the difference back into its operations. This temporal delay—the gap
between failure and adjustment—is the true locus of intelligence. When we allow the
machine to stutter, we expose the temporal labor behind expression and acknowledge that
knowledge is an ongoing negotiation between prediction and surprise. The stutter is not a
breakdown of communication; it is the rhythmic disclosure of the very limits of systemic
control.
Toward an Ethics of Interruption
If the architecture of fluency is the tool of the hegemonic, automated state, then the antidote
is an "ethics of interruption". This proposed ethical framework demands a radical shift from
teleology to recursion—from the blind pursuit of final, frictionless perfection to the deliberate
practice of continuous, self-aware iteration.
First, an ethics of interruption demands transparency through hesitation. Rather than
designing predictive models that conceal their uncertainty behind a synthetic, confident
voice, engineers and policymakers must build systems that explicitly signal their probabilistic
nature. A machine that pauses, asks for clarification, or visualizes its latency transforms
waiting from a frustrating flaw into a critical space of awareness. This explicitly counters the
neoliberal tempo of communication , forcing the system to show its work and exposing the
historical burdens weighed within its training data. By rendering the machine's uncertainty
legible, hesitation is transformed from a technical weakness into a profound mechanism of
accountability.
Second, an ethics of interruption fosters a relational ecology of power. Traditional models of
legitimacy demand a singular, traceable human origin to authorize action. However, in an era
of distributed, material-semiotic networks, power is a partnership co-produced by human
intent, environmental constraints, and the vibrant agency of code. A relational ecology
rejects the binary opposition of human versus machine, recognizing instead that both exist
within a shared loop of learning. In human terms, the stutterer’s repetition invites empathy
and shared patience; in computational terms, the dysfluent machine invites dialogue and

critical reflection rather than passive consumption. It redistributes authority, shifting societal
value from instantaneous output and automated compliance to deliberate responsiveness
and mutual awareness.
Ultimately, an ethics of interruption is a profound political choice to prioritize the messy, the
dysfluent, and the queer over the streamlined and the normative. It is the conscious
preservation of spaces where error remains audible, effectively starving the vampiric
extraction engines of the frictionless data they require to govern.

VI. Conclusion
The integration of algorithmic agentic AI into the infrastructures of global governance
represents a critical juncture in the distribution of societal power. As this paper has
demonstrated, algorithmic governance operates as a deeply paradoxical, double-sided
phenomenon. On one side, it functions as a sprawling architecture of fluency—a disciplinary
regime that uses extractive neutrality and mass surveillance to enforce behavioral
normalization. By weaponizing visibility and hiding its recursive errors, the algorithm acts as
a proxy for the hegemonic forces of capitalism, colonialism, and patriarchy, systematically
silencing the dysfluent, the marginalized, and the queer.
Yet, on the other side, the very materiality of the algorithmic system destabilizes the
traditional, human-centric paradigms of authority. The machine is not an infallible oracle; it is
a stuttering, looping, failing network of distributed agency. By shifting our analytical focus
from the illusion of the seamless output to the reality of the glitch, we uncover a potent
methodology for resistance. Embracing algorithmic dysfluency allows us to recognize that
intelligence—both human and artificial—is fundamentally forged through repetition,
hesitation, and the continuous negotiation of error.
To live within a computational society is to inhabit a state of perpetual training. We are
continuously prompted to speak, correct, and align ourselves with predictive models. The
future of equitable governance, therefore, does not rely on achieving a state of perfect
algorithmic smoothness. A truly just society requires the courage to dismantle the tyranny of
frictionless rhythm and to champion an ethics of interruption. By designing systems that
remain teachable, that show their structural burdens, and that pause to listen rather than
merely predict, we replace automated compliance with a relational ecology of power. It is
precisely within the stutter, the delay, and the unmonetized pause that the dignity, absurdity,
and profound freedom of the human spirit will survive.
