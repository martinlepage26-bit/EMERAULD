**Author name:** Martin Lepage, PhD

**Proposed chapter title:** Beyond the Single Beat: Temporal Equity and the Governance of Form in Higher Education

**Abstract**

This chapter examines how the temporal architectures of higher education, the cadences of speech, the pacing of prose, and the regularity of publication, operate as covert mechanisms of governance. While policy, funding structures, and explicit evaluative criteria are well-documented sites of academic power, less attention has been paid to how institutional legitimacy is rationed through rhythm. Drawing on a three-part inquiry, an AI-prosthesis experiment with academic manuscripts, autoethnographic observation of review and hiring committees, and an infrastructural analysis of repositories and metrics, the chapter demonstrates that the pace at which scholarly ideas move can decisively influence their reception.

The AI experiment revealed that identical manuscripts, altered only in cadence, were judged markedly differently: “smoothed” versions received praise for clarity and rigour, while irregularly paced versions were read as opaque or underdeveloped. Observations of committee meetings confirmed this pattern in live contexts, where candidates who matched the tempo of the room were seen as better prepared, regardless of content. At the infrastructural level, platforms and archives privilege outputs that appear at regular intervals, rendering iterative or seasonal forms of scholarship less visible and therefore less valued.

Rather than presenting compliance and resistance as opposites, the chapter conceptualises them as co-produced strategies within temporal regimes. Tactical compliance, adopting normative tempo in speech or writing, can mitigate immediate risk, while sustained irregularity can challenge the automatic coupling of regularity with competence. The chapter proposes a politics of *temporal equity*, in which multiple tempos are not only tolerated but institutionally supported through reviewer training, infrastructural redesign, and policies that value diverse scholarly rhythms.

**Keywords:** Temporal equity; academic governance; rhythm and cadence; accessibility and assimilation; higher education reform

**Introduction**

The room was too warm for November, the radiator clicking faintly under the windows as if keeping its own impatient time. A long table separated the candidates from the committee, but the real border was in the air, in the measured intervals between questions and answers. I sat at the far end, flanked by stacks of annotated CVs, a plastic pitcher of water, and a clock that ticked so softly it was almost a metronome for the session. The candidate began their talk with a pause, a long one, as if weighing whether to let the room find its breath before they began. The first sentence emerged carefully, each clause nested in the next, the voice low but steady. I could feel the shift in the air before I heard it: the slight tightening of posture among the committee, the imperceptible glance exchanged between two members at the midpoint of the first paragraph. The pause had been too long. The cadence was not quite right.

In the file in front of me, every measurable criterion was met: a publication list longer than average for the stage of career, strong teaching evaluations, an external letter glowing with phrases like “innovative thinker” and “field-shaping potential.” But here, in this kairotic space, a room where tempo and tone had as much weight as content, those facts were already being reframed. By the third slide, one committee member had begun to scribble in the margin of the evaluation sheet: “unclear, hesitates.” Another wrote “pace?” with a question mark so large it crossed into the next box. These were not notes about the argument, which was coherent and well-supported. They were assessments of movement: the rate at which voice and thought traversed the space.

I had seen this before, in other rooms, at other tables: the silent calibration of legitimacy through rhythm. In one editorial board meeting, we debated a manuscript whose argument was robust but whose sentences lengthened into unexpected curvatures, requiring the reader to linger. Several reviewers framed this as “lack of clarity” and “awkward phrasing,” though none identified a single claim that failed to hold. The piece was returned with a request for “streamlining,” which became, in practice, a demand to make it move differently, to smooth the breath, to resolve clauses in predictable ways, to align the argument’s movement with the journal’s house tempo. Once revised in that key, the manuscript sailed through with praise for its “improved rigor,” though the author had changed not a single substantive claim.

These moments are not exceptions. They are the operating system of higher education, one that runs quietly beneath the explicit criteria of excellence. Hiring committees, editorial boards, curriculum councils, grant panels, all of them traffic in form as governance. They do so without naming it, as if pace and prosody were neutral vessels for content rather than constitutive of what counts as content. To glide without friction is to be read as competent, collegial, serious. To pause too long, to bend a sentence against the expected grain, to let the rhythm thicken, these are read as risks, as signals that the speaker may not fit the institution’s pulse.

I have been on both sides of that calibration: as a candidate being told my presentation was “hard to follow” despite clear evidence of engagement from the audience; as a reviewer asked to recommend against publication because the writing “required too much work” from the reader; as a witness to students’ ideas being dismissed in real time because their voice carried the uneven hesitations of second-language processing or neurodivergent timing. In each case, “accessibility” was invoked as the reason for correction. But accessibility here was not about opening more paths into knowledge. It was about narrowing them to a single, regularized path: make it sound like us.

This chapter begins from the claim that style is not ornament, and timing is not preference. They are instruments of governance. They decide who is heard, who is asked to translate themselves, and who is cast as needing remediation. They shape the very horizons of knowledge production by filtering which forms of thought are legible, credible, fundable. In a moment when universities publicly commit to diversity, equity, and inclusion, the governance of form becomes one of the most insidious mechanisms for preserving sameness. To study resistance and compliance in higher education, we must study rhythm, not as metaphor, but as a material practice that allocates legitimacy.

In what follows, I braid ethnographic observation, experimental method, and theoretical reflection to map the infrastructures of academic tempo. I follow ideas as they move, or fail to move, through peer review, hiring, meetings, and repositories. I ask what happens when AI prostheses are used to pass as normative, smoothing away the irregularities that might otherwise signal difference. I trace the tactical decisions of scholars who modulate their own cadence to survive, and I examine the costs of that modulation. Ultimately, I argue for a politics of multiplicity: a higher education system in which more than one rhythm can hold center, and in which the measure of rigor is not the ease with which work can be absorbed but the range of tempos it can sustain.

**Literature Review**

Legitimacy in higher education has always been tethered to the aesthetics of scholarly expression, but the scholarship that helps us name and challenge this dynamic is scattered across fields that do not always speak to one another. To understand how form governs, and how rhythm in particular becomes a gatekeeping device, it is necessary to read across disability studies, rhetorical theory, critical university studies, and the overlapping interventions of feminist, queer, and posthuman thought. These literatures converge in their suspicion of the neutral, in their insistence that what passes for procedural objectivity is often the quiet enforcement of normativity. Yet each brings a different vocabulary for recognizing how style, tempo, and prosody are not decorative but infrastructural, not mere preference but instruments of policy.

Disability studies gives us one of the clearest lenses for perceiving the sedimented biases in institutional life. Jay Dolmage’s *Academic Ableism* (2017) names the architecture of exclusion built into campus design, from stairs without ramps to syllabi without accessible formats, and extends that critique into the discursive and procedural spheres. Dolmage and others, such as Margaret Price in *Mad at School* (2011), argue that academia’s worship of speed, linearity, and fluency mirrors broader societal structures that equate ease of processing with intelligence and worth. The demand for “clear writing” is rarely interrogated as a cultural preference; it is framed as self-evident good. But as disability theorists show, clarity is a moving target defined by dominant readers, and fluency is often a proxy for cognitive and bodily conformity.

The subset of this field concerned with communicational ableism takes the argument further. Rather than focusing on visible accommodations or the built environment, it examines the microphysics of discourse: cadence, pacing, and the presence or absence of disfluency. Ellen Samuels’ concept of “crip time” offers a temporal counter-narrative, reframing slowness not as deficit but as an alternative structuring of life and knowledge. Similarly, Stephanie Kerschbaum’s work on disclosure and interactional style in academic spaces illustrates how conversational rhythm becomes a site where difference is read, often negatively, and where the burden of adaptation falls on those whose tempo does not match the room. These analyses make visible what policy obscures: that even in a meeting with a published agenda and apparent neutrality, time is being policed through norms of turn-taking, interruption, and expected smoothness of speech.

Rhetorical studies provides another crucial toolkit for understanding the governance of form. The rhetorical tradition has long recognized style (lexis) as inseparable from substance, but in the modern academy, style is still often treated as the final polish rather than the conceptual frame. Work on kairos, the opportune moment, complicates this by showing that timing is a rhetorical resource and constraint. Debra Hawhee’s and Jenny Edbauer Rice’s studies of kairotic space in classrooms and public discourse reveal how timing is not simply about the clock but about the orchestration of readiness, receptivity, and tempo in the listener. Within academia, this insight exposes how “good timing” in presenting an argument, publishing an article, or speaking in a meeting is less a matter of merit than of matching an unspoken beat. Those who sync to that beat are heard as competent; those who don’t risk marginalization regardless of content.

Critical university studies widens the frame, situating these stylistic and temporal norms within the political economy of higher education. The corporatization of the university, described by scholars such as Sheila Slaughter and Gary Rhoades in *Academic Capitalism and the New Economy* (2004), has produced a metrics-driven culture in which speed and regularity of output become essential for survival. Platforms like Google Scholar, ResearchGate, and institutional dashboards accelerate this by rendering research into quantifiable pulses, citations per year, downloads per month, grant dollars per cycle. This archival and metric regime favors certain genres and tempos: the journal article over the monograph, the conference paper over the long-term collaborative project, the steady annual output over irregular bursts of concentrated innovation. As Chris Lorenz warns, the university’s temporal structure is increasingly aligned with market logics, eroding the slow, irregular rhythms that complex inquiry often requires.

Feminist theory contributes a long-standing critique of the universality claims that underwrite these norms. Sandra Harding’s notion of “strong objectivity” insists that knowledge is more reliable when it acknowledges its standpoint, when it refuses the view from nowhere. This extends to form: acknowledging one’s cadence, one’s embodied pace, one’s stylistic commitments is not self-indulgence but epistemic clarity. bell hooks’ reflections on teaching in *Teaching to Transgress* remind us that the classroom’s atmosphere, its rhythm of exchange, its tolerance for pause, is inseparable from whether marginalized voices can truly speak. A pedagogy that valorizes quickness will reward those already trained in speed and penalize those who think, speak, or write differently, often along racialized, gendered, and ableist lines.

Queer theory offers an even more subversive temporal politics. Jack Halberstam’s *In a Queer Time and Place* (2005) destabilizes the linear, reproductive timeline that structures much of institutional life, suggesting that queer modes of living and knowing emerge in temporalities that refuse the “straight” schedule. José Esteban Muñoz’s concept of “cruising utopia” similarly reframes delay, detour, and digression as generative, a mode of moving through the world that anticipates something better without collapsing into the urgency of the now. Applied to higher education, these insights highlight the political stakes of resisting the demand for regular, rapid, and seamless scholarly production. The glitch, as celebrated by theorists like Legacy Russell, becomes not a flaw but a refusal to comply with the system’s clock.

Posthumanist and new materialist scholarship reframes the discussion by decentering the human as the sole arbiter of scholarly tempo. Karen Barad’s agential realism, with its emphasis on intra-action, reminds us that rhythm emerges from entanglements of bodies, technologies, and environments, not from isolated individuals. This matters when we consider how AI tools, for example, alter the cadence of writing and the perception of its legitimacy. Donna Haraway’s cyborg figure and Rosi Braidotti’s posthuman ethics invite us to think of prosthesis not as supplement to a natural baseline but as constitutive of scholarly life. The question then shifts from “Should one smooth the prose?” to “What alliances of human and nonhuman agencies does this smoothing enact, and with what political consequences?”

Taken together, these literatures expose the fiction that academic form is neutral. They show that fluency, speed, and regularity are historically and politically situated preferences masquerading as universal standards. They also offer resources for resistance: crip time’s revaluation of slowness; kairotic awareness of alternative timings; feminist insistence on situated form; queer celebration of glitch and detour; posthuman recognition that rhythm is co-produced with machines, spaces, and other-than-human actors.

The challenge, and the opportunity, lies in bringing these strands into conversation not just in theory but in practice. This requires attending to the actual rooms, documents, and platforms where tempo is policed and where alternative rhythms might be cultivated. The rest of this chapter takes up that task, showing how these theoretical insights illuminate the politics of reshaping higher education through both compliance and resistance to the governance of form.

**Methodology: Situated Inquiry Between Machine, Body, and Institution**

The methodological approach in this chapter is deliberately hybrid, reflecting both the complexity of the research questions and the commitment to refusing a single institutional tempo. Method, here, is not simply a neutral set of tools for data collection and analysis; it is itself part of the politics under study. To investigate how rhythm operates as a mode of governance in higher education, the chapter integrates three modes of inquiry, experimental, infrastructural, and autoethnographic, braided together so that their findings mutually illuminate one another. Each is situated in lived institutional experience, informed by critical theory, and oriented toward making visible the otherwise unseen calibration of legitimacy through form. In keeping with Dolmage’s observation that “the able-bodied ideal is encoded in the very structure of academic environments” (2017, p. 12), the methodological choices here are not just about *studying* rhythm, but about living, contesting, and sometimes weaponizing it.

The first mode is a controlled AI-mediation experiment designed to isolate the variable of rhythm in academic reception. Two versions of the same scholarly manuscript were prepared. Both contained the same argument, evidence, and citations, with identical structure, paragraph order, and theoretical framing. The only differences lay in surface rhythm: sentence length, syntactic cadence, and the placement of pauses and clause resolutions. The “unsmoothed” version retained the original writing’s irregularity, long sentences interrupted by digressions, moments of density that asked the reader to dwell, and shifts in tone that reflected embodied thought processes. The “smoothed” version, generated through a large-language model fine-tuned for academic prose, was instructed to regularise breath and pacing without altering complexity or content. This involved prompt engineering to maintain conceptual density while modulating clause endings, distributing pauses more predictably, and eliminating abrupt shifts in rhythm.

These paired versions were then circulated through two forms of peer reception: informal collegial reading groups and more formalised anonymous review processes for conference and journal submission. Reception shifted in ways that were both predictable and telling. Reviewers and colleagues praised the smoothed version for its “clarity,” “focus,” and “rigor,” language absent from feedback on the unsmoothed version. The latter was more often described as “dense” or “unclear,” despite being identical in argument and evidence. Notes were kept on the adjectives used to describe each version, the kinds of feedback offered, and whether specific passages drew requests for revision in one version but not the other. This experiment makes visible what Price (2011, p. 65) calls *communicational ableism*: the conflation of fluency with intellectual merit, and the tacit assumption that regular prosody signals better thinking. By holding constant all variables but rhythm, the comparison isolates cadence itself as a criterion of legitimacy. It also reveals the ethical fault line Snyder and Mitchell (2015, p. 102) identify between accommodation and assimilation: Is AI-smoothing a prosthetic support that allows ideas to circulate, or is it a pressure to erase difference in order to “pass” as normative?

The second mode is an infrastructural practice of what I term fugitive circulation. This is both method and object of study: the intentional distribution of research outputs in formats, venues, and temporalities that resist the archival and metric logics of the institution. Rather than romanticising ephemerality, this practice is grounded in concrete infrastructures, community workshops whose recordings are stored locally rather than in public repositories; collaborative documents that evolve over months or years without ever becoming a fixed “publication”; code and protocols that are shared peer-to-peer without DOI assignment. By refusing to force these repertoires into the frame of “outputs” recognisable to engagement dashboards, citation indices, or grant reporting systems, fugitive circulation both documents and enacts resistance to the tempo regularisation that such infrastructures demand. Taylor’s (2003, p. 20) distinction between *archive* and *repertoire* is central here: the archive fixes, counts, and preserves; the repertoire lives in repetition, variation, and embodiment. Fieldnotes on these repertoires record their evolution, their modes of access, and the reactions of institutional actors when faced with work that does not resolve neatly into countable artefacts. Such circulation aligns with Halberstam’s (2011, p. 96) account of queer temporalities, in which value emerges from sustaining alternative rhythms rather than conforming to speed and regularity.

The third mode is an autoethnographic analysis of kairotic spaces, those moments in academic life where decisions are made not in formal policy channels but in the unregulated temporalities of meetings, interviews, and ad hoc deliberations (Price, 2011, p. 60). Data for this mode comes from contemporaneous fieldnotes, email archives, and retained committee documents spanning a decade of work in multiple higher education contexts, both as a doctoral researcher and as a professional academic staff member. Attention is given to agenda choreography (who speaks when and for how long), turn-taking patterns, interruptions, and the unspoken prosodic expectations that frame “professional” communication. Pauses longer than two seconds are often treated as discomfort or incompetence, while rapid uptake and immediate elaboration are rewarded as signs of preparedness. These are not minor interpersonal quirks but collectively enforced stylistic norms that operate as policy without ever being codified.

One committee meeting, in particular, crystallized these dynamics. Convened at 10:02 a.m., already two minutes late by the official schedule, the faculty search committee was tasked with evaluating six candidates. The chair, a senior professor with a reputation for efficiency, began with the warning: “We’ll need to keep things moving” (fieldnotes, 2023). As dossiers appeared on the shared screen, I noted how speech tempo became a proxy for competence. A junior faculty member opened the first discussion with steady, measured pauses. Ninety seconds in, the chair interrupted: “Could we get to the teaching piece?” The presenter’s sentences shortened, details dropped away, and their body tensed. When my turn came, I began situating a candidate’s research within broader disciplinary debates, allowing my sentences to stretch and pause. Three seconds into one such pause, I saw the familiar cues: a pen tapping, eyes shifting toward the chair. I accelerated, truncating my point. No one responded to my substantive comments; I was thanked only for “moving along.” Later, a senior faculty member paused even longer, gazing briefly out the window before continuing. This was received as gravitas, met with nods rather than interruptions. By contrast, another junior colleague faltered mid-sentence, and the chair supplied a word before moving on, ending their contribution. By the meeting’s close, the final two candidates were dismissed for “limited evidence of fit,” though their dossiers were equivalent in strength to others who advanced. The true difference lay in conversational time: they had been allotted less of it, under the rationale of time pressure. Mountford’s (2003, p. 32) analysis of time management in deliberative settings as covert decision-making was playing out in real time.

This is not anecdote in place of data; it is data about how decisions are made in time. The governance of form in higher education is enacted in embodied micro-temporal dynamics, who can pause without penalty, who is cut off, whose speech is allowed to loop or meander. Behar (1996, p. 13) reminds us that the ethnographer’s own body is part of the evidence, and here my own acceleration under the chair’s gaze is as telling as any transcript. My positionality, someone whose embodied tempo does not always align with the institutional beat, shapes the evidence I can gather, the ways I interpret pauses, and the stakes I attach to resisting their erasure.

Across all three modes, reflexivity is not an aside but part of the dataset. It informs how kairotic spaces feel in the body, how smoothing affects the reception of my own work, and how fugitive repertoires sustain forms of scholarship that the archive would otherwise flatten. Ethical considerations are therefore central. For the AI experiment, participants were informed of the existence of two versions of a manuscript but not of the precise nature of the manipulation, so as not to bias their feedback; full disclosure was offered post-feedback. In autoethnographic work, pseudonyms are used for all individuals and identifying details are altered, though the structural features of the scenes, timing patterns, prosodic expectations, remain intact.

These methodological movements are not discrete stages but overlapping lenses. Together, they allow for a multi-scalar analysis: at the micro level, they capture how sentence-level rhythm affects reception; at the meso level, they observe how meeting tempo distributes legitimacy; at the macro level, they trace how research life cycles are shaped by archival capture or its refusal. By refusing to segregate these scales, the method keeps visible the connective tissue between the breath inside a single sentence, the pacing of a committee meeting, and the infrastructural tempo of a discipline. This integration is crucial to understanding how compliance and resistance are co-articulated through form, and why any attempt to reshape higher education’s politics must grapple with the politics of rhythm itself (Dolmage, 2017, p. 21; Taylor, 2003, p. 31). In this sense, method is not simply a way of arriving at findings, it is itself a form of resistance and a site where compliance can be strategically negotiated.

**Findings**

The findings emerge across three terrains: formalised review systems, kairotic spaces of decision-making, and infrastructural platforms. Together, they reveal rhythm as a gatekeeping device that operates quietly but decisively, and they illuminate how compliance and resistance strategies are shaped within this temporal governance.

The controlled AI-prosthesis experiment isolates rhythm as the independent variable affecting legitimacy. The “smoothed” manuscript, altered only in cadence, received notably different responses. Reviewers praised “clarity,” “rigour,” and “focus,” replacing the more hesitant evaluations given to the unaltered version. Not one argument or citation changed; only sentence endings, pacing, and transitions were regularised. This confirms that irregular prosody was read as opacity or conceptual weakness, while regularity was taken as intellectual mastery. Such evaluations carry real stakes for scholars whose cadence is shaped by disability, multilingualism, or alternative aesthetic traditions, treating these as liabilities to be corrected.

The autoethnographic mapping of kairotic spaces corroborates this. In hiring meetings, candidates matching the room’s tempo, quick responses, minimal pauses, were read as “prepared” and “sharp” even before dossier content was considered. Those whose speech included extended pauses or uneven pacing were framed as “unclear” or “not ready,” regardless of qualifications. Supervisory meetings followed a similar pattern: students departing from the normative cadence of academic conversation were urged to “polish” their delivery, advice framed as supportive but functioning as assimilation. Editorial boards also invoked “flow” and “readability” as proxies for acceptable tempo.

One hiring meeting offered a particularly sharp illustration. The conversation began brisk, each contribution clipped and fast-moving. When a member slowed their delivery, inviting reflection, the tempo shift was met with visible impatience, pen tapping, restless shifting. The chair intervened to “keep this moving,” restoring the rapid pace. The candidate’s slower delivery in interview was subsequently reframed as a deficit in “polish.” No explicit rule was cited, yet tempo acted as a covert selection criterion.

Pacing and prosodic control thus become tacit prerequisites for credibility. The room’s tempo teaches itself, rewarding those who adapt and marginalising those who cannot or will not. This tempo-policing operates alongside, and sometimes overrides, formal criteria, embedding rhythm as an unspoken measure of worth.

The third terrain, platforms and archives, demonstrates the infrastructural scale of this governance. Academic repositories and citation indices privilege work that appears at steady intervals in standardised formats. Iterative collaborations, seasonal teaching repertoires, or evolving community protocols rarely count unless converted into archive-friendly outputs like journal articles or datasets. The translation strips away qualities like open-endedness and responsiveness, qualities central to many alternative scholarly traditions.

Over time, metrics reset the field’s sense of what counts, nudging researchers toward tempos the archive can capture. This narrows the ecology of scholarship, making irregular rhythms invisible. Invisibility, in turn, is conflated with irrelevance. Projects that refuse translation risk exclusion from recognition, funding, and advancement. Their survival depends on communities that value them outside formal reward systems, support that remains precarious.

Across all three terrains, the institution’s preferred beat emerges: not necessarily faster, but more regular. This is what AI prostheses can produce, what meetings test for, and what platforms reward. Regularity narrows the range of legitimate scholarly movement, filtering out entire ways of knowing. Resistance, through deliberate tempo shifts, fugitive circulation, or selective smoothing, must navigate this preference carefully, weighing the costs of noncompliance against those of passing.

These findings position rhythm as a structural dimension of governance in higher education, operating across policy, practice, and infrastructure without being named. They also open the way for the discussion that follows: an exploration of how compliance and resistance are co-produced in this temporal economy, and how a truly equitable academic culture would require multiplying the tempos through which knowledge is allowed to move.

**Analysis**

The evidence gathered through experimental manipulation, infrastructural design choices, and autoethnographic observation converges on a single, difficult truth: in contemporary higher education, the tempo of scholarly expression is not an incidental feature of style but an axis of governance. This governance operates not through explicit rules about sentence length or speaking pace, but through the sedimented preferences and unspoken calibrations embedded in review protocols, committee deliberations, and platform metrics. The institutional beat, its regularity, predictability, and ease of processing, becomes an unacknowledged standard of legitimacy, such that those who move otherwise are rendered risky, unclear, or deficient. It is in this quiet alignment of aesthetic preference with evaluative authority that the politics of rhythm reveal their full force.

The AI-prosthesis experiment isolates this politics with unusual clarity. That two manuscripts, identical in argument, structure, and evidence, could be received so differently based solely on cadence is more than a curiosity about style. It is a demonstration that the formal economies of higher education, peer review, editorial selection, conference programming, are primed to treat prosodic regularity as a proxy for intellectual coherence. This is not a matter of superficiality; rather, it is the structural conflation of ease of uptake with quality, a conflation whose roots lie deep in the cultural politics of language. Scholars such as Jay Dolmage (2017, p. 32) have traced this conflation to what he terms *academic ableism*: the preference for communicative forms that mimic the unmarked, able-bodied ideal of smooth, uninterrupted expression. In this context, the “smoothed” manuscript aligned with that ideal, eliciting praise for its “clarity” and “focus,” while the “unsmoothed” version’s irregularities, long clauses, sudden shifts in register, moments of conceptual density, were read as weaknesses to be corrected.

The implications are not merely aesthetic. When rhythm becomes a criterion of judgment, it functions as a filter that disadvantages scholars whose communicative styles are shaped by disability, neurodivergence, multilingualism, or alternative intellectual traditions. Christina V. Cedillo (2018, p. 73) has argued that communicational norms in academia disproportionately reward the already-privileged, because they align with the prosodic habits of those trained from early life to speak and write in the dominant mode. The AI-prosthesis experiment, by removing all other variables, exposes this bias in operation, showing not only that such norms exist, but that they can be artificially replicated or bypassed, raising ethical questions about whether using a language model to “pass” constitutes accommodation or assimilation.

It is in kairotic spaces, the moments of live, unrecorded interaction, that this politics becomes most embodied. Here, the calibration of tempo is not simply an evaluative criterion; it is the air of the room. In one hiring committee I observed, a candidate’s research presentation was followed by a Q&A in which the most rapid-fire respondents, those who could begin speaking within one or two seconds of a question’s end, were met with nods and verbal affirmations. A different candidate, whose thoughtful pauses extended to four or five seconds before answering, prompted visible restlessness: shifting in chairs, glances at watches, even an audible sigh from one committee member. When deliberations began, the language used to describe the first candidate included “quick on their feet” and “well prepared”; the second was “interesting, but not quite ready.” The dossiers told a different story, both met the stated criteria, and the slower respondent’s publications and teaching evaluations were, on paper, superior. Yet the decision leaned decisively toward the candidate whose performance matched the room’s tempo.

This is the lived reality of what Margaret Price (2011, p. 60) calls *kairotic space*: environments in which opportunity is distributed through timing more than through content. In such spaces, pauses can be read as uncertainty, irregular pacing as lack of polish, even when they are integral to the speaker’s thought process. The room teaches its tempo as the price of entry, and those who cannot or will not match it are subtly, but effectively, excluded. Importantly, this exclusion is almost never formalised; it hides behind the language of “fit,” “communication skills,” or “professionalism,” terms that are elastic enough to absorb any number of unstated preferences.

The infrastructural dimension of this governance extends the same logic across longer temporalities. Academic repositories, grant reporting systems, and engagement dashboards are designed to register regular, measurable outputs, quarterly reports, annual publications, steady citation accrual. As Johanna Drucker (2020, p. 142) observes, such systems are not neutral containers but active shapers of scholarly life: by privileging outputs that can be cleanly counted and compared, they incentivise research practices that conform to their temporal grids. In this environment, projects that develop irregularly, collaborative protocols refined over years, community-based research that moves at the speed of trust, iterative pedagogical designs, are structurally disadvantaged. They may be rich in impact and depth, but unless they are translated into archive-friendly surrogates (a peer-reviewed article, a dataset with a DOI, a final report), they fail to “count” in the ways that secure funding, promotion, or institutional prestige.

Fugitive circulation, as both method and tactic, is one response to this constraint. By intentionally keeping certain works outside the archive, shared in workshops, circulated via local networks, maintained as living documents, scholars can preserve tempos and forms that would otherwise be flattened. Yet this resistance is precarious. Without the imprimatur of a recognised output, such works risk invisibility, and invisibility is easily conflated with irrelevance in an academy governed by metrics. Here, the politics of rhythm intersects with the politics of visibility: to remain legible to the institution often requires surrendering the irregular pulse that gives a project its integrity.

The co-production of compliance and resistance within this temporal economy is complex. Tactical compliance, using AI to smooth prose for a particular submission, adopting the committee’s tempo during a job talk, producing an archive-friendly artefact to accompany a fugitive repertoire, can be a survival strategy. It can redistribute risk, opening doors that might otherwise remain closed. Yet, as Sara Ahmed (2012, p. 148) warns, passing in this way risks reinforcing the very norms one wishes to dismantle, because it leaves the standard intact. Resistance, in this frame, is not the wholesale rejection of evaluative criteria but the cultivation of multiplicity: the deliberate maintenance of alternative tempos alongside the capacity to navigate the dominant beat when necessary.

In practical terms, this means recognising that the politics of rhythm are not merely symbolic. They shape who is hired, who is published, whose work circulates, and whose careers are sustained. They are embedded in the micro-temporalities of conversation and in the macro-temporalities of output cycles, reinforced by both human judgment and machine metrics. The challenge in reshaping higher education, therefore, is not only to diversify content or expand access, but to redesign the temporal architectures that underwrite legitimacy itself. This involves confronting the ways that accessibility has been collapsed into stylistic sameness, a collapse that, while often framed as inclusive, operates as a homogenising force. True accessibility, as Ellen Barton (2006, p. 170) reminds us, is about multiplying pathways of uptake, not narrowing them to a single, frictionless track.

The analysis here insists that rhythm is not an ornament of communication but a site where power is enacted and contested. It is through rhythm that institutions naturalise their preferred modes of thought, masking aesthetic preference as objective quality. To make this visible is to open a space for negotiation, where compliance and resistance are not moral absolutes but tactical responses to the uneven distribution of temporal privilege.

The AI-prosthesis experiment’s implications extend beyond the surface of text into the deep structure of institutional life. It exposes what Foucault (1977, p. 184) called the “micro-physics of power”: the dispersed, often invisible operations that govern conduct through norms so ingrained they pass as natural. In this case, the norm is prosodic regularity, enforced not by policy documents but by the embodied reactions of reviewers, committee members, and colleagues. It is a disciplinary mechanism precisely because it is deniable, a reviewer can insist they are judging only “clarity” or “rigour,” while in fact responding to a rhythmic familiarity that reassures them they are in safe epistemic territory.

This deniability is key to its persistence. In a doctoral defence I observed, the external examiner praised the candidate’s “engaging style”, a comment that, when unpacked, referred almost entirely to the regular pacing of the candidate’s spoken responses. The committee congratulated itself on being “open to different styles” because the dissertation itself included a hybrid of ethnography and theory. Yet when I later asked why another candidate’s similarly hybrid dissertation had been judged “unclear,” one committee member admitted that “she just didn’t seem comfortable”, a phrase that in context meant her pauses were too long, her delivery uneven. The content was equivalent in complexity and originality; the difference lay in cadence. The governance of form thus hides inside what appear to be interpersonal impressions, protecting the institution from the charge of bias while reproducing it through tempo.

Judith Butler’s (1997, p. 136) notion of performativity helps clarify how this works: the academic subject comes into being through repeated acts, delivering papers, responding to questions, drafting manuscripts, that must cite the institution’s conventions to be recognisable. Rhythm is one such convention. To depart from it too far is to risk not being recognised as a member of the category “competent scholar.” This is not simply about fitting in; it is about survival in a system where recognition is tied to material resources, from hiring to publication to funding. Those who cannot or will not perform the beat, because of disability, linguistic background, or political choice, are rendered precarious.

The precarity becomes more pronounced in spaces where time is compressed and visibility is high. In a faculty search meeting, candidates’ teaching demonstrations were discussed immediately after they concluded. One demonstration involved a deliberately slowed pace, allowing students to process complex material before moving on. Several committee members noted that “the energy in the room dropped,” interpreting it as a sign of disengagement. Another candidate moved quickly through slides, punctuating each with an anecdote; this was praised as “keeping the students with her.” In both cases, the judgments were made within minutes, with no revisiting after the initial impressions. The slower candidate’s evaluations remained lukewarm, even when later evidence showed students retained more from her session. The committee’s tempo preferences had already locked in the decision.

This rapid lock-in mirrors what Berlant (2011, p. 199) described as “cruel optimism”: the attachment to forms, in this case, the form of the quick, smooth delivery, that promise to secure good pedagogy but in fact can undermine it. The institution’s optimism that regular, fluent rhythm equals effective communication blinds it to the possibility that other tempos might foster deeper engagement. By rewarding only the familiar beat, it narrows the pedagogical repertoire and penalises those whose methods require more temporal space.

The infrastructural layer compounds this narrowing. In an archival project on departmental minutes, I found that contributions recorded in writing disproportionately reflected those made in the dominant tempo of the meeting. Interjections, clarifications, and rephrasings, often made by faster speakers, were more likely to be transcribed than slower, more deliberate statements. Over years, this created a paper trail in which certain individuals appeared more active, more central, more authoritative. The archive, which future committees might consult to trace “institutional memory,” was thus already shaped by the kairotic tempo politics of the original meetings. As Derrida (1995, p. 11) reminds us, “there is no political power without control of the archive.” Here, control is exercised not through overt censorship but through the subtle privileging of speech that fits the room’s pace.

Even in ostensibly asynchronous platforms, the demand for regularity persists. Academic social networks like ResearchGate or institutional profile systems encourage, and in some cases automate, updates that keep the scholar “visible” in regular pulses: a preprint uploaded, a project milestone announced, a conference presentation listed. Algorithms elevate profiles that show steady activity, creating a feedback loop in which the visible become more visible. This echoes Chun’s (2016, p. 113) point about the tyranny of the update: the pressure to produce not simply work but the appearance of constant, regular work, regardless of the actual rhythm of research. Those who operate in bursts, or whose projects require long periods without public output, fall behind in visibility rankings, which in turn can affect perceptions of relevance when hiring or funding committees “look them up.”

Fugitive circulation intervenes here by refusing to feed the update cycle. But its refusal has costs, as I have experienced in my own career. A collaborative research-poetry project I co-led unfolded through irregular gatherings, sometimes months apart, with outputs that were performative rather than textual. We deliberately chose not to film or transcribe these performances, allowing them to live only in memory and local conversation. Within the community, this was a form of care, protecting the work from extraction and misinterpretation. In the eyes of the institution, however, it was a void. Annual reviews listed no outputs; grant applications could not point to citable artefacts. The very strategy that preserved the project’s integrity also rendered it invisible to those holding institutional power.

This tension between integrity and visibility is central to the politics of reshaping higher education. Any attempt to diversify knowledge production must confront not only what content is valued but what tempos of production are made legible. Without infrastructural change, the burden falls on individuals to either conform to the beat or accept marginalisation. Tactical compliance, smoothing a manuscript for review, speeding up in an interview, packaging a living project into a fixed output, may secure short-term survival, but it risks reinforcing the very norms it navigates. As Ahmed (2012, p. 148) warns, the labour of fitting in can make the walls of the institution feel “softer” while leaving them just as enclosing.

The AI-prosthesis experiment offers one more cautionary note. While it exposes the bias toward regularity, it also demonstrates how easily that bias can be exploited. If a language model can adjust rhythm without altering content, it can be used to pass institutional filters without challenging them. This could become a compliance tool as much as a resistance tactic: smoothing to get the paper accepted, then returning to one’s own style in less monitored spaces. The danger is that the underlying preference for regularity remains unchallenged, its legitimacy reaffirmed each time smoothing “works.” Resistance, then, must be careful not to reproduce the conditions it opposes.

To genuinely reshape higher education’s politics of rhythm would require interventions at multiple levels: retraining reviewers to recognise and value diverse cadences; redesigning archives and platforms to accommodate living, irregular outputs; shifting committee cultures so that tempo is not conflated with competence. Such changes cannot be mandated solely from the top, because the enforcement of rhythm operates through dispersed, everyday practices, the raised eyebrow at a pause, the offhand comment about “flow,” the automated ranking of regular updaters. They require what Foucault (1978, p. 95) called “counter-conduct”: collective, sustained refusals to move to the prescribed beat, coupled with the invention of new rhythms that can hold in the face of institutional smoothing.

**Discussion**

If rhythm is governance, the politics of reshaping higher education must be understood not only in terms of policy documents or strategic plans, but in the micropolitics of how ideas move and the temporal architectures that channel them. Compliance and resistance are enacted not simply in what is said, but in whether it moves at the pace, in the phrasing, and with the breath that the institution recognises as proper. That recognition is not neutral: it is the product of sedimented preferences shaped by cultural scripts of authority, academic decorum, and communicative efficiency, reinforced by infrastructures that store and circulate knowledge.

When committees praise “clarity,” “rigor,” and “focus,” they are often praising the way an argument behaves in time. That behaviour is learned, rehearsed, and policed through training, peer modelling, and the implicit hierarchies of review and hiring. The experiment here shows that identical arguments, stripped of all differences except cadence, receive measurably different receptions. This bias is often unconscious, but it shapes who advances, whose work is taken up, and which epistemologies set disciplinary agendas.

Resistance to this governance of form cannot rest solely on individual will. One may “glitch” writing or speech to assert difference, but as long as evaluation equates irregularity with incompetence, such resistance will be penalised. Likewise, tactical compliance, smoothing prose with AI, matching the tempo of a meeting, may secure survival but rarely shifts the structural terms of inclusion. The challenge is to move from individual tactics to collective and infrastructural interventions.

Collectively, scholars can normalise multiple tempos by cultivating peer review cultures where variation in pacing is expected. Reviewer training could offer language for distinguishing opacity from density, poor organisation from deliberate complexity. As with inclusive citation practices, journals could guide evaluators to see rhythm as a meaningful dimension of scholarly practice rather than an incidental flourish.

Infrastructurally, archives, platforms, and metrics must be recalibrated so that repertoires outside the standard beat still register as legitimate contributions. Repository metadata could account for iterative or collaborative work; grant reporting could value process alongside product; dashboards could present multiple, non-linear indicators of impact. Without such change, the institution will continue to misread irregular tempo as absence, erasing the work of those who already bear the greatest translational burden.

Policy change should also address the temporal choreography of kairotic spaces. If the unspoken tempo of meetings or interviews decisively shapes judgments of “fit,” then meeting design becomes an equity intervention: agendas circulated in advance, structured turns, intentional pauses, and rotating facilitation can make room for multiple rhythms. These are not procedural niceties but acts of temporal redistribution, expanding both airtime and the affective safety to speak in one’s own tempo without penalty.

AI complicates this picture by blurring the line between accommodation and assimilation. Used reflexively, it can function as a prosthetic, enabling scholars to meet institutional expectations without erasing epistemic commitments. Used unreflectively, it risks reinforcing the norms it helps individuals survive. Reflexive use, acknowledging mediation, situating its purpose, can turn AI into a tool for exposing the institution’s rhythmic preferences rather than silently complying with them.

This discussion also reframes “accessibility” in higher education. Too often it means flattening form into predictable pacing, shorter sentences, simplified syntax. While useful for some, this erases the density and irregularity that make other tempos legible. A more radical accessibility multiplies tempos: offering transcripts and recordings, layering summaries with full-length versions, and creating parallel modes of engagement so that no single pacing is made the gold standard.

Destabilising evaluators’ comfort with a single predictable beat is not a flaw but a condition for justice in knowledge production. Rigour can inhabit slow, meandering arguments as much as rapid-fire ones; fit can mean making space for difference rather than training difference to fit.

Ultimately, governance of form is a core mechanism of higher education. Challenging it demands individual reflexivity, collective cultivation of multiple tempos, infrastructural redesign to value irregularity, and policy shifts that redistribute temporal power in kairotic spaces. None of these alone can undo the coupling of legitimacy and regularity, but together they can loosen its grip, making space for thought that takes the time it needs, for scholarship that refuses to march to a single beat.

**Conclusion: Temporal Equity as Structural Change**

If legitimacy in higher education is rationed through rhythm, then governance of form is not incidental, it is the institution’s operating principle. Across committees, peer review, platform metrics, and kairotic rooms, regularity is silently upheld as the measure of intellectual worth. The pace that moves without hesitation, the clause that resolves predictably, the steady publication stream, these are read as competence. What is less visible is the cost: the exclusion of forms, bodies, and knowledges that move otherwise.

Compliance and resistance are not opposites but co-produced strategies within temporal regimes. Compliance, via AI smoothing, matching a meeting’s tempo, or translating irregular work into metric-friendly outputs, can shield precarious scholars but rarely shifts the structure. Resistance, through glitch, sustained irregularity, or alternative infrastructures, can expose and disrupt the beat but often incurs penalties. The goal is an institution where compliance is optional, not survival, and resistance can occur without disproportionate risk.

Reshaping evaluation requires reviewers, panels, and funding bodies to distinguish cadence from content, density from opacity, and to separate barriers to understanding from constitutive complexity. Clarity is not uniform rhythm. A genuinely inclusive practice makes multiple tempos navigable through thoughtful scaffolding co-produced by author and institution.

Policy should embed temporal equity as a criterion. In publishing, this means reviewers naming the kinds of movement in a text and distinguishing translation from assimilation. In hiring and promotion, it means valuing repertoires that unfold slowly, irregularly, or cyclically, rather than penalising them for failing to produce a constant beat.

Institutional design must extend this equity into meetings, teaching schedules, and decision-making. Speed should not be conflated with competence; pauses can be generative, irregular pulses as intellectually weighty as steady ones. Facilitators should be assessed on their ability to host multiple tempos in a single space.

AI and other prosthetic tools should be recognised as potential accessibility supports, but their use must be transparent. Accommodation is not assimilation: mediation should open multiple paths into the work, not overwrite an author’s cadence.

Archives and platforms should be reengineered to value irregularity: metadata for iterative or seasonal work, citation practices for sustained engagement, dashboards that register impact beyond throughput. Without this, irregular forms remain invisible, and invisibility is read as irrelevance.

Temporal equity is not an accommodation for the “slow” or “different” at the edges; it is a redesign of the core to hold multiplicity without forcing sameness. This will unsettle, complicate metrics, and disrupt tidy timelines, but it will also make the institution more resilient, diverse, and just.

When higher education can host multiple tempos without penalty, it can sustain forms of knowledge now filtered out before taking root. Compliance and resistance become tactics in a shared project of widening the commons. The beat ceases to be a border and becomes shifting ground on which new thought can take hold. The governance of form is the governance of access; justice requires building it into the breath of the room, the cadence of the page, and the pulse of the archive.
