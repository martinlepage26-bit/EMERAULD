CHAPTER ONE – THE ROOM WITHOUT SOUND

He will always remember it as the day the
refrigerator stopped humming.
At first, he thinks it’s a power outage. The silence
sits on him like a second skin, raw, immediate,
sticky. He stands in the doorway of the kitchen,
barefoot, his hair unwashed, the smell of oranges
drying in the sink. The morning is bright but cold;
light enters without warmth, brittle as frost on glass.
The hum that had always filled the air, that invisible
current linking one second to the next, has vanished.
The gap it leaves behind is unbearable. He does not
yet know that this silence will become his native
language.
For a long time, he just stands there. Every object
around him seems slightly altered, less itself. The
sugar jar glints too sharply. The bowl of fruit on the
counter leans toward the light as though trying to
escape. Even the air seems bruised. He notices the
sound his breath makes, the faint friction of lungs
moving through absence, and realizes he has never
really listened to breathing before.

3

Then he hears the ticking of the clock. Too loud, too
deliberate. Each second lands like a question he
cannot answer. He thinks of calling out to her, but
the shape of her name feels foreign. It has been too
long since he pronounced it without irony or
impatience. He fears that if he says it now, it will
not come out right.
His mother lies on the couch, one arm bent oddly
behind her, as though reaching for a remote she
never found. Her body looks casual, but the stillness
underneath it is wrong. From the doorway, she
seems asleep, caught mid-breath, eyes half-closed,
mouth slightly open. The television screen is black,
reflecting the pale oblong of the window. The
reflection trembles faintly, perhaps his own motion,
perhaps the afterimage of life still rehearsing itself.
He crosses the room. The floorboards creak in a
slow, uncertain rhythm. For a moment, the sound
almost comforts him: proof that the world still
produces something. The smell of detergent mixes
with the faint perfume she always wore, cheap
floral with a trace of smoke, and something inside
him folds. He opens his mouth.
“Maman?” The word comes out wrong, too small,
like a note played on a broken instrument. He hates
waking her. He always has. There was always that
moment when she startled from sleep, half animal,
4

half human, snapping at whatever had touched her.
Even now, he half expects that reaction, a sleepy
protest, a sigh, the flick of her hand dismissing him.
But she does not move. The word evaporates in the
air between them. He stands there, ridiculous,
repeating it, softer, then louder, the sound of his
own voice unconvincing. The silence afterward
swells like an organ pipe, pressurized and bright. It
feels intelligent, purposeful. It feels as though it is
listening back.
He steps closer. The silence thickens. It coats his
skin, fills the gaps between heartbeat and thought.
He crouches beside her. Her sweater is navy, worn
at the elbows, the fibers matted and uneven. When
he touches her shoulder, the texture is strange,
damp, heavy, almost pliant. Underneath the fabric,
the flesh has changed state, from warmth to
something else. The skin beneath his hand no longer
belongs to her.
He says her name again. The third time, it is not a
word but a gasp folded in half. It leaves his mouth
incomplete.
He waits for something: the usual complaint, the
irritation at being touched, the joke about his hair,
his lateness, his uselessness. But all that comes is

5

more quiet, a dense, swollen quiet that swallows the
edges of thought.
He kneels. The silence pools around him. It feels
alive, like breath withheld too long. He touches her
again, her wrist this time, the hollow where the
pulse should be. Nothing. The absence of that
rhythm feels louder than any scream could.
He does not remember standing. He does not
remember picking up the phone. He knows he must
have, that at some point his body moved, that his
mouth produced words. But all memory of it has
been erased, overwritten by static. Later, he will
recall fragments: the cold plastic of the receiver
against his cheek; his own voice saying numbers he
no longer recognized; the phrase “she’s not
breathing” repeated in a tone that didn’t sound like
his own. Everything between those moments is
blank.
When the paramedics arrive, they move like people
in a language he doesn’t speak. Two of them, a man
and a woman, glide through the doorway, their
voices clipped, practiced, fluent. Their efficiency
disgusts him. Their gestures have rhythm, their
words have symmetry. He hates them for the calm
precision of their phrases, for the way their speech
carries the illusion of wisdom. They know exactly
what to do. He can only watch.
6

“She’s been gone a while,” the man says finally,
checking a watch, not a pulse. The words land on
Matisse’s chest like a punch. Gone a while. As if
she had gone to the store. As if she might come
back with milk. As if language could soften this.
They zip the bag. The sound slices through the air.
He feels the zipper in his teeth. That is the first real
sound since the hum disappeared.
After that, time fractures. The house fills with
strangers. The landlord appears, smelling faintly of
tobacco and damp wool. A neighbour hovers near
the door, murmuring condolences that dissolve
before reaching him. A man from the coroner’s
office arrives, a large man, polite, perfumed with
leather and the faint sweetness of latex gloves. They
talk in low tones, each trying to sound competent.
They use words that make him sick: deceased,
transport, identification. They sound rehearsed,
learned long ago for moments like this. Their
smoothness feels like betrayal.
“She was your mother, right?” one of them asks.​
He nods.​
“Do you have someone who can come get you?”
He thinks of his sister, of her sharp voice, her
endless certainty, her way of drowning silence with
speech. He can already hear her crying, loudly,

7

theatrically. He resents it. He envies it. He tells the
man yes, she’s coming. She isn’t. He hasn’t called
her.
The coroner signs papers. The sound of pen on
paper is like fabric tearing. Then there is the sound
of the zipper again, shorter this time. The bag
closes, and the world tilts. He presses his hands
over his ears, but silence pushes back through his
palms.
Later, how much later, he cannot tell, the house
empties. The air still smells of antiseptic. He walks
back into the kitchen. The refrigerator is still dead.
The orange on the counter has begun to shrivel. He
picks up the knife, turns it once, twice, then cuts the
orange perfectly in half. The sound is obscene: a
wet sigh, intimate, too human. The juice runs across
the counter in thin threads. He watches it pool,
thinking absurdly of veins.
He sits. The orange glows under the weak light, its
halves twin wounds. He knows he should cry. He
doesn’t. He knows he should feel something, rage,
guilt, fear, but instead there is only stillness. A
smooth, perfect emptiness. He bites the orange. It
tastes metallic, almost medicinal, as though the
world itself has rusted overnight.

8

When his sister finally arrives, she doesn’t knock.
She storms in, smelling of ammonia and winter air,
her uniform still dusted with hair clippings. She
takes one look at him, and the sharpness drains from
her face.
“Mat?” she says. Her voice breaks on the vowel.
“What, ?”
He points to the living room. She goes. He waits.
The silence between them expands. Then comes the
sound, an animal sound, deeper than grief, older
than language. When she returns, her face is wet.
She keeps shaking her head, repeating no like a
prayer. She asks if he called anyone. He nods. She
asks if they took her. He nods again. She stares at
the orange halves on the table.
“Did you eat?” she asks.
He wants to laugh. Instead, he looks at her hands.
Her fingers are trembling, her nails still rimmed
with dye. He thinks: these are the same hands that
braided my hair when I was little, the same hands
that learned to hold scissors, the same hands that cut
and shape and chatter all day. They never stop
moving. They are fluent. Fluent in everything
except what matters.

9

The day stretches. The light moves across the floor
like a slow clock with no face. People call. People
come. The landlord brings tea. A neighbour drops
off soup. A priest appears, uninvited, speaking of
rest, of peace, of the better place. His words glide
smoothly, meaningless as air. Matisse hears only
tone. Tone without substance. The priest’s voice is
too polished; grief should not be fluent.
He escapes upstairs. Her bedroom smells of
lavender, dust, and the faint sweetness of sleep. On
the nightstand, a lamp glows. The shade casts a
small circle of gold on the wall. He finds a
hairbrush, a half-empty pill bottle, her reading
glasses folded neatly beside a paperback. He
touches the dent in her pillow. Still warm. Still
almost her.
He sits at the edge of the bed. The silence in the
room feels different, denser, private. He presses his
palm against the duvet, imagining her weight still
there, the shape of her body stamped into fabric. He
pictures the moment she died, the body realizing
itself as body, the heart slowing like a metronome
losing time. He imagines her looking around,
confused, realizing no one is coming, that he is
asleep in the next room. He imagines her saying his
name once, softly, perfectly pronounced, the last
fluent sound of her life.

10

He thinks of all the times he refused to answer her,
every slammed door, every silence he mistook for
power. Now he would give anything to hear her
voice mispronounce his name.
Night comes slowly. He lies on the couch where she
died. The fabric smells faintly of her shampoo, a
floral synthetic scent that makes his throat close.
The silence breathes through the house, patient,
curious. He dreams of her in the kitchen, humming
a song he cannot place, something in 3/4, gentle but
incomplete. He tries to hum along, but the melody
keeps slipping. The words won’t come. His voice
cracks. In the dream, she looks up, smiling, as if to
say, see? you never learned to listen.
When he wakes, the room is dim. There’s a faint
ringing in his ears, a ghost frequency. He covers
them with his palms. The ringing persists. The
silence presses harder.
Morning arrives like a mistake. His sister is gone,
off to arrange the funeral, to speak for both of them.
He makes coffee and pours it down the sink. He
cannot stand the sound of it. He sits at the table,
staring at the dried orange peels. They look
fossilized. He imagines archaeologists finding them
centuries later and declaring: evidence of ritual.

his feet, paws twitching. The city is still singing;
only its tempo has slowed.

At sunrise, he walks to the river once more. The
snow muffles sound, blanketing the city in
deliberate hush. Footsteps carve soft parentheses
into the white. The water slides under ice,
whispering. He stands long enough for cold to find
his bones.
Somewhere, a bell rings, E and F, forever
unresolved. It feels like the world reminding itself
to breathe unevenly.
“I remember,” he says.
The air accepts the words, unbothered. A moment
later, the river ripples once, pink-gold, then silver,
before resuming brown. The same hue as the cat’s
fur, as the first shimmer from the kettle, as the spiral
that remade the sky.
He smiles. Perhaps everything ever loved ends this
way, folded into another element’s patience.

Evening again. Windows glow, radio songs drift.
No miracles, just lives continuing their imperfect

322

tempo. Matisse feeds the cat, opens the window, lets
the cold in. A child’s voice floats upward, singing
nonsense syllables. He listens until it fades.
He touches a single piano key, not to play but to feel
contact. The note he imagines never sounds; its
absence vibrates nonetheless. Silence has learned to
sing.
He looks out once more. The city gleams under
snowlight, breathing. He thinks of everything that
happened and everything that refused to stop
happening.
“Good night,” he says, to the cat, the stranger, the
hum still buried somewhere under concrete.
The wind shifts. A shimmer crosses the glass, faint
but deliberate. He smiles, ambiguous, hopeful,
nostalgic, and lets the darkness settle around him,
not as curtain,​
but as the long, imperfect chord​
that keeps the world alive.
He almost laughed, because of course even endings
have to learn their own timing.
