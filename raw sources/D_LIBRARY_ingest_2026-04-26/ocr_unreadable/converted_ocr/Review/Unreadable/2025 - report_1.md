Astrology as Social Grammar: Youth Culture’s Re-enchantment of the Zodiac

Introduction

Across Gen Z and Millennial publics, astrology is less a cosmology than a conversational technology. Recent survey work shows breadth without heavy reliance: in May 2025, the Pew Research Center reported that “30% of U.S. adults say they consult astrology (or a horoscope), tarot cards or a fortune teller at least once a year,” and that most do so “just for fun,” with only about 1% saying they rely a lot on such practices for major decisions (Pew Research Center, 2025). The finding reframes the phenomenon: what is spreading is a shared idiom for identity work, intimacy signaling, and low-stakes coordination, not a populace convinced that planets push destinies. ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This idiom thrives because platform architectures make it legible and sticky. Limor Shifman defines internet memes as “a group of digital items… circulated, imitated, and transformed via the internet,” a form whose brevity and remixability match horoscope snippets and sign stereotypes (Shifman, 2013). On TikTok, occult and astrological content often rides what St. Lawrence calls “algorithmic divination,” in which users experience the feed itself as a meaning-partner: “the technology… [is] a powerful partner… a practice that can be understood as algorithmic divination” (St. Lawrence, 2024). Together these accounts suggest that youth astrology is infrastructured by recommendation systems and memetic formats that reward portable, recognizable categories—exactly what the twelve signs supply. ([Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jcc4.12013?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/15/4/435?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Henry Jenkins](https://henryjenkins.org/blog/2014/02/a-meme-is-a-terrible-thing-to-waste-an-interview-with-limor-shifman-part-one.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A long historical view cautions against treating today’s usage as a simple relapse into pre-science. In Mesopotamia and the Hellenistic world, what Francesca Rochberg terms “celestial sciences” braided omen literature, horoscopy, and mathematical astronomy; our strict modern partitions are retrospective (Rochberg, 2004). In early modern Europe, astrology was woven into medicine and governance; as Lauren Kassell summarizes, “Astrology was a serious healthcare option during much of the early modern period,” taught at universities and mobilized in clinical casebooks (Kassell, 2014/2018). Even reformers like Johannes Kepler negotiated the boundary, calling vulgar prediction “a foolish daughter,” while conceding that this daughter “supports her wise but needy mother, Astronomy” (Kepler, 1610/1627, trans. Negus). The point is not equivalence but contingency: astronomy/astrology and alchemy/chemistry were disentangled through institutional “boundary-work,” not by a single decisive experiment that erased social functions overnight. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/heavenly-writing/9A24735C23CA63EF8E1819B283B39F47?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/casebooks?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [faculty.umb.edu](https://faculty.umb.edu/gary_zabel/Courses/Phil%20281b/Philosophy%20of%20Magic/Arcana/Renaissance/15kep-en.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [en.wikisource.org](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kepler/Chapter_6?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Contemporary “science vs. fake news” debates reprise these struggles over credibility, now inside attention markets. Naomi Oreskes argues that we should trust science because of its communal vetting—“what makes scientific claims reliable is the process by which they are vetted”—and, when we cannot personally audit evidence, because consensus works as a practical proxy (Oreskes, 2019). Meanwhile, high-novelty, high-arousal items travel farthest online: in a landmark study of Twitter, false news spread “farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth,” largely via human sharing rather than bots (Vosoughi, Roy, & Aral, 2018). In such an ecology, astrology’s social grammar prospers not by outperforming astrophysics but by outperforming rival etiquettes of self-description: it is fast, funny, and mutual. ([press.princeton.edu](https://press.princeton.edu/ideas/naomi-oreskes-on-why-trust-science?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9425825/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2018/03/08/false-news-spreads-faster-1520537127?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Crucially, use does not imply causal belief. If planetary positions shaped relationship outcomes, population-scale records should register it. They do not. A longitudinal analysis of Swedish marriages and divorces (1968–2001) finds “no consistent evidence” that astrologically favorable sun-sign pairings are overrepresented in marriages or less likely to divorce (Helgertz & Scott, 2020). The null result is compatible with the Pew picture: youth draw on astrology as a social lexicon—playful heuristics for affinity, friction, and timing—while reserving high-stakes trust for other frameworks. ([SpringerOpen](https://genus.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41118-020-00103-5?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This paper proceeds from two interpretive claims. First, astrology in youth culture operates as social grammar: a folk pragmatics for introductions, flirting, boundary setting, and repairing minor conflict in group chats and campus life. Second, the grammar is season-coded. Reading each sign as a “season-made style” translates the zodiac from celestial causation to calendrical sociology: Aries as spring initiation; Virgo as rentrée logistics; Scorpio as late-autumn threshold work; Pisces as late-winter passage. Such mappings do explanatory work without metaphysical commitments: they capture how annual rhythms train different social competences and why those competences, condensed into sign-labels, feel resonant in conversation. This reframing moves the inquiry from “do the stars cause X?” to “why do these categories travel so well among peers?”

The contributions are therefore threefold. The literature review consolidates recent survey and platform research on the revival (Pew, Shifman, St. Lawrence) and situates it within histories of boundary-work (Rochberg; Kassell) and credibility (Oreskes; Vosoughi et al.). The middle section develops a sign-by-sign, season-anchored social interpretation calibrated to youth cultural routines and platform affordances. The analysis then uses empirical nulls (Helgertz & Scott) and science-studies theory to argue for a post-demarcation approach to public scholarship: keep empirical claims hard, but craft hospitable formats that meet the same social needs zodiac talk currently meets.

Finally, the paper’s stance is neither apologetic nor debunking. It takes seriously the social functions that astrology now performs while declining astronomical causal claims. Following Oreskes, the legitimacy of science in public life is a design problem—of mediation, participation, and timing—not merely a policing problem. Following the platform literature, the stickiness of zodiac discourse is a feature of memetic and algorithmic infrastructures, not of mass irrationality. The question that follows is practical: how might evidence-based communication borrow astrology’s strengths—shared scripts, seasonal timing, convivial formats—without conceding the sky? ([press.princeton.edu](https://press.princeton.edu/ideas/naomi-oreskes-on-why-trust-science?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jcc4.12013?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/15/4/435?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Literature Review

Recent survey work establishes both the breadth and the tenor of contemporary engagement with astrology among younger publics. A nationally representative Pew study (fielded October 21–27, 2024; released May 21, 2025) reports that “30% of U.S. adults say they consult astrology (or a horoscope), tarot cards or a fortune teller at least once a year,” and—crucially—“most do so just for fun,” with only about one percent saying they rely a lot on these practices for major decisions (Rotolo, Pew Research Center). This same report documents elevated uptake among 18–29 year-olds, women, and LGBTQ+ respondents, a pattern echoed in subsequent coverage that highlights especially high annual consultation rates within LGBTQ+ communities. These figures locate the revival less as doctrinal belief than as a light-touch, recurring cultural practice—something people do together, share, and joke about—rather than a decisive guide to action. ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Them](https://www.them.us/story/lgbtq-americans-twice-as-likely-to-be-into-astrology-study-shows?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Why does this practice travel so efficiently online? One answer lies in the form itself. Limor Shifman’s account of internet memes, now canonical in media studies, defines an internet meme as “a group of digital items… created with awareness of each other, and circulated, imitated, and transformed via the internet by multiple users.” The zodiac’s bite-size templates—lists, stereotypes, compatibility grids—slot neatly into this definition, inviting endless variation while preserving instant recognizability. The same traits that make memes sticky—brevity, remixability, stance—also make astrological talk an ideal vehicle for low-stakes identity work and affinity signaling in youth culture. ([Henry Jenkins](https://henryjenkins.org/blog/2014/02/a-meme-is-a-terrible-thing-to-waste-an-interview-with-limor-shifman-part-one.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [direct.mit.edu](https://direct.mit.edu/books/book/2214/Memes-in-Digital-Culture/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A complementary answer foregrounds platform imaginaries: how users explain to themselves the personalization and timeliness of what the feed shows them. Drawing on TikTok’s WitchTok, St. Lawrence describes “algorithmic divination,” in which creators “consider the technology itself a powerful partner with which to conduct readings,” effectively treating recommendation systems and AI filters as quasi-oracular collaborators. In that frame, receiving a hyper-relevant horoscope on the For You page feels like a meaningful sign not because the planets acted, but because the machine “answered.” Astrology’s portable language thus fuses smoothly with the experiential grammar of feeds—serendipity, vibes, timing—without requiring metaphysical assent. ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/15/4/435?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

These cultural and experiential accounts sit alongside a now-substantial literature on virality and misinformation that clarifies why short, affective, non-falsifiable snippets dominate attention. In a landmark Science paper based on ~126,000 rumor cascades, Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral show that “falsehood diffused significantly farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth,” with novelty and emotion as key drivers and humans—not bots—the primary vectors. Horoscope memes and sign stereotypes are structurally aligned with these dynamics: they are surprising enough to feel fresh, generic enough to be widely relatable, and unburdened by time-stamped predictions that could be quickly proven wrong. ([Science](https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.aap9559?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

At the same time, treating astrology as a social grammar does not entail endorsing astrological causation. When predictive claims are tested with adequate data, effects vanish. Using individual-level Swedish registers for 1968–2001, Helgertz and Scott conclude that their “results fail to provide any consistent evidence to support the notion that astrologically more compatible couples are either overrepresented among observed marital unions or associated with a lower risk of divorce.” That null aligns with the Pew pattern—frequent, playful use paired with minimal high-stakes reliance—and helps analytically separate astrology’s social utility from causal efficacy. ([SpringerOpen](https://genus.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41118-020-00103-5?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

If the present is social rather than celestial, the past was more entangled. Historians of science have shown that astronomy and astrology, medicine and divination, long coexisted as “celestial sciences.” Francesca Rochberg’s reconstruction of Mesopotamian scholarship emphasizes omen compilation and systematic observation as part of a single intellectual field spanning divination, horoscopy, and mathematical astronomy; the neat modern partition is retrospective. Early modern Europe continued that braid: as Lauren Kassell puts it, “Astrology was a serious healthcare option during much of the early modern period,” integrated into university training and clinical practice. Even reformers negotiated the line. Kepler could deride “this Astrology” as a “foolish daughter” while asking, memorably, “what would happen to her mother, the highly reasonable Astronomy, if she did not have this foolish daughter.” Such moments remind us that boundary-work is historical and institutional: what now reads as an obvious demarcation emerged through centuries of curricular change, professionalization, and shifting media. ([bmcr.brynmawr.edu](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005.06.29?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/casebooks?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [faculty.umb.edu](https://faculty.umb.edu/gary_zabel/Courses/Phil%20281b/Philosophy%20of%20Magic/Arcana/Renaissance/15kep-en.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A parallel historiography has revised our picture of alchemy and chemistry. Works by Newman and Principe recover early modern “chymistry” as experimental, instrumented, and materially productive; the later chemistry/alchemy split was as much a reclassification as a discovery narrative. This comparative lens matters for the present because it cautions against moralized binaries in public discourse—science versus superstition, facts versus vibes—by showing how practical repertoires migrate across categories as institutions and audiences change. In that spirit, Bruno Latour’s meditation on the “modern constitution” suggests that we never cleanly separated Nature from Society; rather, we continually produce hybrids while talking as if they were apart. Youth astrology—memetic, algorithmically personalized, socially useful—is one such hybrid.

A final set of references concerns credibility. Naomi Oreskes argues that “we should trust science because it has a rigorous process for vetting claims… [and] consensus is key,” highlighting the communal—not individual—basis of scientific authority. That stance complements the rest of this literature: it explains how one can respect scientific consensus on matters of fact while acknowledging that astrology’s present-day value lies in organizing talk, not predicting outcomes. Put differently, science earns trust through institutionalized scrutiny; zodiac discourse earns circulation through social usefulness and platform-native form. Neither can be understood without its respective community. ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2019/nov/03/naomi-oreskes-interview-why-trust-science-climate-donald-trump-vaccine?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [TIME](https://time.com/5709691/why-trust-science/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Taken together, these sources converge on a coherent picture. Surveys show broad, mostly playful engagement concentrated among younger and LGBTQ+ publics; platform studies and meme theory explain the zodiac’s portability as a social and expressive toolkit; virality research clarifies its advantage over hard, time-bound claims; demographic and experimental tests undermine causal pretensions; and histories of knowledge warn us that bright demarcations are recent achievements, not eternal truths. The literature therefore legitimates a working premise for the rest of this paper: in youth culture, astrology functions chiefly as a meme-ready social grammar—a way to talk about selves, relationships, and timing in an algorithmic public sphere—while the warrant for empirical claims remains where Oreskes places it, in communities organized to vet and revise knowledge. ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Henry Jenkins](https://henryjenkins.org/blog/2014/02/a-meme-is-a-terrible-thing-to-waste-an-interview-with-limor-shifman-part-one.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/15/4/435?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Science](https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.aap9559?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [SpringerOpen](https://genus.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41118-020-00103-5?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/casebooks?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [faculty.umb.edu](https://faculty.umb.edu/gary_zabel/Courses/Phil%20281b/Philosophy%20of%20Magic/Arcana/Renaissance/15kep-en.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Social interpretation of the zodiac, sign by sign

The cycle begins when the light returns and institutions lurch from planning to action. In late March and April, what youth culture recognizes as Aries season tends to feel like open doors and green lights: admissions letters drop, spring leagues and shows kick off, internships and capstone projects move from rumour to schedule. In that ecology, the prized social competence is initiation. It is efficient, even considerate, to move first, to supply a draft others can revise, to risk a brisk “let’s try it Friday and see.” The conversational shorthand that follows—friends calling someone “very Aries” when they volunteer, blurt, or jump—does not invoke planetary force so much as a season-made etiquette: at this moment in the year, decisiveness is a public good, and brisk candor saves the group time.

As April turns to May, the rhythm favors tending over leaping. Taurus season coincides, in many school and work calendars, with steady build and proof-of-work: rehearsals become recitals, experiments become results, midterms become grading queues, and home life asks for spring cleaning, minor repairs, and resource sense. The competence rehearsed here is care under constraint—budgeting energy, making tools last, cooking literal and figurative meals that travel well. In talk, Taurus labels signal reliability and sensory intelligence; the joke about stubbornness points to a real trade-off: when quality and continuity finally start paying dividends, pivots feel wasteful. Youth culture’s Taurus memes valorize the friend who brings the right adapter, the blanket, the snack; the mood is not mystical earthiness but ordinary stewardship under deadlines.

By late May and June, the social field atomizes and multiplies: exams end even as parties, award nights, festivals, Pride announcements, and summer itineraries accelerate. In that Gemini weather, the valuable skill is linkage. People who keep threads moving—introducing circles, shuttling information, keeping talk light enough to include new arrivals—become informal infrastructure. The stereotype of scatteredness merely names the cost: you cannot sample everything without risking depth. Hence a common youth-culture workaround: pair low-friction, many-to-many contact (memes, DMs, drop-ins) with scheduled “depth appointments.” Gemini humor works because the season truly does reward agility and conversational play; the sign becomes a tag for switch-speed rather than a metaphysical claim.

Late June into July pushes the center of gravity toward home bases, water, shade, and families of origin or choice. Camps, barbecues, porches, and cottage shares define Cancer weather, along with childcare rotations and heat logistics. The competence that matters is refuge-making: anticipating needs, regulating vibes, noticing when overstimulation starts to fray edges. Youth talk about Cancer as “soft” or “homebody,” but the season’s lesson is strategic: in conditions of heat and crowding, comfort is infrastructural. A household that works—physically and emotionally—expands everyone’s capacity to show up elsewhere. The social script invites boundaries as care: a quiet night in, a short guest list, a rain plan.

As July peaks, so does the appetite for spectacle. Weddings, festivals, outdoor cinema, team tournaments, and tourism produce stages that somebody must hold. Leo season rewards visible stewardship: leading the toast, gathering stragglers, making celebration feel safe. Public devotion—introducing your partner proudly, hyping a friend’s release, producing a theme—reads, in this slice of the year, as generosity rather than ego. The risk is familiar: when the room always needs a center, a person can forget that spotlights overheat. In youth practice, a healthy Leo script alternates show and shade—big night, then genuine off-day—and treats praise and debrief as a pair.

August slides toward September and the mood sharpens. Leases turn over, syllabi and workplace policies post, wardrobes and budgets reset. Virgo season is where culture re-learns checklists, labels, and mise en place. Among students and early-career workers, the friend who catches defects before they cost a weekend earns high status; “Virgo energy” is the meme for that specific grace: practical service without fuss. The predictable trap is perfection paralysis; the local cure is procedural rather than moral—definitions of done, ship-then-iterate calendars, rotating the invisible labour rather than letting it accrete to the conscientious.

September into October reopens public rooms and rubrics—orientations, auditions, interviews, first meetings, premieres. Libra weather asks for interface intelligence: arranging chairs, writing agendas, setting timeboxes, distributing turns, dressing the part without letting form smother substance. Youth talk about Libra as diplomatic or indecisive because the season genuinely penalizes premature conflict and rewards fair-seeming process. The mature move is not endless weighing but explicit scaffolds: criteria now, deadline soon, decision then, debrief after. In dating and friendship, the style prefers paced entrances and public poise; the boundary script is courteous and legible.

As days shorten and audits, midterms, and remembrance rituals arrive, the atmosphere contracts. Late October into November—the time popularly tagged Scorpio—trains threshold skills: keeping confidences, distinguishing on/off, and deciding when intensity is appropriate. Small rooms replace big ones; proof beats promise; loyalty grows in the dark. Youth use Scorpio language to mark appetite for depth and a desire for earned access. The shadow is secrecy-as-default or trials that others did not agree to. The seasonal fix is contractual: consent about what circulates, when, and to whom; vulnerability windows with cooldowns; a shared stop rule for digging.

Then the calendar explodes. From late November through December—what youth culture calls Sagittarius season—attention splinters across ceremonies, travel, gift economies, end-of-term crushes, and “year in review” narrative markets. The competence that thrives here is amplification: saying it plain and funny in big rooms, adding seats to the table, making bridges between crews. The memes about bluntness and overpromising track real pressures; windows are short and weather is unreliable. Healthy practice pairs reach with rails: promise budgets, explicit handoffs, travel buffers, and a ritual of the next day’s repair (water, food, quiet).

A switch flips in late December and early January. Holidays swallow and then spit out exhausted publics into budgets, inventories, and cold light. Capricorn weather puts structure and delivery back on the throne. Among students, staff, and early-career workers, this reads as calendars, scopes, and the dignifying of maintenance. The stereotype of austerity is a seasonally accurate risk: a culture that never schedules pleasure will slowly export its joy to people with fewer responsibilities. Youth communities that do Capricorn well celebrate what stayed reliable, not only what appeared new, and move birthdays away from the holiday blender so recognition has a fighting chance.

By late January into February, the noise dies down and the work of clubs, rehearsals, committees, zines, mutual-aid pods, and pilots takes center stage. Aquarius season is mid-winter laboratory time: write the charter, open the doc, specify consent norms, run the small test, invite dissent, revise the rule. The “detached” stereotype reflects a style that delivers care through structure; the antidote is deliberate warmth: soup nights, blanketed co-working, rule-free hours for being together without a project. In youth contexts, Aquarius energy is how scenes stay fair and porous when charisma would otherwise dominate.

Finally, the year dissolves toward thaw. Late February into March—the period linked to Pisces—brings handoffs, admissions decisions, first meltwater, last storms, breaks and returns. The competence is passage: honoring endings, cushioning re-entries, and using image and story to keep groups together until facts land. Youth use Pisces labels for tenderness, ambient care, and boundary-blurring risks. The practical hygiene is to containerize empathy—office hours for heavy talk, two-sentence feelings plus one ask—and to schedule one clear-plan day a week so softness does not become drift.

Read this way, the zodiac looks less like a physics of persons than like a civic calendar of competences. Each segment of the year trains a different style that, once named, travels well as a conversational tag. Aries announces; Taurus steadies; Gemini links; Cancer shelters; Leo convenes; Virgo tunes; Libra frames; Scorpio seals; Sagittarius enlarges; Capricorn builds; Aquarius designs; Pisces releases. Youth culture keeps these tags because they are fast, funny, and mutual: you can tease, request, or confess in 12 words where a diagnostic label would feel heavy. The labels also dovetail with the mediums where young people live. Short videos and screenshots prefer portable forms; memes, as Limor Shifman observes, are “groups of digital items…circulated, imitated, and transformed,” and zodiac talk is almost purpose-built for such circulation. Feeds do the rest; when a For You page surfaces something uncannily apt, it can feel—as one TikTok study puts it—like “the technology itself [is] a powerful partner” in meaning-making. In that sense the sky is not causing behavior so much as providing a durable index for it: an old, legible set of names that map neatly onto the rhythms of school, work, climate, and celebration, now repurposed for algorithmic publics.

Astrology’s contemporary resurgence among youth looks less like a return to pre-modern cosmology than the adoption of a shared social grammar—cheap, portable, and endlessly remixable. In practice, zodiac talk works like a meme ecology: people slot “I’m a Scorpio rising” or “that’s such Aries energy” into everyday interaction to signal temperament, manage expectations, and negotiate closeness. Limor Shifman’s communication-oriented account of memes maps neatly onto this use: an internet meme is “a group of digital items…created with awareness of each other…circulated, imitated, and transformed via the internet by multiple users.” The point is not cosmological truth; it’s social circulation, stance-taking, and play. Youth deploy sign labels as stance—micro-scripts for flirting, boundary-setting, or affiliating—precisely because they are modular and remix-ready. ([Henry Jenkins](https://henryjenkins.org/blog/2014/02/a-meme-is-a-terrible-thing-to-waste-an-interview-with-limor-shifman-part-one.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jcc4.12013?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Two structural features of the current media environment amplify this “zodiac as social grammar.” First, algorithmic feeds double as oracles. On TikTok’s WitchTok, creators explicitly frame the For You Page as a divinatory partner—“a practice that can be understood as algorithmic divination”—where the system’s uncanny personalization carries fate-like authority: “you saw this for a reason.” That experiential opacity preserves an “occult” feel while enabling mass address. As St. Lawrence shows, many practitioners “consider the technology itself a powerful partner” in readings, fusing machinic patterning with human ritual. ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/15/4/435?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Second, platform logics favor spreadable content over settled knowledge. False or simply more surprising claims travel faster than careful ones; as Aral and colleagues found, false news on Twitter was 70% more likely to be retweeted than truth, driven by novelty and status-seeking. The result is a mediasphere where socially useful, identity-forward frames (e.g., “mercury retrograde explains our group chat meltdown”) can outcompete accurate but interaction-poor explanations. ([WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/twitter-false-news-elections-scientific-study?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This social turn helps explain why astrology usage spikes among demographics for whom quick, low-stakes coordination tools are especially handy. Recent Pew data show roughly **3 in 10** U.S. adults engage at least annually with astrology, tarot, or fortunetellers, with much higher rates among younger women and LGBTQ+ respondents; in the latter group, a majority consult horoscopes yearly. People use these tools “just for fun” or for light insight far more often than for high-stakes decisions—a profile consistent with a social-grammar function rather than a metaphysical commitment. ([AP News](https://apnews.com/article/41a815371e107ddef503910eaa9d8378?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Them](https://www.them.us/story/lgbtq-americans-twice-as-likely-to-be-into-astrology-study-shows?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Historically, none of this is entirely new. Early modern Europeans treated astrology as a practical art embedded in everyday problem-solving—timing a journey, diagnosing an illness, picking an auspicious date—more than a rigid doctrine of celestial causation. Casebooks from Simon Forman and Richard Napier preserve tens of thousands of consultations where horary charts were read alongside urine and circumstance; as Lauren Kassell sums up, “what we see in his records is a full, social dynamic of healing.” In other words, astrology functioned as a language for organizing care and counsel in publics short on other diagnostic resources. ([casebooks.lib.cam.ac.uk](https://casebooks.lib.cam.ac.uk/astrological-medicine/early-modern-astrology?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/magic-and-medicine?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Babylonian materials show an even earlier braid of astronomy, omen literature, and natal techniques; as Rochberg’s synthesis makes clear, what we call “astrology” emerged from technical sky-watching entangled with social interpretation, not from a single doctrine about planetary beams. ([bmcr.brynmawr.edu](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005.06.29?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [JSTOR](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/200842?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

If present-day youth aren’t returning to antique star-causation, what are they doing? They’re re-enchanting social life under conditions of data saturation and institutional distrust, while keeping one foot in the pragmatic. Jason Ā. Josephson-Storm’s thesis is useful here: modernity’s story of total disenchantment is itself a myth; belief and enchantment persist, mutate, and migrate (“magic never truly vanished”). The zodiac offers a way to *safely* perform meaning—lightweight, semi-ironic, opt-in—without signing up for doctrinal commitments. ([ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/religion/jason-josephson-storm-and-the-myth-of-disenchantment/11168130?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [University of Chicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/M/bo26032843?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This does not mean astrological claims about personality or fate hold up in formal testing. Large-N studies and classic experiments have struggled to find effects beyond chance, from Carlson’s double-blind Nature study (“The experiment clearly refutes the astrological hypothesis”) to population-level register data in Sweden that show no durable pattern linking astrological “compatibility” to marriage or divorce risk. The divergence between laboratory/noisy population nulls and everyday uptake simply confirms that astrology’s value is, for many users, social rather than predictive. ([Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/438984110/a-double-blind-test-of-astrology-carlsons-original-article-in-nature-pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [SpringerOpen](https://genus.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41118-020-00103-5?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The long, uneasy marriage between astronomy and astrology also illuminates the present moment’s science/fake-news tangle. Johannes Kepler, no enemy to horoscopes as income or pedagogy, still called astrology the “foolish daughter” who “supports her wise but needy mother, Astronomy”—a tart acknowledgment that popular divination subsidized technical sky science even as it risked misleading the public. Today’s attention economy recapitulates that trade: engagement-heavy formats cross-subsidize knowledge institutions, while simultaneously flooding the zone with rumor, vibes, and para-explanations. ([faculty.umb.edu](https://faculty.umb.edu/gary_zabel/Courses/Phil%20281b/Philosophy%20of%20Magic/Arcana/Renaissance/15kep-en.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

How, then, should science position itself in a culture that finds zodiac grammar socially useful? Naomi Oreskes argues that we should trust science not because of a single ritualized “method,” but because of its *social* vetting—peer review, open critique, and, crucially, consensus. “We should trust science because it has a rigorous process for vetting claims,” she notes, and because the authority is collective, corrigible, and expert rather than charismatic. That logic distinguishes science’s epistemic norms from the spread-first dynamics of platforms—and helps explain why scientific claims can falter in social media markets optimized for novelty. ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2019/nov/03/naomi-oreskes-interview-why-trust-science-climate-donald-trump-vaccine?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [TIME](https://time.com/5709691/why-trust-science/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Bruno Latour’s critique of the “modern constitution” sharpens the point. Our world is full of hybrids—social/technical/natural knots like algorithmic feeds performing oracular work—but modern talk often insists on a clean Nature/Society split. As Latour puts it, “as soon as we direct our attention simultaneously to the work of purification and the work of hybridization, we immediately stop being wholly modern.” Zodiac talk on platforms is precisely such a hybrid: a cultural script animated by machine learning; a public intimacy device wrapped in cosmological language; a playful fiction that organizes real behavior. Taking hybrids seriously keeps us from misreading astrology’s rise as mere credulity or, conversely, as harmless fun detached from consequences. ([diagrammatica.jrosborn.georgetown.domains](https://diagrammatica.jrosborn.georgetown.domains/exhibits/show/diagram-gallery/item/32?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The alchemy/chemistry story is an older version of this hybridity. Principe and Newman show early “chymistry” as a continuum of experiment, craft, and metaphysics, not a tidy pre-science. Debates over whether art could *perfect* nature (transmutation, homunculi) prefigured contemporary disputes over bioengineering and AI, while laboratory routines seeded later chemical method. “Alchemy was not an unformed and capricious precursor to chemistry,” Newman argues; it staged enduring arguments about the artificial and the natural. Seeing that lineage helps us avoid a simple science/superstition binary and instead ask how publics negotiate meaning and authority when tools outpace explanations. ([University of Chicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/P/bo3641550.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [JSTOR](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/190791/pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Put together, the evidence supports a clear reading: youth astrology practices are better explained by *social* affordances than by belief in planetary causation. They furnish scripts for self-presentation, consent and conflict navigation, and low-risk intimacy; they hook neatly into algorithmic infrastructures that feel oracular; and they let users rehearse agency in a volatile world. Meanwhile, the scientific record—null effects in prediction, robust process for adjudicating claims—remains intact, and trust in that record must be argued for at the level Oreskes recommends: institutions, evidence, and consensus rather than charisma. The task, then, is not to sneer at zodiac grammars but to contextualize them—historically (from Babylonian horoscopy to early modern casebooks), sociologically (as meme-like stance performances), and epistemologically (as hybrids in a platformed public sphere where novelty often beats accuracy). In that light, the rise of astrology is less a threat to science than a mirror held up to our media ecology: a reminder that humans keep inventing practical languages for coordinating life together—and that the work of science communication is to meet those languages without surrendering its own. ([casebooks.lib.cam.ac.uk](https://casebooks.lib.cam.ac.uk/reading-the-casebooks/what-are-the-casebooks?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [bmcr.brynmawr.edu](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005.06.29?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [1library.net](https://1library.net/article/defining-internet-memes-shifman-limor-memes-digital-culture.z32o6o8q?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/twitter-false-news-elections-scientific-study?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Vox](https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/385140/science-trust-rfk-jr-trump-pew-partisan?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Findings

The evidence converges on a simple but powerful reframing: in youth culture, astrology functions chiefly as a meme-ready social grammar rather than a theory of celestial causation. Survey data show wide participation with low reliance for high-stakes choices; platform studies show sign-talk thriving because it fits the grain of short, remixable formats; historical work reminds us that sharp boundaries between astronomy/astrology (and chemistry/alchemy) are recent achievements, not timeless divides; and population-level tests of astrological prediction return null results. Taken together, these strands support the following integrated findings.

First, the primary value of astrology for younger cohorts is interactional. Sign labels supply quick scripts for self-presentation, boundary setting, and low-risk intimacy. In crowded, time-pressed settings—a hallway outside a rehearsal, a group chat planning night—“I’m such a Virgo about this doc” or “that was peak Leo” communicates stance in seconds. The practice is less belief than pragmatic speech: a vocabulary for doing social coordination at speed.

Second, platform affordances amplify this interactional value. Short videos and screenshots prefer portable forms, and sign stereotypes arrive pre-compressed into 12 legible slots. Youth report experiences that align with “algorithmic divination”: the feed feels uncannily apt, as if “the technology itself [were] a powerful partner.” That sensation of timely relevance gives zodiac snippets a quasi-oracular charge without requiring doctrinal assent, helping them outcompete slower, context-heavy frameworks in everyday coordination.

Third, engagement is best described as playful and selective rather than credulous. Many users bracket causal claims while leaning into social utility: horoscopes and compatibility memes are treated as conversation starters, mood labels, and soft ways to ask for care. This pattern coheres with survey findings that most participants engage “just for fun,” and it explains why astrological talk often coexists comfortably with respect for scientific consensus in other domains (vaccination, climate, coursework).

Fourth, where astrology’s claims are testable, they underperform. Classic experiments and administrative-data studies find no robust evidence that natal configurations or sun-sign pairings predict life outcomes such as personality profiles, marriage formation, or divorce risk. These nulls do not undermine the observed social utility; they simply mark the boundary between a useful vernacular and a predictive model. In practice, youth already behave as if this boundary exists: they use zodiac talk to lighten interaction, not to replace evidence when stakes are high.

Fifth, a season-coded reading clarifies why the twelve labels travel so well. Each segment of the year rehearses distinctive social competences—initiation in early spring, stewardship in late spring, linkage in early summer, refuge in high heat, spectacle at peak summer, mise-en-place before the rentrée, diplomacy at the rentrée, thresholding in late fall, amplification at year’s end, structure at the cold restart, systems in late winter labs, and passage in the thaw. Naming these competences with old, legible sign-words makes them easy to circulate, joke about, and request from one another. The zodiac endures because it doubles as a civic calendar of moods.

Sixth, historical genealogy cautions against moralized binaries. In earlier regimes of knowledge, astronomy interlaced with divination and calendrics; alchemy braided experiment with metaphysics. Modern demarcations arose through institutions and media, not by a single decisive experiment that erased social functions overnight. The present revival, then, is not a relapse into pre-science but a recontextualization: astrology is being redeployed as peer-to-peer etiquette in platform publics, much as earlier societies mobilized it for medicine or statecraft.

Seventh, science’s authority and astrology’s stickiness answer to different logics. Scientific credibility is collective and procedural—peer review, replication, and ultimately consensus—whereas the zodiac’s circulation is participatory and aesthetic—brevity, affect, recognizability. In attention markets where “falsehood diffused farther, faster, deeper,” interaction-friendly frames enjoy native advantages. The policy implication is not more scolding but better design: formats that translate method and uncertainty into artifacts people can actually share and use together.

Eighth, the science/fake-news bind is partly a mediation problem. Treating all vernaculars as rivals to science misdiagnoses the need they meet: people want soft categories for navigating closeness, consent, and conflict. When institutions offer only hard claims (“studies show…”) without hospitable social scripts, zodiac talk fills the gap. The constructive response is to keep empirical claims hard while supplying humane, low-friction ways to talk about needs and limits—decision ladders, boundary sentences, consent check-ins—that can circulate as easily as memes.

Ninth, risks exist but are tractable. Sign labels can harden into stereotypes, excuse unkindness, or substitute for accountability. They can also crowd out other identities or flatten cultural difference. Those risks are best mitigated not by prohibition but by literacies: teaching the difference between stance and essence, reminding users that playful tags do not outrank consent or evidence, and modeling ways to retire a label when it harms.

Tenth, the hybrid status of today’s zodiac practices offers an opportunity rather than a threat. Because the grammar is already seasonal and civic, educators and communicators can time interventions to social rhythms (threshold guides in late fall, decision aids at year end, rest protocols in January). Because the grammar is already participatory, scientists and scholars can render process visible—open notes, replication badges, “what would change my mind?” prompts—so that credibility has social handles.

In sum, the findings point to a division of labor. Astrology, as used by youth, is a compact language for managing everyday social life in an algorithmic public sphere; astronomy and the rest of science remain the place for claims about how the world works. The two are not competitors so much as differently scoped tools. The practical task for institutions is to let scientific claims keep their rigor while learning from the zodiac’s strengths—shared scripts, seasonal timing, convivial form—so that true things can travel as fast, feel as usable, and live as socially as the memes they compete with.

Discussion
 The evidence suggests the contemporary “rise” of astrology among youth is less a reversion to celestial causation than a pragmatic retooling of an old semiotic system for new social ends. Survey data show that “30% of U.S. adults say they consult astrology (or a horoscope), tarot cards or a fortune teller at least once a year,” with most doing so “just for fun,” not for high-stakes decisions (and younger women and LGBTQ+ respondents leading the trend). ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) In other words, the practice functions chiefly as a way to coordinate talk, identity, and intimacy under conditions of abundant media and limited shared scripts. This is consistent with memetic dynamics in youth culture: as Shifman notes, an internet meme is “a group of digital items… circulated, imitated, and/or transformed via the Internet by many users,” a definition that maps neatly onto star-sign banter, compatibility charts, and inside-joke “call-outs.” ([Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jcc4.12013?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Part of astrology’s current traction stems from platform logics. In TikTok’s occult subcultures, the algorithm is not just a channel but a collaborator: users describe technology itself as “a powerful partner” in ritual and interpretation, a form of “algorithmic divination.” ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/15/4/435?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) This helps explain why zodiac talk often reads less like a doctrine than a medium—flexible, remixable, and optimized for circulation. The same features that power meme diffusion also amplify mis/disinformation; as Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral showed, “falsehood diffused significantly farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth.” ([Science](https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.aap9559?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) In that attention economy, short, archetypal labels (“chaotic Aries,” “loyal Taurus”) do double duty: they compress social information and reward shareable affect.

Seen historically, this pragmatic, socially oriented astrology has strong precedent. Early modern “astrologer-physicians” offered care, counseling, and practical judgments; as Kassell puts it, “Astrology was a serious healthcare option during much of the early modern period,” woven into everyday healing alongside urine inspection, bloodletting, and household remedies. ([University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/casebooks?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Kepler himself walked the line between astronomy and the paying public, quipping that “this Astrology is a foolish daughter… [that] supports her wise but needy mother, Astronomy,” an ambivalent acknowledgement that popular forms sustained scientific work even as they simplified it. ([en.wikisource.org](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kepler/Chapter_6?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Reconstructions of earlier knowledge cultures thus complicate a simple science/superstition split. Rochberg’s account of Mesopotamian “celestial sciences” shows that what later Europeans carved up into astronomy/astrology was originally an integrated textual and observational enterprise, not cleanly separable into “physics” and “belief.” ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26872170_The_Heavenly_Writing_Divination_Horoscopy_and_Astronomy_in_Mesopotamian_Culture_by_Francesca_Rochberg?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Principe’s history of alchemy makes a similar point for chemistry: early practitioners blended hands-on experiment, symbolism, and medicine, and much that looks like “nonsense” reads as disciplined craft once decoded. ([University of Chicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo12335123.html/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Cambridge University Press & Assessment](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/medical-history/article/lawrence-m-principe-the-secrets-of-alchemy-chicago-il-university-of-chicago-press-2013-pp-v-281-1750-hardback-isbn-9780226682952/6D94BF2E44364085AABD532CB113F811?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [lareviewofbooks.org](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/one-book-opens-another-on-the-secrets-of-alchemy?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This matters for how we talk about “science” in popular culture. Oreskes argues that public trust should hinge less on heroic method and more on institutions and social processes—“We should trust science because it has a rigorous process for vetting claims… [and] consensus is a crucial indicator.” ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2019/nov/03/naomi-oreskes-interview-why-trust-science-climate-donald-trump-vaccine?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) By that standard, modern, platform-native astrology is not a rival evidentiary system so much as a vernacular social grammar: a way of negotiating self-presentation, compatibility, and conflict when formal institutions aren’t designed for those intimate needs. Notably, when social scientists test astrology’s predictive content under strong designs, they find nulls. A large, register-based Swedish study reports results that “fail to provide any consistent evidence” that sun-sign compatibility predicts marriage patterns or divorce risk. ([SpringerOpen](https://genus.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41118-020-00103-5?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Meanwhile, the platform conditions that boost astrology’s spread also boost rumor and falsehood, creating what youth experience as an affectively rich but epistemically noisy environment. The upshot is not that “science loses”; it’s that different systems address different problems—causal knowledge versus social coordination—and sometimes get misread as in zero-sum competition.

The science/fake-news “conundrum” looks different from this angle. Youth aren’t necessarily rejecting science; they are inhabiting what Latour called a world of hybrids, where the modern division between “Nature” and “Society” never fully obtained—“Modernity has never begun. There has never been a modern world.” ([Medium](https://hup.medium.com/we-have-never-been-modern-b8c1a9d940a3?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) In that hybrid space, alchemy/chemistry and astrology/astronomy are not simple before/after pairs but braided histories. The current renaissance of star-talk as social shorthand is better read as re-enchantment with tools for meaning-making than as credulous cosmology. Josephson-Storm makes the broader claim: “we have never been disenchanted,” since magical and scientific sensibilities have coexisted throughout modernity. ([ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/religion/jason-josephson-storm-and-the-myth-of-disenchantment/11168130?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) The “alchemy/chemistry” and “astrology/astronomy” stories therefore caution us against tidy declension narratives in which critical reason expels enchantment once and for all. They also caution against romantic reversals that would replace science with esoterica. What changes across epochs is not the presence of enchantment but its uses and institutions.

Taken together, the literature suggests a productive reframing: treat popular astrology as a social technology—akin to memes, personality tests, and micro-genres—that helps coordinate interaction under algorithmic mediation. This reframing avoids both scolding (which mistakes the function) and credulity (which inflates the epistemic claim). It also clarifies why the same feeds that deliver horoscope skits can supercharge misinformation: both are compact, affectively charged heuristics designed to travel. The policy and pedagogical implication is not to suppress vernacular grammars but to scaffold them—folding platform literacy, uncertainty reasoning, and evidence norms into the very spaces where youth already perform identity and care.

Conclusion
 If youth astrology today works primarily as social grammar, three conclusions follow.

First, its popularity is empirically compatible with stable (not surging) “belief” and low decision reliance. Pew’s 2024/2025 surveys indicate steady belief levels (about 27%) and that most participation is “mostly ‘just for fun’,” with only ~1% reporting they rely “a lot” on insights for major life choices. ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) That pattern fits a coordination tool more than a cosmological commitment.

Second, historical scholarship weakens the myth that hard demarcations were ever lived realities. From Mesopotamian celestial sciences to astrologer-physicians’ casebooks—where astral judgment, talk therapy, and household medicine intertwined—astrology’s social roles have long exceeded deterministic cosmology. ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26872170_The_Heavenly_Writing_Divination_Horoscopy_and_Astronomy_in_Mesopotamian_Culture_by_Francesca_Rochberg?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/casebooks?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [JSTOR Muse](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/564670?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25557513/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Likewise, alchemy’s laboratory practice fed into chemistry rather than simply opposing it. ([University of Chicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo12335123.html/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Cambridge University Press & Assessment](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/medical-history/article/lawrence-m-principe-the-secrets-of-alchemy-chicago-il-university-of-chicago-press-2013-pp-v-281-1750-hardback-isbn-9780226682952/6D94BF2E44364085AABD532CB113F811?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [lareviewofbooks.org](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/one-book-opens-another-on-the-secrets-of-alchemy?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Latour’s point about hybrids helps us see why such braids persist: modern media ecologies do not eliminate enchantment; they rearticulate it. ([JSTOR Muse](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/1/article/8061?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [Medium](https://hup.medium.com/we-have-never-been-modern-b8c1a9d940a3?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Third, the epistemic status of astrological claims remains distinct from their social utility. Strong-design tests find no support for zodiac-based predictions in relationship outcomes, underscoring the need to keep evidentiary and expressive functions conceptually separate. ([SpringerOpen](https://genus.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41118-020-00103-5?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [SpringerLink](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41118-020-00103-5?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) That separation, however, should not collapse into disdain for meaning-making practices that address needs orthogonal to causal prediction—belonging, flirting, boundary-setting, repair.

From these premises, a constructive cultural program comes into view. Science communication should foreground process and institutions—peer review, replication, qualified consensus—because, as Oreskes argues, “consensus is a crucial indicator” of claims that have survived organized scrutiny. ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2019/nov/03/naomi-oreskes-interview-why-trust-science-climate-donald-trump-vaccine?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Platform literacy should be taught with the memetic mechanics that actually shape youth discourse: how and why “novel” and emotionally arousing content outruns sober correction (“falsehood diffused… farther, faster, deeper”). ([Science](https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.aap9559?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) And educators might borrow astrology’s social affordances without its causal pretensions—using archetype talk to open discussions of personality science, or “compatibility” games to teach base rates, effect sizes, and the difference between narrative fit and predictive power.

Astrology’s current renaissance thus neither vindicates celestial determinism nor indicts a generation as anti-scientific. It signals a savvy adaptation: young people are rebuilding communal sense-making with the materials at hand—short-form video, memes, platform algorithms, and accessible archetypes. The task for scholars and communicators is not to choose between astronomy and astrology, chemistry and alchemy, science and “fake news,” but to map how these languages circulate, which problems they solve, and how to align them with the institutions that test claims about the world. If we can do that, we honor both sides of Kepler’s uncomfortable pairing—the “wise… Astronomy” and her unruly “daughter”—without confusing their jobs.
