# Big Data’s Disparate Impact — Barocas & Selbst 2016 (Title VII, data mining)

## Summary
Solon Barocas and Andrew D. Selbst’s 2016 essay explains how **data mining and predictive modeling** can produce discriminatory outcomes as an **emergent property** of the pipeline (labels, training data, features, proxies, masking) rather than explicit intent. It then analyzes how U.S. antidiscrimination law (Title VII) struggles to govern these harms, especially when “business necessity” defenses align with what prediction systems are designed to optimize.

## Key Ideas (vault-usable)
- Data mining can discriminate through:
  - target variable / class label choices
  - biased or historically tainted training data
  - feature selection that embeds social inequality
  - proxies (non-sensitive features that stand in for protected classes)
  - masking (making causality hard to prove)
- Disparate impact doctrine is the most plausible legal route absent intent, but predictive correlation can still be treated as “job-related” or “business necessity.”
- The reform problem exposes the tension between **anticlassification** vs **antisubordination** theories of discrimination.

## Why it matters here
- A canonical bridge between “fairness” talk and **pipeline-level governance** language (where the harm actually enters).
- Pairs well with contextual integrity when writing about surveillance/collection + downstream decision harms: [[Privacy as Contextual Integrity — Nissenbaum 2004 (Public Surveillance)]].
