# Automating Inequality — Eubanks (Digital Poorhouse, profiling and punishment)

## Summary
Virginia Eubanks’s *Automating Inequality: How High-Tech Tools Profile, Police, and Punish the Poor* is framed as a social justice critique of automated decision systems in public benefits and state interventions. The central claim (as summarized by a 2019 book review) is that “high-tech” systems frequently reproduce and harden class/race bias—creating a “digital poorhouse” in low-rights environments and then extending those governance techniques more broadly.

## Provenance note
This note is grounded in a **book review** capture (not the full book text). Use it as a routing/anchor node; verify claims against the book before quoting.

## Key Ideas (from the review)
- Automated decision systems shift power from human casework to “sophisticated machines” while accountability and political debate lag.
- Integrated databases + predictive models can tag people as “risky/problematic,” enabling surveillance and intervention.
- A “nonharm” design posture is proposed (including a draft Hippocratic Oath for data/engineering/admin practice), but the deeper claim is that ethical design must be paired with dismantling structural racism and poverty conditions.

## Why it matters here
- Complements pipeline-level discrimination analysis in [[Big Data’s Disparate Impact — Barocas & Selbst 2016 (Title VII, data mining)]] by focusing on the **welfare/state** context and the lived consequences of classification.
- A strong “governance of legibility” case: who becomes governable, how risk labels travel, and how care gets displaced by efficiency.
