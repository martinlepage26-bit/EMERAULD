# The Psychology of Conspiracy Theories — Douglas, Sutton, Cichocka 2017

## Summary
Douglas, Sutton, and Cichocka (2017) review the social psychology of conspiracy beliefs: why people adopt conspiracy explanations (secret plots by powerful malevolent groups) and what the consequences are. They organize motives into three families — **epistemic** (understanding), **existential** (safety/control), and **social** (self/group image) — and note that consequences are under-studied and often do *not* show “motive satisfaction.” Provides the psychological substrate underneath [[Transparency Against Democracy — Paquin 2025 (Sweden Democrats, trust)|Paquin’s]] manufactured-distrust mechanism and underneath the [[Narrative Capture Failure Taxonomy — Substituting Theory for Contact|narrative-capture]] failure mode.

## Key Ideas (vault-usable)
- Motive families:
  - **Epistemic:** desire for meaning/patterns, cognitive closure, relief from uncertainty — the same pull that produces [[AI Iterative Loop — Frame Capture and Recursive Validation Failure|recursive validation failure]] when AI fluency offers premature closure.
  - **Existential:** compensatory control when powerless/anxious; threat management — the affective register of [[Mental illness, addiction, and AI psychosis|AI psychosis]] and operator burnout.
  - **Social:** maintaining positive self/group image; identity-protective reasoning — the same pattern named in [[Emotional Alliance vs. Evidentiary Discipline in AI]] as “emotional alliance.”
- Conspiracy theories can be **more appealing than satisfying**: experimental exposures can increase uncertainty rather than resolve it — a structural cousin to fluency-without-evidence in [[Fluency, Interruption, and Institutional Accountability]].
- Links to analytic/rational constraints: conspiracy belief correlates with lower analytic thinking and education, and with agency-detection/intentionality where it doesn’t exist; cf. [[The Compulsion to Complete — AI as Gap-Closer]] for the gap-closure analog in AI outputs.

## Why it matters here
- Supports governance writing about narrative control and accountability by grounding “conspiracy style” as a psychological pattern rather than treating it as only ideology — anchors the [[Governance by Denial|governance-by-denial]] argument that legitimacy degradation has a measurable psychological profile.
- Useful as a caution for governance comms: uncertainty + powerlessness conditions can predict conspiratorial uptake; pairs with [[Big Data’s Disparate Impact — Barocas & Selbst 2016 (Title VII, data mining)|Barocas & Selbst]] for the structural conditions that produce the affected publics.
