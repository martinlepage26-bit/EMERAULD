# Intelligence Definitions and AI Bias — 1956 Lecture

## Summary
A modern lecture-style text (source file: `1956 - Artificail Consciouness.txt` — the "1956" in the filename refers to the year "Artificial Intelligence" was coined, not the date of composition) tracing competing definitions of intelligence in AI and cognitive science, arguing that the term "Artificial Intelligence" (coined in 1956 at the Dartmouth Conference) encodes a human-centric bias that distorts research goals. Connects to [[Martin Lepage — Professional Profile|Martin's]] broader interest in the epistemology of AI claims and [[Governance by Denial]].

## Context

## Details

### The Core Argument
There is no agreed definition of intelligence. Definitions range from "ability to adapt to the environment" to "what is measured by IQ tests." Most AI definitions combine two axes: act/think × rational/human. All of them assume intelligence is scalar and rankable — a hierarchy with humans at the top.

That hierarchy is a cultural artifact, not a natural fact. Aristotle's *Scala Naturae* (the Great Chain of Being) put humans above all living things. Evolutionary science shows that all living species share a common origin and have all evolved for the same duration — no living species is the ancestor of another. The "smarter than a mouse" intuition is human preference, not objective measure.

### Non-human-centric Reframing
Different species are better adapted in different domains. Sensory systems humans lack entirely may enable forms of intelligence we cannot recognize or evaluate. Cognitive scientist Donald Hoffman: "A Sapiocentric view of intelligence is as plausible as a Geocentric view of the cosmos."

If intelligence is not one-dimensional, "superintelligence" (AI surpassing humans in all domains) is as incoherent as claiming a species is superior to all others in all environments. Paper clip maximizer and tumor-injecting AI scenarios may reflect *human cognitive pathology* projected onto AI, not genuine AI threat models.

### Embodied Cognition
Researcher Melanie Mitchell: the cognitive limitations of embodied, social, emotional, irrational humans may be precisely what enables general intelligence. "Distractibility, wild associations, emotionality" are not bugs to be eliminated — they are features that produce generality.

### Terminological Note
The term "Artificial Intelligence" nearly lost to "Complex Information Processing" in 1956. Peter Domingos: "People worry that computers will get too smart and take over the world, but the real problem is that they're too stupid and they've already taken over the world."

## Key Ideas
- Intelligence has no agreed definition and never has
- The Great Chain of Being bias underlies most AI threat narratives
- Embodied irrationality may be constitutive of general intelligence, not opposed to it
- "Superintelligence" is probably incoherent as a concept
- Preferred term in the field: subfield-specific rather than "AI" (machine learning, NLP, artificial consciousness)

## Insights
- The 1956 origin moment encodes Sapiocentric assumptions that still govern both popular and technical discourse
- AI anxiety (see [[AI Society Manuscript — From AI Anxiety to Recursive Governance]]) is partly a mirror of the field's foundational bias: we fear the thing we imagined in our own image
- The lecture anticipates the [[Self-Polygraph Protocol and Suprametacognition]] approach of asking AI to state the limits of its own claims
